Clarinet

A cylindrical tube with a single reed — it overblows a twelfth, which splits it into three distinct registers

Related

The clarinet is the most "divided" instrument in the woodwind group: on one tube, three registers that sound like three different instruments. That is not a matter of style but of bore — the clarinet is one of the few cylindrical woodwinds.

⚠️ One conceptual point: what really separates the clarinet from the oboe is not "one reed or two" but the bore. The clarinet's is cylindrical, so it sustains only odd harmonics (1, 3, 5…) and overblows a twelfth; the oboe family's is conical, so it sustains all harmonics and overblows an octave. Hence a clarinet needs a twelfth key where an oboe needs only an octave key. The key's name tells you the bore — the most useful single test in woodwind acoustics.

Structure: mouthpiece, cylinder and twelfth key

Three points:

1. A separate mouthpiece, unlike the oboe family: the single reed is bound to a rigid mouthpiece that fits into the top of the tube. Changing a reed takes seconds and reeds are factory products — a much lower barrier. 2. A cylindrical bore. Apart from the bell the body is a near-uniform tube. This decides all its acoustics. 3. A twelfth key, raising the note by a twelfth rather than an octave — the name states the fact.

Three registers, three characters

Why three registers? Because a cylindrical tube only admits odd harmonics. The low register uses the fundamental, the middle the 3rd harmonic, the top the 5th — skipping the 2nd and 4th. So each register change is a jump in colour, not a smooth transition.

Compare: the flute and oboe (conical) change register gradually, so they never sound "in three pieces". That is the cylinder's signature.

Range

  • Sounding D3–B♭6, over three and a half octaves — among the widest in the woodwind group (with the bassoon).
  • The working register is D3–F5, with the clarino the most comfortable.
  • The low register (D3–B♭3) is its second personality: thick and dark, and a favourite for lyrical phrases.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. The clarino is the canonical voice: bright, free and loud — where nearly all melodies sit. 2. The chalumeau is a different instrument: dark, thick, faintly buzzing. 3. The altissimo has to be squeezed: thinner in colour and harder to place, used with care. 4. Its dynamic range is the widest of any woodwind — from a whisper to a fortissimo, which is why wind bands give it the leading line.

Playing techniques

  • The break is the great obstacle: crossing it near B♭4 needs fingers, air and embouchure to change together — every clarinettist's rite of passage.
  • Glissando / smear. The clarinet is one of the few winds that can produce a true glide; the scooping attack of jazz comes from here.
  • Overblowing with the twelfth key and the embouchure to reach the altissimo.
  • Vibrato is rare in classical playing and common in jazz and Eastern European folk.

History

PeriodState
c. 1700 (Nuremberg)improved from the traditional chalumeau with an added overblowing key — the clarinet begins
18th c.keys multiply; Mozart writes it a concerto and chamber music, and brings it into the orchestra and wind ensemble
19th c.two rival key systems appear — French (Böhm) and German (Oehler) — both still in use
Late 19th–20th c.fixed as the modern clarinet; becomes central to orchestra, band and jazz
20th c.jazz makes it a solo protagonist; contemporary music exploits it heavily

Its success has a practical cause: no reed scraping, a big sound, a wide range and a huge dynamic range. Those four make it both the easiest woodwind to start and the most versatile one.

Common misconceptions

  • "Oboe and clarinet differ only in reed count." The decisive difference is the bore: a cylinder has only odd harmonics and overblows a twelfth; a cone has all of them and overblows an octave. Even the register behaviour differs.
  • "The nickname \u201cblack tube\u201d means it is black and low." The nickname is about its looks (ebony or black resin) and says nothing about pitch; the clarinet actually sounds higher than the oboe.
  • "A clarinet is a recorder." The recorder is a duct flute with no reed and no key system — a different family entirely.
  • "A bass clarinet is just a louder clarinet." It is a separate instrument in B♭, written a major ninth higher, with a different size and use (see its own entry).
  • "The three registers come from playing habit." They come from the bore: a cylinder skips the even harmonics, so register changes must jump in colour.

Next

Two directions within the family: higher, the E♭ clarinet, and lower, the bass clarinet — all three sharing one fingering logic and differing mainly in register and size.

For the other end of the "cylinder versus cone" test, go to the bassoon: also conical with a double reed, its tube so long it must be folded in a U. Then the saxophone — single-reed drive with a conical bore, the point where the two dimensions cross.
