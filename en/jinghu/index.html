Jinghu

The smallest-bodied huqin — with a range of barely a tenth, because all it has to do is follow a human voice

1 min read224 characters条目Chinese BowedUpdated 2026-09-26

On a Peking-opera stage, when the voice climbs, one instrument is always a little above it. It never covers the singer, yet you hear it the whole time. That is the jinghu's post.

The smallest of the bodies

Both the neck and the body are bamboo. The body is about 11.4 cm long with a rear opening a little over 4 cm across, snakeskin on the front and left open at the back. It is the smallest body in the huqin family, and the smallness is not economy: each step up in bore adds weight and drops the pitch, so a smaller cavity is higher, brighter and crisper. A pair of wind openings runs through the neck, a second resonating space.

The bow is short, about 74 cm, with the hair drawn tight. It responds fast, which suits the short, hard strokes of opera.

Standing beside the voice

What the audience listens to is the singing. The suona and the drums make themselves big, with a broad, tall spectrum that overrides the room. The jinghu puts itself in the right place instead: a narrow high peak just above the voice. Each sound keeps its own band, and neither covers the other.

That explains the odd-looking design. The smallest body pushes energy into the high frequencies; a tenth of range is enough to follow a throat; a short, tight bow clings to every syllable.

A tenth is enough

The full range is about two octaves, of which traditional accompaniment uses barely a tenth. That is rare: the range is not narrow because it cannot be wider, but because wider is not needed. Where the throat cannot go, the string does not follow — wherever the throat can reach, the string can reach.

Tuning follows the aria: erhuang sol·re, xipi la·mi, fan-erhuang do·sol, fan-xipi re·la. Change the play and you change the tuning.

From soft bow to steel strings

In 1790 the four Anhui troupes arrived in Beijing, Peking opera took shape, and the jinghu grew with it. Early instruments had a short neck, a small snakeskin or python-skin body, silk strings and a soft bow. The hard bow appeared in the 19th century, the modern form settled in the first half of the 20th, and steel strings came in during the 1950s. The name is recent too: until the 1950s the opera world called it simply huqin, then added jing to mark it off from other regional styles.

Its accompanying craft shows best in playing high under a low phrase and low under a high one, the voice and the string moving apart on purpose. It now has solo pieces of its own; Deep Night was adapted from an opera tune.
