The jinghu is the smallest of the family and the narrowest in range — and those two facts are the same fact.

⚠️ Its range is not limited by craft; it is specified by its job. | | Erhu | Banhu | Jinghu | |---|---|---|---| | Body | medium · python skin | larger · paulownia board | ⭐ smallest · snakeskin | | Working range | three octaves | two octaves and a fourth | ⭐ barely a tenth in operatic use | | Its job | solo · ensemble | lead of bangzi opera | follow a Peking-opera singer | Why can an instrument get by on a tenth? Because it has exactly one task: to stay with a human voice. One source says it outright: "Wherever the throat can reach, the string can reach." The voice never goes further, so neither does the instrument.

Structure: the smallest of the bodies

Three points:

1. The smallest body in the whole huqin family. About 11.4 cm long with a rear opening of about 4.3 cm — and each step up in bore adds darkness and drops a note; a smaller bore is higher, brighter and crisper. Its smallness is not economy; it is tuning. 2. Bamboo. Both neck and body; the bamboo's stiffness gives the bright tone, and the neck carries paired "wind openings" through it — an extra resonating chamber beyond the body. 3. A short bow with tight hair. About 74 cm, with the hair drawn tight. That makes the bowing extremely responsive and suits the short, decisive strokes of opera.

How do you make yourself heard?

This figure adds a new dimension to the "volume is social radius" thread.

  • Suona and drums: make the energy big — a broad, tall spectrum that overrides the environment.
  • The singing voice: what the audience is actually listening to.
  • Jinghu: put the energy in the right place — a narrow high peak standing right beside the voice. It does not compete for the voice's position; it stands next to it.

That explains the jinghu's strange-looking design choices: why the body is the smallest (small cavity → high frequencies → a spike), why a tenth of range is enough (following a throat needs no more), and why the bow is short and tight (fast response, to cling to every syllable).

And the accompanying aesthetic follows from it. Two phrases from the sources sum it up: "wherever the throat can reach, the string can reach", and the technique of "playing high under a low phrase, playing low under a high one" — reaching the state described as "you in me, me in you."

Range

  • About two octaves, of which only a tenth is actually in use — the only time on this site that a narrow range is a specification rather than a limitation.
  • The exact opposite of the ruan and zheng, whose logic is "add strings, add range"; the jinghu's logic is "enough is enough". How wide a range should be depends on whom the instrument has to talk to.
  • Tuning follows the aria: erhuang *sol·re*, xipi *la·mi*, fan-erhuang *do·sol*, fan-xipi *re·la* — a different mode, a different tuning (the same logic as the banhu).

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Clear, bright, extremely penetrating. The bamboo gives that "aloft and upright" quality. 2. Thin and narrow, but perfectly distinct. It has no body, yet every note can be picked out of the band. 3. It is the voice's neighbour in frequency. It follows the singing but sits slightly above it, so the two can hear each other without either covering the other. 4. Short, decisive bowing. A short, tight bow makes staccato, legato, détaché and tremolo all crisp — exactly the rhythmic feel of *pihuang* music.

Playing techniques

  • Tuning changes with the aria. Xipi and erhuang each have their own string-names (*sol·re*, *la·mi*).
  • Left hand stops the strings, with vibrato, slides and tapped notes — the operatic "inflection" is made here.
  • Right-hand bowing: long, separate, slurred, staccato, détaché, tremolo.
  • Accompaniment has its own vocabulary: *support, follow, lift, hold*; *simple against elaborate*; "play high under a low phrase, low under a high one" — accompanying here is not "following along" but "conversing with the voice."
  • The bow is short with tight, hard hair. That is the biggest practical difference from the erhu: faster response, harder to make soft.

This table finishes the story of "what one resonator material grows into": python skin (erhu, sanxian) · paulownia board (banhu) · snakeskin with the smallest body (jinghu) — one family, one bowing technique, three materials and three sizes, three destinies.

Its history contains a way of naming that has not appeared before: it is called "jinghu" in order to *distinguish* it from others. Which completes this batch's naming stories:

How an instrument got its name tells you almost its whole social life: whom it learned from (an action) · whose name it once carried (a change of place) · who took its name away (a theft) · what choice it made (a material) · whom it had to be distinguished from (jinghu).

Common misconceptions

  • "A jinghu is loud." Its volume is not large; what is large is its penetration — a small cavity concentrates energy into high frequencies, so it burns out of the band and the voice.
  • "Its narrow range means backward craft." The opposite: the range is specified as a tenth by its job — "wherever the throat can reach, the string can reach," and there is no need to go where the voice cannot.
  • "It is much like a banhu — both are high huqins." Different material and size: the jinghu is bamboo with snakeskin and the smallest body; the banhu is coconut shell with a paulownia board and a larger body — one "clear and bright", the other "high and soaring".
  • "It only accompanies Peking opera." Since the 20th century it has been a solo instrument, with pieces adapted from operatic tunes such as *Deep Night*, *Xiao Kai Men* and *Liu Qing Niang*, and it plays lead with large orchestras as well.
  • "The huqin family is all skinned, so all membranophones." No: the strings vibrate (chordophone); snakeskin, python skin and paulownia board are all only resonators. Three resonator materials, and the class never changed once — the test is always "what vibrates".
  • "The bayin says silk, so only silk strings will do." As with the guqin: the bayin records the material of its own time; since the 1950s the jinghu has generally used steel strings.
