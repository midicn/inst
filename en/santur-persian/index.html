The Persian santur

Four strings per course, eighteen movable bridges — an instrument built for changing mode

Related

SanturCimbalomYangqin (hammered dulcimer)The qanunThe oudThe neyTanbur

⭐⭐ The main line: an instrument built for changing mode

「Modulating between maqams on the Santur is difficult and requires completely retuning the strings; therefore, professional musicians often use several different Santurs during concerts, each pre-tuned to a specific maqam.」

The easiest thing to miss about the santur is this: it is not one instrument, it is a set of them.

⭐⭐ Players bring several santurs to the platform, each tuned in advance to one maqam —— ⭐⭐ because changing mode on the santur is not something the hands do; it is something the instrument has to be re-tuned for: the strings are re-tuned and the bridges re-placed.

⭐⭐ The only instrument on the site whose mode change costs you a new instrument:

InstrumentWhat changes the modeDoes the instrument change?
OudThe finger's position on a fretless boardNo
QanunA brass lever (mandal)No
NeyA different pipe from the setYes (seven of them)
SanturRe-tuning the strings and re-placing the bridgesYes (several)

⭐⭐ Four instruments, one maqam system, four completely different answers —— ⭐⭐ the freedom to modulate depends on where the variable element has been put: on the fingertip, on a lever, on a spare pipe, or simply on bringing a second instrument.

⭐⭐ The bridges are the mechanism

⭐⭐ The Persian santur carries two sets of nine bridges (kharak), eighteen in all, dividing the strings into three positions. ⭐⭐ Four strings tuned in unison cross each bridge —— and that string is cut by the bridges into three segments, each a note of its own.

⭐⭐ The exact reverse of the site's intuition that one string means one note: seventy-two strings yield twenty-five distinct pitches. (Against the harp: forty-seven strings give six and a half octaves, one pitch per string plus pedals; ⭐⭐ two opposite bargains — the santur takes few and precise, the harp many and complete.)

⭐⭐ The bridges slide —— moving one changes the speaking length of the string, and so changes its pitch. ⭐⭐ This is the site's 「changing geometry」 route to tuning (tension · geometry · mass): the oud's geometry lives under a fingertip, the qanun's under a brass strip, ⭐⭐ the santur's under a small wooden bridge.

⭐ Form: a trapezoidal flat box (walnut or maple · about 90 cm wide × 35 cm deep × 8 cm high) · two sets of nine movable sheep-shaped bridges kharak · seventy-two strings in eighteen courses of four · ⭐⭐ brass or copper for the bass half on the right, steel for the treble half on the left (silk before the 20th century) · two sound holes · three positions of nine pitches (E♭3–F4 · E♭4–F5 · E♭5–F6), twenty-seven positions in all, ⭐⭐ with the top F counted twice, twenty-five distinct pitches.

⭐⭐ Copper on the right, steel on the left — two metals, two colours of sound —— and it is plainly audible: the bass has a dull, thick, brassy ring, the treble a clear steel voice.

⭐⭐ The microtones are fixed at tuning time, not at playing time

⭐⭐ Quarter tones on the santur are obtained by moving a bridge: koron below, sori above. ⭐⭐ And the rule is — 「the note G can either be natural, sharp, flat, koron or sori for a given register」: ⭐⭐ one note name, one form, within a register.

⭐⭐ That is the root reason a mode change means retuning: in one register G can only be one of the five, and another maqam may need another form. ⭐⭐ A string on the santur does not hold a pitch found by pressing; it holds a pitch found by placement —— ⭐⭐ the site's third shape of 「intonation is a position, not a scale」: the oud's position is under a fingertip, the qanun's under a brass strip, ⭐⭐ the santur's under a wooden bridge.

⭐⭐ A rule found nowhere else: because a note name can take only one form within a register, the santur cannot play most Western classical repertoire —— ⭐⭐ a limit in an instrument's tuning draws the boundary of the music it can play (against the auto-tune, whose algorithm precedes its effect · the qanun, designed as a theoretical object —— ⭐⭐ the santur is an instrument constrained by its modes).

⭐⭐ The name: it is already written in the Book of Daniel

⭐⭐ 「the Biblical Aramaic form psantērīn is found in the Book of Daniel 3:5」 —— ⭐⭐ an instrument's name entering scripture: the oldest case on the site of a name being written into a text.

⭐⭐ There are two accounts of the etymology: Persian sanṭīr, related to the Greek ψαλτήριον (psalterion); and another reading that splits it into Persian san + tur, meaning 「a hundred strings」 —— ⭐⭐ while it actually carries seventy-two (⭐⭐ the number in the name does not match the number in the instrument — against the site's naming series, where a name sticks to the most typical shape a thing ever had).

⭐⭐ The ancestor's image: the oldest depictions are on the carvings of Babylon (1600–911 BCE) and Neo-Assyria (911–612 BCE), ⭐⭐ with the instrument hung from the player's neck. ⭐⭐ Habib Hasan Touma holds that the Babylonian santur is the common ancestor of the harp, the yangqin, the harpsichord, the qanun, the cimbalom and the hammered dulcimers —— ⭐⭐ 「strings lying flat and struck」 matters far more than how many strings there are (against the site's master axis: what classifies an instrument is how it is made to speak).

⭐⭐ Playing: two feather-light hammers, and a habit of damping

⭐ The hammers are called mezrab: oval, thin, as light as a feather, 「held between the thumb, index, and middle fingers」; ⭐⭐ the string is damped at once by a finger or the palm after each strike —— ⭐⭐ an undamped santur buzzes, so damping is a necessary action, not a flourish.

⭐⭐ Glissandi are made by sweeping a hammer across adjacent strings.

⭐⭐ Melody in one hand, accompaniment in the other: the right hand carries the line, the left the rhythm and harmony. ⭐⭐ The technique of riz — tremolo — comes from the setar: both hands strike the same string rapidly in alternation to stretch out a note; ⭐⭐ and octave doubles are always played as an alternating trill, never together —— ⭐⭐ the most conspicuous texture in Persian santur playing.

⭐⭐ Players: Abolhasan Saba (1902–1957, whose radif version is the most widely played) · Faramarz Payvar · Hossein Malek · Mohammed Heydari · Parviz Meshkatian (1955–2009) · ⭐⭐ Ardavan Kamkar — 「the inventor of the contemporary Santur」 —— the man who freed the instrument out of its tremolo mould and gave it polyphony and two independent hands; ⭐⭐ Pashang Kamkar then became the founder of its pedagogy (The Method of Playing the Santur).

⭐⭐ The twelve dastgahs: the modal system of Persian classical music comprises seven principal dastgahs and five avaz, ⭐⭐ and the whole repertory is called the radif —— ⭐⭐ a body of music handed down through the santur and its sister instruments.

⭐⭐ Three descendants, each changing the one thing that mattered most to it

DescendantWhat it changed
Iraqi santur92 steel or bronze strings · 23 courses of four · ⭐⭐ its bridges are called dama, 「like chessmen」
Indian santoorBorrowed directly from Persia, wider, more rectangular, more strings; ⭐⭐ the mallets are held and played differently (Pandit Shivkumar Sharma's line carried it into North Indian Hindustani music)
Hungarian cimbalomLarger, fully chromatic, with legs and pedals (fixed in Budapest by Schunda in 1874)

⭐⭐ One instrument, three lines of descent: ⭐⭐ the Iraqi line changed 「the number of strings and the look of the bridges」 · the Indian line changed 「the shape and the technique」 · the European line changed 「the whole mechanism」 —— ⭐⭐ three directions of divergence: more strings · different form · added machinery (against the site's 「variants after export»: the oud added frets and became the lute).

⭐⭐ The cimbalom is the end of that line: ⭐⭐ legs, pedals and full chromaticism — solving in one stroke the trouble of 「carrying a second instrument to change mode」 that the santur lives with. ⭐⭐ The clearest case on the site of machinery doing a human job: the santur solves modulation by moving instruments, the cimbalom solves it with pedals.
