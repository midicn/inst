The paiban is the smallest, least instrument-like thing on this site: three boards of hardwood, one cord, eighteen to twenty centimetres.

No skin, no metal, no resonator — less a musical instrument than a tool. Yet the entire rhythmic system of Chinese opera is named after it.

The last entry (the dap) asked how many sounds one skin can hold. This one asks something else:

⚠️ How large can a thing be in music when it makes neither pitch nor, really, timbre? The answer is hidden in the word ban-yan (板眼): ban = the clack of the clappers (the strong beat); yan = the drum-slip tapping the drum (weak and secondary beats). And the journey of *ban* does not stop there — it climbs three steps: | Step | Name | What it is | |---|---|---| | object | paiban | three boards | | measure | ban-yan · ban-shi | the unit of the beat, the form of the beat | | system | ban-qiang-ti | the musical structure of most opera genres in China | ⭐⭐ Rule ㉘: an instrument need not make sound — it can be the measure. The paiban produces no content; it produces the scale. A name walked out of a thing and became a unit, a term, the name of a system — and finally settled into everyday Chinese: you-ban-you-yan ("with board and eye" — orderly), zou-ban ("off the board" — off track).

Structure: three boards, one cord

Three points:

1. Three boards. The modern form is three rectangular hardwood boards — rosewood, mahogany, boxwood… ⭐ The name *tanban* is simply "boards of sandalwood (檀)" — an instrument named for its material. 2. Two plus one. The front two are bound into a pair with a silk cord or cloth tape; the back board is single, joined to them by cord; holes at the top, free to open and close at the bottom — that hinge is the whole mechanism. 3. How it sounds. Left hand holds the back board and strikes the front pair with its lower edge (the "lip") — sound comes from board against board. In Sichuan opera it is called the "carry-hand" (提手): a red silk band linking three boards, the hand pinching the lower one and swinging the upper two. ⚠️ Unlike the bangzi — two whole sticks versus a set of threaded boards — in Hornbostel–Sachs they sit in different cells: 111.11 concussion sticks and 111.12 concussion plaques.

"Board" and "eye": two instruments, one system

This figure is the heart of the page.

*Ban-yan* was not invented by the clappers — it is the division of labour between two instruments:

  • ban = the clack of the paiban, which became the word for the strong beat;
  • yan = the tap of the drum-slip on the drum, which became the word for weak and secondary beats. (That drum is the bangu this site has already written about.)

Four grids hold nearly all Chinese metre:

⭐ And the system was defined in one Ming sentence — Wang Jide's *Rules of Song*: "all tunes, long or short in phrase, many or few in words, tight or slow in tempo — *all are regulated by the board; hence ban-yan*." "Regulated by the board" — that is where the word comes from.

⭐ Details worth setting side by side:

  • It is written into notation. In the gongche family of scores the metre has its own marks — the Nanyin score carries a whole set of "liao-pai" symbols beside each note (see gongchepu).
  • It is not even metre. ⚠️ Not an equals sign for 2/4 and 4/4 — ban-yan is elastic: Jiangnan sizhu says "board steady, eye alive"; Kunqu's *zengban* stretches the whole grid to twice its length.
  • It entered literature early. The Ming scholar Yang Shen, on Cook Ding carving an ox: "the sound of the blade kept time with the ban-yan of the music bureau."
  • It is now part of the language. *You ban you yan* ("with board and eye" — methodical), *zou ban* ("off the board" — off track); in Sichuan dialect *ban-yan-er* means "tricks, business" (a pun: "a bald brush — plenty of *ban-yan-er*").

Three steps: object → measure → system

*Ban-yan* is only the second step. The word keeps climbing:

  • ban-shi — the metrical forms of a sung style: slow board, original board, flowing, two-six, free, guiding, rocking…
  • ban-qiang-ti — ⭐ of the roughly 300-plus opera genres in China, the musical systems are two: ban-qiang-ti and qupai-ti — and "the great majority are ban-qiang-ti." Its full name is "metre-variation form": a matched couplet is the unit, and changes of metre build the music of a whole play. Bangzi and pihuang are its representatives; even narrative-song genres like dagu and Henan zhuizi belong to it. (Compare: qupai-ti links many tunes into suites; ban-qiang-ti varies one tune into every metre.)
  • And in instrumental music the same step appears in another shape — titles that simply count boards: *Eight Boards* (八板) · *Old Six Boards* (老六板).

⭐ Look at the number: *Eight Boards* is "sixty-eight boards" — eight phrases of eight boards, plus four extra boards after the fifth: 8×8 + 4 = 68. The sources are explicit: "each phrase is one *ban*, hence the title *Eight Boards*"; "a hand-board was struck at every beat — hence it is commonly called the sixty-eight-board piece." One strike of a hand-board per beat: the *ban* in the title is that very stroke.

⭐ The reach of the *Eight Boards* family deserves its own line: the *Sixteen Boards* in the 1814 *Xiansuo Beikao* is its earliest known record; its descendants spread across the country — the northern *Old Eight Boards*, Jiangnan sizhu's *Middle-Flower Six Boards* (with the "five generations" chain: six boards · fast · flowered · middle-flowered · slow), Henan's *board-head* pieces, Mongol *Eight Scores*… Nie Er's *Golden Snake Dance* and Li Shutong's *Song of the Motherland* both come from this family. A melody the whole country knows, carrying a wooden board in its name.

⭐ And rule ㉑ (the bangzi: a vocal genre named for a time-keeping instrument) here finds its upgrade: the bangzi named a genre; the paiban named metre itself, and then a system. One name walked the whole road from object to abstraction.

One hand, two instruments: the drum-and-board

The paiban has no player of its own — it belongs to one of the drummer's hands.

HandHoldsGoverns
leftthe paibanevery clack = strong beat
rightthe slip, striking the banguevery tap = eye
  • Together they are "drum-and-board" (鼓板): the clappers are "used mainly to mark the beat in singing, looked after by the opera drummer; customarily named together with the single-skin drum."
  • It is one of the bangu's techniques: the bangu's "single stroke" is precisely "right hand on the drum, left hand on the clappers".
  • ⭐ "Ban-yan", then, is one hand's two jobs — strong beats in the left, weak beats in the right. The whole metric system grew out of these two hands.
  • It can also leave the drummer's hands: in narrative song the book board and falling board (the bamboo *jianban*) are played by the singer himself — as in Henan zhuizi. The board goes back into the singer's own hands.
  • Every genre finds a place for it: Jiangnan sizhu, Suzhou drumming, Fujian nanyin, shifan drumming, Shanxi suites — usually struck on the strong beat; over three hundred opera genres use it.

A Tang dynasty inside five boards (the nanyin paiban)

In Fujian nanyin the paiban grew into another shape — five boards:

  • five long boards of lychee wood on a cord; the two outer boards a little thicker; cord and tassels at the top.
  • The singer plays it himself: in the classic "four instruments complete" setting, "the singer sits at the centre, holding the paiban" — pipa and sanxian to the right, dongxiao and erxian to the left, the clappers at the middle.
  • How it is played: "two boards in the right hand, three in the left," clapped together on each measure's beat, marking the strong beats — a method the sources say "still keeps the Tang way of playing."

⭐ And "the Tang way" comes with a whole chain of evidence:

⚠️ One clarification: the nanyin paiban has five boards, not three; and antiquity also had a nine-board "great paiban" — the count was never fixed, as the next section shows.

⭐ The Tang sentence hides its whole secret — "struck in place of clapping":

  • To "clap" is *bian*. "Struck in place of clapping" = it stands in for the hands.
  • And the same sentence says "as long and wide as a hand": about the size of two hands.
  • From name (clapping-board) to size (hand-sized) to use (instead of clapping) — it is a stand-in for the hand from head to foot. (The dap on this site needed two hands; the paiban exists to let the hands rest.)

⭐ And the variable count deserves its own line:

four (mid-Tang Dunhuang) · five (nanyin) · six (Song court, Wang Jian's tomb, the *Night Revels*) · nine (the ancient "great paiban") · three (today) — the sources conclude: "the number was never fixed; it varied with era and use." The number is variable; the function is one: to beat time.

Common misconceptions

  • "The paiban is the bangzi." ⚠️ Both are time-keeping wood, but: the bangzi is two whole sticks (concussion sticks, 111.11); the paiban is a set of threaded boards (concussion plaques, 111.12). ⭐ One named a vocal genre; one named the metric system itself.
  • "It only beats time; it hardly matters." ⚠️ The reverse — it is the ruler: ban-yan → ban-shi → ban-qiang-ti, three steps; dictionaries gloss *ban-yan* simply as "the metre of opera music."
  • "The nanyin paiban is three boards too." It is five (lychee wood); and Kaiyuan Temple keeps both five-board (Tang apsaras) and six-board (Southern Song pagoda) evidence.
  • "Fewer boards means simplification, progress." ⚠️ Don't rank them: four · five · six · nine · three all occur; the sources say only that it "varied with era and use." It is a function name, not a shape name.
  • "You-ban-you-yan is just an idiom." It was first a musical term (board = strong, eye = weak) before entering everyday speech; in Sichuan it even grew into "tricks, business."
  • "It cannot solo, so it ranks low." ⚠️ It does not solo — it does not produce programmes; it produces time. Every phrase of three hundred genres stands on its scale.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Three cues:

1. Solid, short, piercing. It must cut through a whole band and stage so that every performer hears it — which is why it must be a point, not a line: one strike, no tail. 2. The dryness of hardwood on hardwood. Three boards clapped together: clean, dry, no metal — the name *tanban* comes straight from the material (sandalwood). 3. The timbre of a hand. Remember "struck in place of clapping": its archetypal gesture is a clap — and it sounds like what it is: a harder, surer, louder hand.

  • ⭐⭐ 南音拍板：「泉州南音……拍板，由五块荔木片串成，演唱者双手执拍板，于乐曲强拍处撩拍」；「传统的南音清唱形式是『四管全』，演唱者自执拍板坐于内中」；「南音拍板的奏法，仍保持唐代演奏方法，演奏者右手握两块，左手握三块，双手挚拍板，两手合击在每小节的拍子上」依泉州学研究资料与地方文化资料
  • ⭐⭐ 南音的唐制证据链：「其形制与江苏扬州邗江五代墓出土的拍板一样」；「南音拍板的形制和演奏方法与成都五代前蜀王建墓乐舞浮雕中的击拍板图像相同」；「泉州唐代垂拱年间（公元 686 年）建的开元寺中飞天乐伎的拍板为五板」；「泉州南宋建的东塔塔座须弥座 40 幅浮雕中，有幅《天人赞鹤》图……双手按拍着古制六板的南音拍板」；「开元寺中拥有六板与五板的拍板，也正符合拍板历史的发展轨迹」依泉州学研究与地方文化资料
  • ⭐⭐ 历史（唐以前至清）：「拍板于魏晋时期从西北少数民族地区传入中原」（其源头有资料追溯至上古「击壤」）；「唐代时称『拍板』，用于散乐，在音乐舞蹈中地位颇高，起控制节拍的重要作用……唐玄宗时，梨园乐工黄幡绰善奏此板」；「宋以后……用于民间说唱 · 民间器乐 · 教坊大乐和马后乐，以及唱赚 · 诸宫调等说唱艺术；元代用于宫廷音乐和杂剧；明 · 清时期用于宫廷和民间音乐」依通行乐器资料
  • ⭐⭐ 《旧唐书·音乐志》：「拍板，长阔如手，厚寸余，以韦连之，击以代抃」（「抃」即拍手：用它代替拍手）；「拍板在唐代已列为胡部之乐器，贞观乐舞图也有拍板」；「中唐敦煌乐伎则为四板」；「五代前蜀王建墓石雕 · 《韩熙载夜宴图》为六板，而《西方净土变》则为五板」；「宋代教坊所用为六板」依唐代文献与敦煌 · 文物资料
