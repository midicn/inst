The dunun (Mande bass drums)

One word, two instruments — "dunun" is both this drum and the category "drum"

Related

The djembeThe koraThe mbira (thumb piano)The talking drumTimbre

⭐⭐ The main line: one word, two instruments

「Dunun is also a generic term for 'drum' in the Manding languages.」

⭐⭐ In Manding, dunun is not the name of an instrument — it is the generic word for the category "drum" (dundunba, sangban and kenkeni are the specific names). ⭐⭐ And it has a near-homophone namesake: "The drum is also not to be confused with the dùndún, a type of talking drum used by the Yoruba people" — ⭐⭐ the Mande bass-drum family and the Yoruba talking drum transliterate almost identically and are completely different instruments (compare the talk box's three claimants, which fought over "who"; here two things simply share a name).

⭐⭐ And a Western misnomer: "The name djun djun is a common western misnomer. There is no such word in the Malinké language and this term should not be used" — ⭐⭐ the fourth kind in the site's name-vs-reality lineage: the invented name (compare the proverb naming purpose (djembe), the transliteration naming blood (bili), the misunderstanding naming history (a cappella), the ancestor naming the being called (mbira), the belittled loanword naming identity (griot) — this one is subtler: the West invented a word for an instrument and then used it for a century).

⭐⭐ "Ba" means "big" in Malinké — dundunba literally means "big dunun". ⭐⭐ Compare China's eight-tone system classifying by material (metal, stone, clay, hide, silk, wood, gourd, bamboo) with West Africa classifying by size: two family namings, one built on the body, one on the size.

⭐⭐ And the division of labour sums up in one sentence: "the dunun ensemble……organises the entire rhythmic structure that makes each rhythm recognisable and meaningful" — ⭐⭐ the three drums are not just "the low register": they are three parts — the highest steadies, the middle defines, the deepest sinks.

⭐⭐ Two organisational forms: one player with three drums, or three players with one

  • The Mandingue style: each player takes one drum, laid on its side on the floor or a stand, striking the head with a stick in one hand and a bell (the kenken) mounted on the drum or held in the other;
  • The ballet style (so called from the national ballet ensembles): "one player has command of the three dununs standing on the floor".

⭐⭐ Two answers to one question: is the unit one instrument per person, or one person per three instruments? (compare the djembe entry's many-part division — lead, three dunun brothers, dancers — against this one's splitting in a single player). ⭐⭐ And the crucial line: "A melody is created across the interplay of the three dununs" — ⭐⭐ a melody that belongs to no single drum, arising from the relation between them (compare the kora, whose left-thumb bass and right-thumb melody are two lines inside one hand; the dunun is a conversation between three instruments — African polyphony has three methods: division within one hand, interlocking within one instrument, dialogue across several).

⭐⭐ Onomatopoeia as naming: West African instruments are named by their sound

「The names of the drums are onomatopoeic, meaning that they sound like the thing they describe.」

⭐⭐ dunun (dun) · kenken (clank) · shekere (shak) · sege sege · kese-kese (rattle) — ⭐⭐ a whole family on the site whose names are their own sounds (the instance "Dong Ga Da Ling": remember the sound first, then the drum), standing beside mbira's "the ancestors' voice" as the second naming route: ⭐⭐ one names itself by its sound, one names itself by the being it calls — both are more like instructions than names.

⭐⭐ An instrument that changed jobs: it once carried messages

「Formerly the dunun was used for communication to announce local rulers and public affairs.」

⭐⭐ Read against the djembe entry — the djembe side says "the Malinké never used the djembe as a signaling drum" (that was the talking drum's job), while the dunun side says "formerly it announced local rulers and public affairs". ⭐⭐ The signalling function passes from one drum to another: the dunun hands over the job to the dedicated talking drum and goes to work as the foundation — ⭐⭐ instruments change jobs (compare the 808: from commercial failure nobody wanted to hip-hop's central instrument — an instrument's post can be swapped out entirely).

⭐ And its present post is this: the melody of the three is woven, and the weave is the rhythm's identity — each traditional rhythm is bound to an occasion (Kuku, Sunu, Soli, Kassa, Soko, Dununba: harvest, initiation, marriage, a particular caste's celebration, a community festival), ⭐⭐ so in this system "what is this rhythm called" equals "what is this occasion".

⭐⭐ Globalisation: one master runs a school

⭐ Mamady Keïta's Tam Tam Mandingue school has branches in many countries; Famoudou Konaté, Mohamed Diaby and Bolokada Conde teach worldwide; Serge Blanc and others publish methods and video instruction. ⭐⭐ Compare the djembe's route into the world — national folkloric ensembles plus touring masters — with the dunun's: a master running a school — ⭐⭐ the organisation form that teaches survives longer than the one that tours (compare MIDI in K-48: the standard outlived its makers because it taught people how to use it).
