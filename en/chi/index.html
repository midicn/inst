The dizi and the chi look like twins — and their whole difference compresses into one word: the *foot*.

Song-dynasty *Yue Shu* defines the chi in six characters: "the chi is a flute with a foot." The dizi is hollow end to end; the chi is stopped at the end.

⚠️ Beyond that one word, three more differences (the sources list them plainly): where the blow-hole sits (head-end for the dizi, mid-body possible for the chi), how the holes relate (blow-hole and finger-holes in one line for the dizi, ninety degrees apart for the chi), and how it is held (one palm out, one in for the dizi; both palms in for the chi). ⭐⭐ Rule ㊱: "a flute with a foot" — one word apart, two characters; and its whole life hangs on one institution. The foot turns "bright" into "full and solemn," "better suited to sorrow and longing"; and its fate is just as plain: after the Song it lived almost only in ritual music — an instrument that serves one institution dies with it, leaving diagrams, photos and reconstructions.

Zeng Hou Yi's two lacquered pipes

Its most famous "appearance" was in 1978:

  • Two bamboo pipes from tomb no. 1 at Leigudun: one thicker, one thinner, 30.2 and 29.3 cm; flute-like in shape, but the tail closed by a natural node — physical proof of the "foot."
  • Seven holes: one "upper" by the node, five in a row on the outer face, one "inner" by the other node.
  • ⭐ The identification was pure scholarship: compare them with Qing-dynasty chi, consult the chi diagrams in the *Sancai Tuhui*, the *Yuhai* and the *Huangchao Liyue Tushi* — "these two pipes are certainly the chi." The texts and diagrams came first; the burial came later — archaeology checking a picture a millennium old.
  • ⚠️ And they can no longer sound: two thousand years in water left the bamboo flesh rotted away, only skin and lacquer remaining — "like a tube rolled from a film." The holes are clear; the voice is gone. What saved them was lacquer (髹漆).
  • The pitches come from replicas: six notes — a full pentatonic scale plus one altered tone.

Range

⭐ Six notes looks thin — but it spent its life in ritual music, whose repertoire never asks a player to show off: it asks for steadiness, correctness, and blending with bells and chimes.

Three idioms and two stories

No instrument on this site carries more idioms — and all of them are kind:

SourcePhraseMeaning
*Book of Songs*"the elder brother blows the xun, the younger the chi"brothers in harmony
later usage"xun-chi friendship"close friends
later usage"like xun and chi" · "xun and chi in accord"harmony,默契

⭐ All of them are about accord: the xun (earth) and the chi (bamboo) were a playing pair — one low, one high, one earthen, one bamboo. Two instruments that fit together became the word for people fitting together. (The xun page carries the same half-line — both pages are needed for the pair.)

And two stories, both about music solving things:

  • ⭐ Wu Zixu begs with a chi. The *Records of the Grand Historian*: fleeing through Zhaoguan, with nothing to eat, he "puffed out his belly and played the chi, begging in the market of Wu" — a ritual instrument that once served as a beggar's bowl.
  • ⭐⭐ The old woman who played the chi (*Luoyang Qielan Ji*, ch. 4). In the Northern Wei, a maidservant of Prince Yuan Chen named Chaoyun was a fine chi player. When the Qiang in Qinzhou revolted and "repeated campaigns failed to subdue them," the prince had her dress as a poor old woman and play while begging. She played the *Longshang Melody* — their own home tune. And: "The Qiang all wept. They said to one another: 'Why abandon our graves and homes to be bandits in the valleys?' And they surrendered in droves." Hence the local saying: "A swift horse and a strong man are no match for an old woman playing the chi." (A Ming couplet later reworked it as "the fair maid plays the flute, the old maid plays the chi.") ⭐ The loveliest part: she played no lofty ritual music — she played their home dialect in sound. A rebellion that swords could not end was ended by a tune from back home.

From the Rites of Zhou to the Confucian temple: a ritual-only lifeline

⭐ Read beside the guanzi's chart and two fates emerge:

  • The guanzi "sank" from court to folk wind bands — and is played today;
  • The chi stayed inside ritual music — "after the Song it faded, used mainly in court ritual"; by Ming–Qing it left diagrams; the 1957 Qufu photo is its last living appearance.

It did not die of music. It died of an institution.

Common misconceptions

  • "Chi is just an old word for dizi." ⚠️ Two instruments: the dizi is hollow, the chi has a foot — plus blow-hole position, hole alignment, hand position and timbre. (As early as the Han, sources note the two names were "near in sound but different in form" — the confusion is ancient.)
  • "Its foot stops it sounding." ⚠️ The reverse: the foot *is* its form. The Zeng Hou Yi pipes are silent because the bamboo flesh rotted, not because of their design — replicas sound six notes.
  • "Just a bit-player in ritual music." ⚠️ It was one of the twenty fixed instruments of Sui–Tang ritual; in 1113 one court band had twenty-eight chi players; and with the xun it forms the Chinese word for harmony.
  • "Everything in the *Book of Songs* is rarefied." ⚠️ Wu Zixu begged with a chi in the market of Wu; Chaoyun, disguised as a poor old woman, ended a revolt with a tune from home.
  • "Lost means lost forever." ⚠️ Today the Yanfei chi is built from the excavated finds (with extra holes for range), and the Shengyueshu at the Temple of Heaven has reconstructed it from the texts with modern materials — it is coming back, in another body.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Three cues:

1. Hear the stopped roundness. Beside the dizi it loses brightness and gains thickness — the sources' words: "full and solemn." 2. Hear the steadiness. Its whole life was spent blending with bells and chimes — its aesthetic is fitting in, not standing out. 3. Hear the longing. The sources say it suits "sorrow and longing" — poets wrote the dizi often and the chi seldom; and whenever the chi appears, it is nearly always at a distance, or in loss.
