Flute

The only woodwind with no reed — its sound comes from an air jet cut against the edge of the embouchure hole

1 min read84 characters条目WoodwindsUpdated 2026-09-26

The flute is the only woodwind with no reed. Its sound is not pushed out by a reed but made by a jet of air being cut against the sharp edge of the embouchure hole. That distinction matters far more than wood versus metal: modern flutes are almost always silver or gold, yet in Hornbostel–Sachs they sit with the bamboo flute, the recorder and the ocarina in aerophones · edge-tone.

In other words: classification follows what vibrates, not what it is made of. A flute's source is a column of air; the material of the tube colours the sound but does not decide the category.

⚠️ One conceptual point: "woodwind" makes most people think "a tube of wood". But the woodwind section is an orchestral grouping — the instruments driven by an air column — and the flute, though metal, belongs to it, just as the double bass is wooden yet belongs to the strings. Materials change; the way sound is made does not.

Structure: three joints and a key system

Three points:

1. Three joints. Headjoint, body and foot joint meet at tenons and come apart for the case — the flute is one of the few orchestral instruments that stays in tune after being taken to pieces. 2. The hole positions are the intonation. The wall carries a row of holes and the keys merely extend the fingers: press a key, the pad lifts, the effective tube length changes, the pitch changes. So a flute's intonation is geometry, not embouchure. 3. The embouchure hole is not a vent; it is a cutting edge. To sound at all, the jet must strike the far edge at an angle and be split in two — half into the tube, half out. That is the next section.

Keys: modern flutes use the Böhm system (established mid-19th century), in which keys are linked so that one finger can operate several holes. This is what lets all twelve semitones be played with something close to natural fingering — the technical precondition for free modulation.

How it sounds: a jet, cut by an edge

Why does "a cut jet" settle into a steady note?

1. The jet strikes the edge → part of it is steered into the tube, part escapes outside. 2. The part inside makes a compression wave that travels to the end of the effective length and reflects. 3. When the reflection returns to the hole it flips the next piece of jet either into or away from the edge — so the cutting repeats by itself. 4. Once this "jet–edge–wave" loop matches the tube, it becomes self-sustaining.

The key consequence: pitch is set by the effective tube length (the hole positions); jet speed only decides whether it speaks and how loudly. Which is why, on a flute, fingering held constant and air increased barely moves the pitch — but changes the colour a lot.

Range

  • Low register (C4–G4): breathy and covered, with audible breath noise — the flute's most recognisable colour, used since Debussy for "empty, distant, still".
  • Middle register (A4–C6): the fullest and most used; most melodies live here.
  • Top register (above C6): brilliant and piercing, and also the hardest to place in tune (a finer jet and more exact angle are needed).

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. A permanent layer of breath noise. The tone always carries the sound of moving air — most obvious low down. It is the signature of jet drive, and reed instruments do not have it. 2. A soft attack. Unlike a plucked instrument the note fades in; its onset is gentle and unaggressive, which is why the flute suits lyrical lines and not rhythmic accents. 3. More air changes colour, not just volume. Pushing more air adds upper harmonics, so the tone brightens: at p and f the same flute is almost two instruments. 4. Evenness within a register. Every note comes from the same tube with holes opening and closing, so there are no string changes or position shifts to hear.

Playing techniques

The technique list is unusually long, and a few items are the flute's alone:

  • Air / breathy tone: deliberately let part of the jet escape so only noise sounds — the "blown" colour of the low register.
  • Flutter tongue: roll the air as in a uvular trill, giving a buzzing note; a favourite of modern works.
  • Key clicks: no air at all, just pads striking holes — pure percussive clicks.
  • Harmonics: force a higher partial on the same fingering for a hollow, glassy sound.
  • Vibrato: produced by abdominal air pulses, not by the fingers — an important difference from string vibrato.

Note the notation rules in that table. The flute family are C instruments (except the alto flute), yet both the piccolo and the alto flute are written away from sounding pitch — the same problem as the clarinet and English horn. The reason is transposition: so that every wind player can read the same fingering chart, the page shows the fingers, not the sound.

Common misconceptions

  • "A flute is a woodwind, so it is made of wood." Modern flutes are metal. "Woodwind" names a section — the air-driven instruments — not a material.
  • "A flute and a recorder are the same thing." Both are edge-tone, but the drive differs: the recorder's air is shaped by a fixed duct, the flute's by the lips directly. Hence the flute's far greater range of colour, and techniques such as air and flutter that a recorder cannot produce.
  • "Blow harder and the note goes up." Pitch comes from the effective tube length. More air mostly changes loudness and brightness, with only a small effect on pitch.
  • "The flute only plays high." Its low register (around C4) is its most distinctive region — breathy and hollow, and often used to evoke stillness.
  • "Keys exist because fingers are too short." Keys solve intonation and modulation: they let all twelve semitones be played with near-natural fingering, which is what makes free modulation possible.
