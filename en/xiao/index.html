The xiao's relationship with the dizi is the cleanest comparison in this batch: one bamboo tube, one edge-tone principle, six holes each — and only three differences.

⚠️ Of the three, only one can be removed on the spot. | Difference | Dizi | Xiao | Changeable live? | |---|---|---|---| | Way of blowing | transverse | end-blown | no | | Membrane | reed membrane fitted | no membrane hole | yes — peel the membrane off and the sound tilts to the xiao | | Result | bright · outgoing · loud | quiet · inward · soft | — | This is the purest proof of the "added part" thread. The erhu's python skin and the crumhorn's wind cap are cases of "a part was added and the colour changed" — but the dizi membrane is a case of "a part was added and it became a different instrument". It is a wafer-thin strip pasted over one small hole, and it is the clearest dividing line between dizi and xiao.

Structure: a bamboo tube that stands up

Three points:

1. Closed top, notched edge. The bamboo node is left in place at the top and only a notch is cut into its edge — the air is split on that edge (edge tone), so it is blown end-on rather than into a mouthpiece. 2. Five in front, one behind. The back hole exists because of end-blown holding: with both hands vertical, a thumb naturally rests on the back. 3. No membrane hole. One hole fewer and one membrane fewer — and that one hole settles its timbre.

The distance of one membrane

This figure pushes the "added part" thread to its limit:

The mere presence of one wafer of reed separates two instruments. Which is also why the "what vibrates" test is harder than "what it looks like": dizi and xiao are so alike in shape, hole count and even blowing that they could be mistaken for each other — yet one membrane sends their sounds in different directions.

Range

  • About two octaves, slightly narrower than the dizi — end-blowing wastes more of the breath, so the top notes are harder to reach.
  • Deep below, round in the middle, tense above: three registers with distinct characters.
  • Soft playing is where it excels. What moves people is not how brightly it can blow but how quietly it can blow and still hold together — the same aesthetic as the guqin.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Quiet, round, slightly breathy. End-blowing splits the airstream on the notch, so breath and pitch arrive together — the source of its reticence. 2. Little volume, unhurried speech. It is not made for fast figures, but for long lines. 3. Soft playing carries the expression. The opposite of the dizi: the quieter, the more skill it shows. 4. It pairs naturally with the qin. Qin–xiao duets are a staple of literati music: both are quiet instruments, both prize after-ring and restraint.

Playing techniques

  • The embouchure decides everything. No mouthpiece, no membrane: lips and air angle do it all — so the same xiao sounds quite different in different hands.
  • Held vertically, both hands at work. Five holes in front are covered by the fingers, the back thumb hole by one thumb.
  • It wins on breath. Long notes, soft playing and the shaping of the airstream are its main expressive means.
  • Overblowing for the top. As with the dizi: extra pressure excites higher modes.
  • Usually with the qin or in silk-and-bamboo ensembles — Jiangnan sizhu, Cantonese music, Yue opera — and the qin–xiao duet is its most representative setting.

"Transverse is the dizi, end-blown is the xiao" is a well-known mnemonic — but it names only one of the three differences. The real tests remain: is it the air in the tube (aerophone) or something else? And: is the membrane there?

The Qing treatise *Lülü Zhengyi Houbian* puts it beautifully:

"Today's xiao is a little under one foot eight inches, blown from the top, with a hole at the back; the di is blown transversely and has no back hole. So today's xiao is the di of antiquity."

Today's xiao is what "di" once meant. Over two millennia the names changed places: in the Qin–Tang period "xiao" was the panpipe and "di" the end-blown tube; after the Tang the transverse flute grew popular, and the end-blown tube inherited the name "xiao". The same problem as the pipa's "name that describes an action" and the dizi's "one name, several instruments": names outlive objects, and they often fall out of step with them.

Common misconceptions

  • "A xiao is just a dizi held upright." Structurally it differs in a few things, but one of them is irreversible: the dizi has a membrane hole, the xiao does not. Transverse versus end-blown is a matter of technique; the presence of the membrane is a matter of construction.
  • "It is quiet because it is poorly made." Like the guqin, it is designed for small spaces — solitude, a study, a duet with the qin — it has no need to be loud.
  • "It has always been called the xiao." From the Qin to the Tang, "xiao" meant the panpipe, and the single end-blown tube was called *di*. The name only settled on it after the Tang.
  • "Being simpler, it is more primitive." The opposite: end-blowing demands finer breath control, which is why the tradition says the xiao is harder to learn; its volume and intonation depend more on the player.
  • "It only plays sad music." It is known for quiet bleakness, yet it is a regular instrument of Jiangnan sizhu, Cantonese music and Yue opera accompaniment.
  • "The bayin says bamboo, so only bamboo will do." There were historically jade and porcelain xiao; the bayin records its usual material (the same point as the guqin's silk).

Next

Bamboo now has two entries: the dizi (transverse, membraned) and the xiao (end-blown, bare) — together they finish the story of "one material, two treatments".

Next comes the loudest of the winds: the suona. It too has a wooden body, but its sound source is a double reed, and it pushes the batch's "social origin" thread to the opposite extreme: from court ritual out into village weddings and funerals — how loud an instrument can be is usually tied directly to where it is used. :::
