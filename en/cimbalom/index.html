The cimbalom is what the santur's line became when it was engineered in the 19th century:

the same trapezoid box, the same "bridges cut the courses into segments" principle, the same two hammers — but in 1874 somebody added four legs, a damper pedal and four chromatic octaves.

After that it went from a folk instrument to a solo instrument that could stand beside a piano.

Structure: an instrument the size of a table

It is a full size larger than a santur, and three things were added:

AdditionWhat it buys
Four detachable legsthe surface rises to about 75 cm so the player sits — the instrument becomes "furniture", no longer resting on a table or barrel
A damper pedalcontrol over the whole instrument's sustain (next section)
Fully chromatic, four octavesevery semitone, E2–E6 — a folk instrument becomes a composer's instrument

The strings are also laid out in two layers: steel trebles in groups of four, copper-wound basses in groups of three — thick bass strings need only three to carry, thin trebles need four to be heard.

The bridges work exactly as on a santur: two long bridges divide each course into three segments, and the player strikes whichever side they want. So the number of playable positions far exceeds the number of courses.

The damper pedal: short by default, long when pressed

This is the cimbalom's biggest difference from the santur, and the technical reason it can enter a concert hall.

What matters is the default state:

  • Pedal released, the felt rests on the strings → a strike gives a clean, short note.
  • Pedal pressed, all the felt lifts at once → the strings ring freely and the note carries a long tail.

That mechanism turned the cimbalom from "a santur whose notes will not stop" into an instrument that controls its own sustain. Fast passagework with the pedal up (clear), harmonic bedding with the pedal down (resonant). Composers can therefore give it pedal markings, much as they do for a piano.

Range

Four octaves and fully chromatic — the decisive difference from the santur. A santur's bridges are set for a mode (some degrees simply are not on the instrument); a cimbalom's strings cover all twelve semitones, so it can play music in any key without re-placing bridges.

Range, chromaticism and pedal together carried it into art music at the end of the 19th century: one instrument that can read any key, control its sustain, run fast and lay down chords.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. A more solid attack than a santur. Thicker strings, a bigger box and cotton-wrapped beater tips give a definite onset without harshness. 2. Press the pedal and the sound opens up. The same passage with and without pedal are two different effects — the easiest way to identify a cimbalom, and where a player's control shows most. 3. Tremolo. Two hands alternating rapidly on a note or a group produces a continuous sheet of sound — the most common device in Hungarian and Roma music, and the cimbalom's most recognisable moment in an ensemble. 4. Upper and lower halves sound different. Basses on wound strings are thick and dark; trebles on steel are bright and crisp — and the two hands can work in different registers at once, giving two simultaneous lines.

Playing techniques

Four blocks make up the technique:

  • Beaters: two sticks about 25 cm long (cane or wood), tips bare, wound with twine, or covered in felt or cotton. Hard tips for clarity and melody, soft for bedding — changing tips is changing timbre.
  • Tremolo and runs: alternating hands for both sustained notes and rapid passagework. The showiest part of the technique, and the most striking element of the Roma band tradition.
  • Glissando: the beater is swept along the strings for a run. With dense courses a cimbalom glissando is "wetter" than a santur's — close in effect to a harp glissando.
  • Pedal: as on a piano, the pedal is part of the technique. When and how long you press decides whether the same music sounds clear or smeared.

The division of labour with the santur: both use two hammers, but the cimbalom adds a whole layer of time control (the pedal) — so it can do what a santur cannot: make a long note stop.

The cimbalom is the only member of this family that reached the concert hall — and that follows directly from the pedal (and the range and volume that came with it).

History

PeriodState
From the 16th c.the hammered dulcimer reaches Hungary along Roma and Ottoman routes; becomes a village and tavern instrument
18th–19th c.played by Roma bands (*banda cigánya*), mostly to accompany dancing
1874József Schunda of Budapest rebuilds it: longer strings, rearranged bridges, four legs, a damper pedal, fully chromatic, four octaves — the modern concert cimbalom is born

20th c.Kodály (*Háry János*), Stravinsky (*Renard*, *Ragtime for 11 Instruments*), Boulez, Ligeti, Eötvös and Birtwistle all write for it

From tavern instrument to avant-garde scores in under a century — and the technical precondition was those three changes of 1874 (legs, pedal, chromaticism). It is the same rule this site keeps running into: an instrument's fate is often decided by engineering details, not by how "musical" it is (compare the viola's size problem and the viol's volume problem).

Common misconceptions

  • "It is just a bigger santur." Structurally the same branch, but it adds a pedal and full chromaticism. Those two turn "an instrument whose bridges are set for a mode" into "an instrument that can read any score in any key" — a change of kind, not of size.
  • "The pedal is for sustaining." More precisely, the default is damping: felt rests on the strings and the pedal lifts it. Released = short notes, pressed = long ones — the same logic as a piano's sustain pedal.
  • "Struck instruments cannot control their sustain." True of a santur, not of a cimbalom. Being able to control the tail is precisely why it reached the concert hall.
  • "It is only a folk instrument." Folk tradition is its root, but 20th-century scores by Kodály, Stravinsky, Boulez and Ligeti exist. The two lines run in parallel, not in succession.
  • "Any beater tip will do." The tip material (bare wood, twine, felt, cotton) changes the attack and the timbre directly, and players switch hardness with the phrase.
  • "More strings means more notes." As with the santur: two long bridges cut each course into three, so the number of positions far exceeds the number of courses. The mechanism matters more than the count.
