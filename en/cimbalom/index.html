Cimbalom

The Hungarian concert hammered dulcimer — legs, a damper pedal, fully chromatic, four octaves, engineered in 1874

1 min read158 characters条目StringsUpdated 2026-09-26

Three changes in 1874

In 1874 József Schunda of Budapest gave a trapezoid hammered dulcimer four legs, a damper pedal and four fully chromatic octaves. Before that it lived in taverns and at weddings; after it, it could enter a concert hall and sit beside a piano. Same box, same bridges cutting the courses into segments, same two beaters — only three engineering details changed.

An instrument the size of a table

The top measures about 145 by 95 cm and the whole thing weighs around 100 kg, with the playing surface 75 cm off the floor so the player sits, one hand's territory each side. There are 125 to 145 strings: steel trebles in fours, copper-wound basses in threes, because a thick string needs three to carry and a thin one needs four to be heard. Two long bridges cut each course into three, so the playable positions far outnumber the courses.

Short by default, long when pressed

The pedal governs the ring of every string, and the default is damping: felt rests on the strings and a stroke gives a clean short note; press and all the felt lifts together. Fast passagework with the pedal up, harmonic bedding with it down, which lets composers mark the pedal as they would for a piano. The pedal is not only for sustain; it decides where phrases break.

Four octaves, twelve semitones

A santur's bridges are set for a mode, so some degrees are simply not on the instrument; a cimbalom carries all twelve semitones and can read a score in any key. E2 to E6, and not a transposing instrument.

The attack is more solid than a santur's, the beater tips often wrapped in cotton. Press the pedal and the sound opens: the same passage with and without it are two different effects, and a recording shows a player's control there sooner than anywhere else. Tremolo is the common device in Hungarian and Roma music. Liszt put the cimbalom into the orchestral version of the Hungarian Rhapsodies, a road from folk band to concert platform; Kodály, Stravinsky, Boulez and Ligeti followed in the twentieth century, and the folk line never stopped.
