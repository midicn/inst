The ney

A reed that was cut away — on the way back

A reed that was cut away

A ney is a length of reed cut from the bed, bored and bevelled at one end. Rumi wrote about it in the thirteenth century: pipe and body are both hollow vessels, and the flute's lament is a longing to return to the bed it was cut from. It is not sad like a flute; the flute itself is telling a story about separation.

Four and a half thousand years without a break

Players are painted on the walls of Egyptian pyramids, and actual ney were dug out at Ur. It has been played for 4,500 years, one of the oldest instruments still in use; the excavation site and the performance site are the same place. It is also the ancestor of the transverse flute. The name is the material: ney is an old Persian word for reed, the plant Arundo donax.

Seven holes, and what lies between

A hollow stalk of nine segments, bored narrow and bevelled at one end. The Arabic form has no mouthpiece and is blown at the edge; the Turkish form has carried an oblique başpare since the eleventh century. Six finger holes in front, one behind, and a Persian ney about fifty to seventy-five centimetres long.

Each hole spans a semitone to three quarter tones. Microtones come from partly covering a hole, reshaping the mouth, changing the angle and force of the pipe. The oud finds them with a finger, the qanun with a lever, the ney with breath. One maqam system, three answers. The Arabic set divides into seven pipes, from Rast to Ajam, carried as a whole case.

Low or high belongs to the occasion

Pitch marks an occasion rather than a taste. Pastoral players use shorter, higher pipes; schools and religious settings use the low register; the Turks use longer instruments. In the Mevlevi ceremony the flute leads; traditionally only men played it.

The name shifts: gasba in the Maghreb, narh in Sindh, nel in Balochistan, while in Romania nai means the pan flute. English reed covers plant and tongue, so the instrument is often translated as a reed pipe.
