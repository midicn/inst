This page closes the percussion class — not one more instrument, but all of them put back at their posts.

The last page asked how precise one instrument can be; this page asks the other half: how much can a pile of unpitched things carry in a composite art?

⚠️ The answer is written in an old troupe saying: "One set of gongs and drums is half the play." (From a southern-Fujian proverb, passed down in theatre troupes.) A CCTV series on operatic sayings breaks the work into four functions: stirring emotion · magnifying and supporting movement · suggesting place and imitating effects · reinforcing rhythm. And the hardest line of all: "In some scenes the strings may rest, but the gongs and drums run from first to last." ⭐⭐ Rule ㉚: Chinese opera handed its whole "dispatch system" to a group of instruments that play no melody — time is theirs (beats, metres, the "intro" before every song); movement is theirs (gesture, walking, fighting); mood, entrances and exits are theirs too. A composite art handed over its operating system to a percussion band.

"Changmian": the band at the stage mouth

The opera band has an old name: changmian (场面), "the scene." Its origin is a piece of stage history:

  • Early stages were square. The players sat between the entrance and exit doors at the back of the stage: strings to the left, gongs to the right, the drummer holding the clappers in the centre. *Changmian* originally meant that "front of the stage" arrangement.
  • To stop blocking the actors, the band first moved to the right of the entrance door, and finally settled left of the exit door — the corner you see today.
  • It divides in two: the wenchang (strings and winds, serving the singing) and the wuchang (percussion, serving the drama) — together the "civil and martial band." Each half has a head: the qinshi for the strings, the gushi (drum master) for the percussion.
  • Early bands were six to eight players, with doubling — small instruments hang off somebody's hands (as this batch's pengling and bangzi know well).

The wuchang's kit: single-skin drum, tanggu, bowl drum, sandalwood clappers, Guangdong clappers, big gong, small gong, cymbals, small cymbals, big nao, peng zhong, soup gong… But only four are main players: drum-and-board · big gong · small gong · cymbals — and the drum-and-board leads.

⚠️ Line-ups differ by genre: in Yu opera the wuchang is called the "five hard hands" (drum-and-board · bangzi · big gong · hand cymbals · small gong); bangzi genres add their bangzi. "Wuchang" is a table of posts, not a fixed list of instruments.

⭐ More important than the list is how the list is used:

  • Every pattern has a "drum point" — the basic skeleton struck by the bangu, "like the beams of a house."
  • "Sets" are fixed combinations (duo tou, fengdian tou, yifeng shu…), which every player must know cold; but a good drummer never repeats them mechanically — he adds flowers or cuts words: "at a pause, a glance from the actor, the drummer must instantly judge whether it is a cold strike or a muffled gong, and fit the exact silence."
  • Three layers of craft: agreement, measure (speed and force), change (same pattern, different situations).

How it treats the singing: lead · bridge · support · lift

For the voice, four words sum up the accompaniment: lead · bridge · support · lift:

WordWhat it does
leadthe prelude brings the song out (the job of the "intro")
bridgefills the gaps between phrases
supportholds the singing up, making it fuller
liftlets the emotion go further

⭐ And the wuchang's place among these four is special: it does not "support" melody — it supports time. Every gesture, every rise and fall of speech "must fit his drum-point seamlessly"; the start, stop, speed and slowness of the singing are held on the spot by the drum master's left hand (the board) and right hand (the slip).

The command layer: a written language, and a "nerve centre"

What *luogu-jing* is, the small gong page has already unpacked (the syllable chart, onomatopoeia, differences by genre). Here, only its function:

  • It is a command language: "players must first learn — must first memorise — the luogu-jing before they can understand how to play." Chant it first; then play it. One recitation and the crew knows the next stretch.
  • It is the grammar of improvisation: to Peking-opera drum masters it is "the grammar that gives a stage action its time, place and emotional key" — the drama's "auditory engine," the structure's "adhesive"; the drum master is the stage's "nerve centre," who "writes sentences with his mind": finite patterns, infinite situations.
  • It is the eyes on the spot: "before the actor enters, the drum master holds his sticks and stares at the stage mouth; while the gong, small gong and cymbal players stare at the drum master, waiting for his cue."
  • And it is a human craft: "a well-matched drum master and star are usually partners for life — as Hang Zihe drummed for Yu Shuyan for thirty-odd years; Bai Dengyun for Cheng Yanqiu for twenty."

One pattern, two treatments

The system's real test is that the same pattern can be handled in opposite ways:

  • Chang chui: in civil scenes, slow and soft; in martial scenes, tight and fierce.
  • Liushui: usually for walking — but in *The Empty City*, under Zhuge Liang's zither it must be detached and steady, set against the tension.
  • ⭐ Contrast by silence: the hardest move is not playing — "a life-and-death fight at the tightest tempo, and the instruments stop entirely, leaving the stage silent, the audience holding its breath."
  • ⭐ And mixed scoring: in *Farewell My Concubine*'s sword dance, "only strings play the tune [Ye Shen Chen], with a large tanggu keeping the beat" — strings and percussion dividing one scene.

Not playing is one of its uses. That is the last discipline of this family.

Common misconceptions

  • "Percussion is just the 'noise' that accompanies." ⚠️ It is the dispatch layer: time, movement, entrances and mood are all its; "in some scenes the strings may rest, but the gongs and drums run from first to last."
  • "Luogu-jing is a score." It is closer to a set of commands: "memorise first, then you can play" — built to give orders on the spot, not to be framed on a wall.
  • "The two halves work separately." ⚠️ One commander rules both: the bangu is "the band's chief conductor (strings included), and the link between the performance (song, speech, gesture, combat) and its accompaniment."
  • "Percussion is for fight scenes." ⚠️ Civil scenes need it just as much: the "luan chui" in *The Empty City*, the tanggu in *Farewell My Concubine*; even Wang Baochuan's entrance "man chang chui" — it, too, is percussion speaking.
  • "The pattern is fixed; just play it." ⚠️ Playing by the set is only the entrance exam — a good drummer adds flowers, cuts words, and catches a pause or a glance. Agreement, measure, change: all three.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Three cues:

1. Hear the points, then the ensemble. Each instrument is extremely short and dry — all their use lies in when they sound, and with whom. 2. It is a grammar; listen by the grammar. In the audience, ask "who is speaking here?" — a big-gong stroke is weight; a roll of cymbals is density; a small-gong tap is tone. 3. Hear the stop. The hardest pleasure is the silence: those seconds when the drums hold back are often the loudest in the play.
