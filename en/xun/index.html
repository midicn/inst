Xun

A fired-clay vessel instrument whose pitch comes from cavity volume and open area, not from any tube length

1 min read161 characters条目Chinese WindsUpdated 2026-09-26

A nine-hole xun sits in the palm heavier than it looks. A flute of the same size feels like an empty tube; this feels like a river pebble.

A cavity that leaks

Draw the section and the mystery goes: a hollow belly, one blow hole, holes cut into the wall. A dizi is a shortened air column; a xun is a body of air that leaks. Acoustics calls it a vessel flute and models it as a Helmholtz resonator: pitch comes from cavity volume and the total area of every opening.

None of that shows outside. Every hole opens into one cavity, and closing one changes that total area. There is no channel through it; the whole body sounds.

Why the holes can follow the hand

A flute is tuned by how much has been cut away, so its holes land where proportion demands, however uneven that leaves them. A xun is tuned by how much it leaks: where a hole sits matters far less than how much area it opens. The maker places holes where fingers rest, then tunes by reaming the cavity. The layout serves the hand, not the physics. A vessel cannot overblow, so every new note needs a new hole.

A range built hole by hole

The Hemudu xun is seven thousand years old and has a blow hole and nothing else: one note. Banpo added a finger hole and sounded a minor third. By the Spring and Autumn period six holes covered pentatonic and heptatonic scales. A nine-hole instrument measures D4 closed, E5 open, a little over an octave, added generation by generation.

The sound of early autumn

The Book of Rites calls the xun the sound of early autumn, and the image is exact: the clay cavity wears a layer off the highs, leaving a breath-wrapped core, receding, clean, cool. With no duct the volume stays small and phrases cost more breath.

The line broke once. By the Qing the craft had died, until Wu Xunyuan copied a Shang five-hole pear-shaped xun and left the Tanghu Xunpu, the only xun manual printed. In 1979 Chen Zhong made the first eight-hole xun playable on stage. Resin instruments are cheap now; intonation is not.

Next time a melody goes by like wind over an empty bottle, hold off on calling it sad.
