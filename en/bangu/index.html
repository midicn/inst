The bangu is the first instrument in this site's percussion group whose job is not to make sound but to give orders.

Its body is 25 cm across — but most of that is a thick wooden board. The area that actually vibrates and sounds is the middle 5 to 10 cm. By volume it is the smallest instrument in the family; by authority it is the largest: the whole performance's speed and its starts and stops are held in its hands.

⚠️ It forces a turning-point question: what makes a percussion instrument qualify to conduct the band? We have already seen how volume buys social radius (the daluo: eight hundred metres). The bangu takes the other road — its radius does not depend on carrying far: | | Daluo | Bangu | |---|---|---| | The radius | acoustic (800 m) | ⭐ attentional | | What it relies on | being loud | ⭐ everyone having to watch it | | Sound shape | a very long tail (a mass of sound) | ⭐ a very short tail (a point) | | Job | signalling the distance | ⭐ commanding the people in the room | ⭐ Rule ⑰: a radius can also be extended by authority rather than by volume. And authority needs handles. The bangu has two: it hands out *speed*, and speed has to be decided by someone; and its sound is so short that it can be used as a stopwatch. (A long-ringing daluo can be punctuation but never a stopwatch.)

Structure: a small drum inside a board

Three points:

1. ⭐ "Bangu" is literally true of its construction, too. The body is built from five thick hardwood boards and is 25 cm across — most of it is board, and only the skin over the central hole can vibrate freely. (⚠️ The usual explanation of the name is "played by one player together with the *ban* clappers", with a second account "used only by theatre companies"; "a drum inside a board" is a coincidence of construction, but it does help the name stick.) 2. The bore flares like the character 八 (narrow at the top, wide at the base), with an iron ring at the bottom. The skin is stretched over the entire board and wrapped to the rim, held by many closely set tacks — the whole hide is stretched just to leave one small patch in the middle able to speak. 3. Neither volume nor size matters here; concentration of timbre does. A small heart and a tight skin mean a very short tail, a crisp attack and enormous penetration — and that is what makes it a conductor.

One instrument, two signals

This figure is the heart of the page.

Its timbre is short, crisp, concentrated — the physical precondition for conducting: a stopwatch has to tick one tick at a time, and a long-ringing daluo can never set a tempo.

But the real point is the second channel.

⭐ It has two sets of signals.

  • Audible: the drum patterns. "Slow changchui" says *unhurried*; "jijifeng" says *fast and tense*; "sijitou" says *strike a pose here*. Each one is an instruction to the whole company, not merely a rhythm.
  • Visible: the drummer's entire vocabulary of hand gestures, known in the trade as "fan'er" — the height, direction and rotation of the arms and wrists issue start · stop · loud · soft. The traditional lists give gestures such as point · edge · throw · fling · press · draw · withdraw · lead · split · scoop · raise (sources differ slightly on the exact set).

⭐⭐ Rule ⑱: when an instrument must both sound and command, it necessarily grows a technique that makes no sound at all. Those gestures are not playing — they are a conducting language. And traditionally the bangu's technique is *already* divided in two: "audible strokes" (single stick, double stick, rolls, stick on the clappers) and "silent gestures" (the eleven named above).

A manual of strokes and a sign language, growing on the same instrument.

And the pair "guban" left a term in the language: with the ban clappers in the left hand, the "ban" stroke marks the strong beat, while the right-hand stick on the drum produces "yan" and other colours for the secondary and weak beats. ⭐ The word *banyan*, the whole concept of strong-and-weak patterning, is just these two instruments' division of labour written into the language.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Crisp, bright, solid — and gone at once. Not a "boom" but a "tak": a sharp onset with almost no tail. 2. Dead centre and off-centre are two timbres. Striking the very centre: crispest and loudest; striking near the edge of the heart: slightly softer. 3. Three extra colours come from the hands: scraping with the side of a stick gives a sandy hiss; pressing with the left stick and striking gives a muted sound; and pointing the stick (a stab) differs from flat striking it. 4. Different bore sizes are different grades: the small bore (5 cm, high and crackling) for Peking and Ping opera; the middle bore (8 cm) for Yue opera and Shanxi and northern Shaanxi opera; the large bore (10 cm, broad and mellow) for *shifan* drumming, and able to solo a fast drum passage. Another naming gives gan gu (5 cm heart, higher, Peking/Ping opera) and bao gu (10 cm heart, lower, Qin and Jin opera). ⚠️ The rule: a bigger head is lower; a smaller head is higher.

Playing techniques

  • Suspended, then struck. The drum hangs on a stand from cords and touches nothing hard — the same principle as the yunluo: for a clean sound, first remove the contacts.
  • Two sticks, cane and bamboo. It is played with thin sticks of cane or bamboo (also called *drum arrows*), not beaters — it wants a point, not a blow.
  • Three actions: double (both hands together, or alternating rolls) · single (right hand on the drum, left hand on the clappers at the same time) · muted (left stick pressed on the head while the right strikes it).
  • It and the clappers are one person. The ban clappers are three boards of rosewood or boxwood, and the drummer manages both: the left hand holds the clappers and clicks them, the right hand holds the stick. One person is in charge of both the beat and the tempo.
  • It leads in folk ensembles too. In *shifan gu* and *shifan luogu* the bangu and the tonggu (a kind of tanggu) are played by one person, who leads the ensemble and can play whole contrasted drum passages.
  • It has multi-ethnic counterparts: the Naxi huangu is used in Dongjing music, and there it is normally played by the deputy conductor — ⭐ the bangu stepping back into a deputy's seat shows that "who sets the tempo" is an institutional decision, not a musical accident.

One sentence from its history deserves to stand alone: "Its note is tight and urgent; it is the leader of all the instruments, and its striking is exceedingly difficult." Three short clauses covering its sound, its rank and its difficulty — and the writer was compiling an official Qing document.

Common misconceptions

  • "It's a drum, so it must work by volume." The opposite: its sounding area is only 5–10 cm, the smallest in the family. It works by position, not by loudness.
  • "Bigger drums are lower, so the conductor must use the biggest one." The rule is right (a bigger head is lower, a smaller head higher), but the conclusion is backwards: conducting needs short and precise, so it uses the small bore / *gan gu* (higher, shorter). The large bore / *bao gu* is left for *shifan* drumming, where a broad mellow sound is wanted.
  • "The centre is higher, the edge lower." ⚠️ This site does not assert that. What the sources say is that the centre and the edge sound different, and they describe the difference in timbre (dead centre crispest and loudest, the edge of the heart softer). A reliable statement of direction exists only for "head size → pitch". (⚠️ And note: the tanggu sources do say "heart low, edge high" — that is the tanggu's rule and must not be moved onto the bangu.)
  • "Conducting means beating time." The bangu conducts down two channels: drum patterns (heard) and gestures (silent). The tradition itself splits its technique into "audible strokes" and "silent gestures" — if it were only about beating time, eleven named gestures would be pointless.
  • "It is a Han instrument." It has clear roles among the Naxi, Tujia and Dong as well — the Naxi huangu in Dongjing music is played by the deputy conductor. The same instrument occupies different positions in different institutions — a more interesting fact than which ethnicity it "belongs" to.

Next

The Peking-opera percussion section is nearly complete: bangu (gives the orders), daluo (weight), naobo (density) — with only one left: the xiaoluo (small gong).

It is smaller and higher than the daluo, and it uses no beater head at all: a bamboo slip is struck against it edge-on. But its most remarkable feature is not its sound, it is its casting — it accompanies scholars, women and clowns.
