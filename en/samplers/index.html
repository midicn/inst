Samplers

Recording the world as an instrument — pen and thesaurus

Sydney, 1979. A machine priced at twenty-five thousand dollars, a light pen hanging beside the keyboard, a green waveform on the screen that you could redraw by hand.

It was not meant to be a sampler. Peter Vogel and Kim Ryrie set out to build a digital synthesiser; the additive-synthesis software would not come together, so the team tried recording other instruments and playing them back. That worked.

A waveform you can draw on

Sound is drawn as a curve, and wherever the pen lands, that stretch of the sound changes. No bank of knobs, only a screen, a pen and two keyboards.

Pages on a screen

Fairlight split the work into pages: Page 6 drew waveforms, Page 8 took the sample, Page 9 was the keyboard sequencer known as Page R. Most sequencing software since has followed Page R's layout — any timeline in a DAW today traces back to that green screen.

Before MIDI and the mouse, this was the first time someone could see a sound and change it with a hand. The monochrome monitor and its pen became an icon of the decade's music.

Twenty-five thousand, or affordable

E-mu's Emulator arrived in 1981 at a fraction of the price. It too was a rescue: the Audity was to sell for seventy thousand dollars and did not. The name came from flipping through a thesaurus, and it happened to match the company.

The SP-1200 of 1987 took the other road: 12-bit, low resolution, drums with grit in them. Hip-hop producers made that grit their own sound, and a limitation turned into a taste.

The inventor watches it get cheap

By 1985 rival samplers were doing basic sampling for pocket money, and the CMI's processing architecture had never been efficient at it. In 1986 the original Fairlight company went bankrupt.

What it left behind outlived the company. Trevor Horn first used his sampling technique on the second Buggles album, then on Grace Jones's Slave to the Rhythm and on Frankie Goes to Hollywood. One instrument plus one producer's method made the pop sound of the decade.

Press a piano note on a keyboard today: it is probably not a piano, but a recording. The first sentence of that grammar was written by a twenty-five-thousand-dollar machine.
