Snare Drum

The membranophone with snares across its lower head — pitchless, and the heart of military music and the drum kit

A marching band comes through the intersection. The snare hangs at the drummer's left thigh, head tilted up. It plays no melody; its job is to make everyone step off on the same beat.

Wires under the lower head

The shell is a cylinder with a head at each end. Across the lower head lie fine steel wires — the snares — held against the skin or released by a strainer on the side. Strike the top and the bottom head vibrates, dragging the wires across it into a dense high-frequency rattle. Flip the strainer and the wires lift away; the drum turns back into an ordinary wooden-sounding drum, which orchestral scores call snares off.

Why no note comes out

A timpani bowl is closed, so its fundamental stands clear and the ear can hold a pitch. A snare is open at both ends, and the wires smear the energy across a wide high band; what reaches the ear is noise. Calling it no definite pitch is not a matter of taste — the spectrum decides.

Five places on one head

The centre is thick and dull; moving outward the sound tightens. Near the rim it turns crisp. A rimshot lays the shaft across head and hoop at once and gives the loudest crack the drum has; a rim click leaves only a wooden tick on the hoop. One drum, and the player has several notes in his hands. Dynamics run as wide: a brush sweep whispers, a rimshot detonates.

From the march to the kit

In the seventeenth century it was standard issue in European military music, worn at the side and played one stick to a hand. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries brought it into the orchestra for armies, battles and marches, and for rolls that build tension. Jazz put it at the centre of the kit, beside bass drum, toms and cymbals. Orchestra, wind band, jazz, rock and pop still use it, and its place has barely moved.

Next time the sharpest crack in a track goes by, watch whether the player lays the stick into the hoop — that is punctuation, added by hand.
