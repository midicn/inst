Bianqing

A set of suspended stone chimes — partner of the bronze bells, and an instrument whose tuning can only travel one way

The bianqing is the last of the eight bayin categories: stone.

It is the partner of the bianzhong — the ancients called their combined sound "the voice of metal and stone", and the phrase "metal sounding, jade ringing" (from Mencius) later came to name this pairing: the bell begins, the chime closes.

⚠️ It forces the hardest question in this batch: how do you make a pitch in a piece of stone? Metal can be melted, wood carved, skin tightened or loosened — but stone can neither be melted nor added to. The only way to change its pitch is to grind some away. Hence a property no other instrument here has: a bianqing's tuning only ever subtracts — material can be ground away and never added back. (⚠️ The pitch, though, can still move both ways: grind the faces to lower it, the ends to raise it — see the Kao Gong Ji section below.) | | Bianzhong | Bianqing | |---|---|---| | Material | bronze | stone (limestone · marble · jade) | | How pitch is set | casting | grinding | | After a mistake | can be melted and recast | material can only be removed — no return | | Unit of making | formed at once, reproducible | ground piece by piece, not reproducible | One goal — writing the pitch into the material in advance — reached by two entirely different crafts.

Structure: a bent slab, hung on a cord

Three points:

1. A bent plate. The shape is called juju: a slab folded at an obtuse angle with a suspension hole at the top. Shang-period chimes were mostly right-triangles with a curved top; the bent form settled from the Western Zhou onward — it is a product of shape, not of length. 2. Size and thickness set the pitch. A chime is a vibrating plate: a bigger, thinner slab sounds lower; a smaller, thicker one sounds higher (plate frequency is proportional to thickness). A set is therefore a graded row of slabs — structurally the same idea as bells hung in order of size. 3. It is clearer than a bell. The bell is louder with a longer ring, while a chime decays quickly and speaks promptly — so when the two play together the chime is not buried, and instead articulates the rhythm.

Cast or ground: two roads, one goal

This figure is the heart of the page, turning "material decides craft" into a direct comparison.

  • Casting: the melt is poured into a mould, formed at once; a wrong pitch can be fixed by melting and recasting, and copies can be made. (A finished bell is still fine-tuned by grinding its inner wall — but that is adjustment after the form exists.)
  • Grinding: the slab is shaped and then ground into tune; stone can neither be melted nor added, so material can only be removed. But where you remove it decides which way the pitch goes — exactly what the Kao Gong Ji records: "if too high, grind the faces; if too low, grind the ends." Grinding the two faces makes the slab relatively thinner → lower; grinding the ends makes it relatively thicker → higher. Jia Gongyan (Tang dynasty) summed it up as "thick, and the note is clear; thin, and the note is muddy." ⚠️ Material only ever decreases — but pitch can still be tuned in both directions. That is quantitative command of plate vibration, two thousand years early.

And that irreversibility left archaeological evidence: of the 32 slabs of the Zeng Hou Yi bianqing, only 9 survived intact (the rest were crushed and crumbled), while the 65 bells from the same tomb were still hanging on their frame, still remarkably in tune. Two instruments buried together for 2,400 years — stone is more brittle than bronze, so the bayin's material-based scheme produced its own verdict: material decides who gets to come through.

Range

  • Three octaves, all twelve semitones, and key changes — the same class of capability as the bells.
  • But the number of slabs sets everything: the Zeng Hou Yi bianqing has 32 slabs, the bianzhong 65 bells — stone must be ground one piece at a time, so fewer of them were made.
  • C3–C6 is indicative only: a real set's pitches follow the dimensions of its own slabs.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Bright and hollow. Struck stone sounds thinner and emptier than bronze — the old texts call it "clear and ringing", where the bell is "full and loud". 2. Short ring, quick speech. It lacks the bell's long tail: the sound arrives and leaves quickly, which is why it marks rhythm more clearly. 3. It is not masked. The bell is louder and rings longer, but a chime carries — in ensemble each takes a layer: the bell gives thickness, the chime draws the outline. 4. You hear it in slabs. A set is a graded row of stones: higher pitch, smaller slab.

Playing techniques

  • Kneeling to strike. The record for the Zeng Hou Yi set is one player holding a mallet in both hands, kneeling — unlike the bells, which take a team; a set of chimes is far smaller.
  • Strike the longer arm (gu). The longer arm is the main striking area, the shorter one (gu, a different character) the other.
  • Several strokes. Single, double, rolled, scraped and shaken — even though it is a pitched instrument, its technique is not just "one hit".
  • Usually with the bells. A set can play melody alone, but metal and stone sounding together is its formal place in ritual music.
  • Pitch cannot be changed live. As with the bells: it plays only the notes already ground into it.

Metal and stone are the only two "hard material" categories in the bayin — and they share one property: the pitch must be built into the material by craft, before any performance. Their difference is equally plain: one can be redone, the other cannot.

Its fate forms the sharpest contrast in this batch with the bianzhong: one tomb, one era, one ritual system — one set came through almost untouched, the other left nine pieces. The bayin sorts by material; and material, in the end, decides who is still sounding two thousand years later.

Common misconceptions

  • "A chime and a bell are much the same." Different material (stone vs bronze), different craft (grinding vs casting), different consequences (irreversible vs recastable) and different colour (thin and clear vs thick and full).
  • "You cannot make stone sound in tune." The Zeng Hou Yi bianqing covers three octaves, all twelve semitones, with key changes — the same class as the bells. Difficult is not the same as inaccurate.
  • "A chime's pitch can be adjusted while playing." Not at all — it is ground in advance, and like the bells it belongs to the instruments that hand the pitch away.
  • "A chime is just a companion to the bells." It plays melody on its own; and in ensemble it does precisely what the bell cannot: draw the contour clearly.
  • "Bianqing and teqing are the same." The teqing is a single large chime (for sacrifice); the bianqing is a set (for court music) — two classes by venue and arrangement.
  • "Stone in the bayin is just a material label." It is only a material label — but here it decides three things: the craft (grinding only), the colour (thin and hollow), and whether the object survives time.
