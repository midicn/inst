Recording and processing the voice

The history of recording is the emancipation of the voice — four eras, each giving something back

2 min readVoiceUpdated 2026-10-21

Singing into a horn

In the 1920s a singer stood before a large horn and the sound was cut straight to wax. The range ran from 168 Hz to 2,000 Hz, dynamics were flattened, and a loud passage meant stepping back so the stylus would not jump. Being able to sing began with being able to sing into a horn; placement was part of the technique.

The microphone gave subtlety back

In 1925 the Western Electric condenser microphone and electronic amplification pushed the replayable range to 60 Hz–6 kHz. The crooning Crosby pioneered would have been inaudible on an acoustic recording. Distance became the first mix parameter: closer for detail, further back for level, with proximity effect arriving as a side effect.

Tape gave correction back

In 1944 Allied forces captured a Magnetophon at Radio Luxembourg; in 1948 Ampex built the first American professional tape machine, with Crosby an early investor. Les Paul immediately used it to overdub, layer on layer. Tape overload is not a hard clip but soft-knee compression plus harmonics, dense enough to sound like an effect. Multitrack encouraged separation, close mics and little bleed, and the voice moved from one element in a room to the lead in the mix.

Compression, and two counter-intuitive lessons

In the solid-state era compression shifted from damage control to shaping. FET compressors catch peaks, optical ones smooth slowly; tracking takes 2:1 to 4:1 for a few dB, the mix adds 3–10 dB across two units to avoid pumping. A de-esser handles the 5–10 kHz sibilance and belongs after the brightness. Two lessons: cutting 200 Hz hard costs body and usually means the room needs bass traps, so the problem is not the EQ; current pop prefers delay to reverb, and adding reverb to a bad room is painting over a stain. Recording does not just document singing; it invents it.
