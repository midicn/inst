The didgeridoo

A termite-hollowed eucalyptus tube, blown into a continuous low drone by vibrating lips

The didgeridoo is an Aboriginal Australian instrument: a eucalyptus trunk hollowed by termites. The maker wraps beeswax around the mouth end, the player's lips set the tube speaking, and there are no finger holes and no keys.

The main line: termites first, people second

The Yolŋu call it yidaki; "didgeridoo" is an English onomatopoeia first recorded in 1908. Termites hollow the trunk; the maker selects, cuts to length, cleans, tests, and adds a beeswax mouthpiece. Tubes run 1–3 m, often about 1.2 m; every bore differs. It is at least a thousand years old.

Sound and relatives

Vibrating lips give the tube a fundamental; the vocal tract shifts the resonances and brings out certain overtones. Lip tension, tongue stops and overblowing make rhythm and colour on top of the drone; circular breathing stores air in the cheeks and releases it while the nose inhales. With no finger holes it cannot finger a scale; its written range is about A2–G3.

The alphorn is also lip-vibrated and holeless, but it has a bell and a cup mouthpiece and plays melodies from the harmonic series; the ney is edge-blown and fingered. Lips, edge, holes: three routes through one empty tube.

Ritual and the present

In Yolŋu and other communities it appears in ceremony and song cycles with clapsticks and singing; in some communities it is traditionally played by men, a rule that varies by region and family and must not be generalised. It is now heard in workshops, wellness settings and tourism. The argument is who may play on what occasion, who profits, and whether Aboriginal communities are credited and paid.

Common misconceptions

  • "It is a flute." It is a lip-reed tube, classified with natural trumpets, not an edge-blown flute.

Three things to take away

1. Termites first, people second: the bore is made by termites, and every tube has its own voice. 2. Lips and vocal tract, not finger holes: a drone plus overtones and colour is the instrument's "melody". 3. Circular breathing keeps it continuous: but ownership is specific; ask about context and permission before playing.
