The rawap on the previous page divided its strings into "one that works and several that just ring." The tanbur lives in the same land — and gives a different answer.

"Five steel strings, in three groups: the two outermost side by side are the melody strings; the middle string and the two inner ones are accompaniment." Tuning: g · g · d¹ · g · g — two outer strings in unison (one group), two inner in unison, one middle. ⭐⭐ Rule ㊻: one question, two solutions — the rawap's "one melody string ＋ several ringers" versus the tanbur's "melody as a *pair* ＋ three accompanists." The question is the same: several strings on one instrument — how does each get a job? The rawap answers "*one* versus *many*"; the tanbur answers "*grouping*": the melody is not a single string but a unison pair — hence a thicker sound.

Five strings, three groups

⭐ Three points:

1. The melody is a *pair*, not a string. "The two outermost side by side are the melody strings" — two strings in unison playing one line together, thicker than a single string; the middle and inner three (g · g · d¹) accompany. (Compare the rawap: one melody string plus four to six ringers. Same question, different answer.) 2. Its face is wood. "The face is covered with thin pine" (also recorded as paulownia) — ⭐ the plainest difference from the rawap: board versus skin; crescent (or teardrop) sound holes. 3. The plectrum sits in the fingernail. "A pointed steel plectrum wedged in the nail crevice, plucked or strummed, alternating" — its basic technique summed up as "hard and soft plucking with light picking."

One root, a whole family

⭐ A new observation for the naming history: these instruments' names are relatives. Tanbur, dutar, dombra all grew from the same long-necked plucked-lute name system — and "dutar" simply means "two strings": the name carries a spec sheet.

⚠️ One discrepancy worth noting: the Qing *Collected Statutes* record "four iron strings," while today's instrument has five. Instruments change; documents stay where they were.

Range

Its post in the muqam

  • "one of the principal instruments of the classical 《Twelve Muqam》";
  • also of the "meshrep" gatherings;
  • ⭐ and most often in duet: "frequently in duet with the dutar" — one carries the melody, the other accompanies it (exactly where that family tree above lands);
  • its repertoire keeps the muqam's names: *Uzhal*, *Oshshaq*, *Mushawirek*…

⭐ One line for its character: "suited to fervent, sharply rhythmic folk music" — unlike the rawap's "delicate and fine," this is the fast and bright one.

Common misconceptions

  • "The tanbur is much like the rawap." ⚠️ Three hard differences: soundboard (wood versus skin) · string division (melody as a unison pair versus one melody string plus ringers) · timbre (bright and ringing versus delicate).
  • "Many strings means many parts." ⚠️ Only the two outer unison strings carry the melody; the other three accompany. "Five strings" is a configuration, not five voices.
  • "It only accompanies." ⚠️ It is also a soloist (*Ejem*, *Linpat*, *Three Wild Geese*, *Song for Mother*) and a principal muqam instrument.
  • "The name is Chinese." ⚠️ It is "a kind of the Arabic tanbūr"; Chinese spellings run: 弹布尔 / 弹拨尔 / 丹布尔 / 丹不尔 / 弹普.
  • "It was always five-stringed." ⚠️ The Qing statutes record "four iron strings" — the instrument and its documents do not quite match.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Three cues:

1. Hear the thickness. The melody is played on two unison strings — not one line but a pair of lines. 2. Hear the alternating pluck. "Hard and soft plucking with light picking" — its rhythm comes from alternating force, which is why it suits fervent, sharply rhythmic music. 3. Hear the wooden face. A wooden board (not skin) gives a bright, unstuffy sound — in the same land, the rawap is a drum and the tanbur is a board.
