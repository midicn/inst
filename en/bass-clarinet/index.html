Bass Clarinet

A folded low clarinet, written a major ninth above its sounding pitch

Related

IntervalRegisterTimbreArticulation MarksClarinetBassoonE-flat Clarinet

The bass clarinet is the clarinet family's low member, and it does something few low instruments do: it folds its tube once and shifts the whole register structure down unchanged. So it sounds unmistakably like a clarinet — a major ninth lower.

It also carries an extreme figure among woodwinds: written a major ninth above sounding — an octave plus a major second.

⚠️ One conceptual point: both are in B♭, yet the clarinet is written a major second above sounding while the bass clarinet is written a major ninth above (= an octave plus a major second). In other words, the bass clarinet's notation is the clarinet's notation raised a whole octave. That extra octave is deliberate: it keeps the page inside the register a clarinettist already reads, so one fingering chart and one set of muscle memories still work.

Structure: a tube folded once

Three points:

1. Folded once, not the bassoon's U. So its shape is "one long tube plus a turnaround" rather than two parallel bores with a crook on top. The number of folds tells you the tube length. 2. The bell flares upward, unlike a clarinet's downward bell — otherwise the low frequencies would be absorbed by the floor instead of projecting. 3. Very wide key spacing. A long body puts the holes far apart, so it carries a set of extra linked keys so the hand need not actually reach every hole.

Notation: why a major ninth

Nowhere is the purpose of transposing notation clearer:

  • the page the player reads (E♭3–G6) is exactly the clarinet's comfortable register;
  • so one fingering chart and one reading habit transfer unchanged;
  • the price is that the listener hears a major ninth lower — and the reader must never forget it.

That is the standard logic of woodwind transposition: it unifies the fingers, not the sound. The same applies to the piccolo (written an octave low) and the English horn (written a fifth high).

Range

  • Sounding D♭2–F5, covering the whole lower stretch of the clarinet's range.
  • The working register is D♭2–B♭4: it works mainly low, which is why it exists.
  • Above C5 it can play, but the colour turns into "a fatter clarinet" and it is used sparingly.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Dark, thick, faintly grainy, most obviously low down — as if the clarinet's tone were wrapped in something. 2. Still a clarinet. The bore is unchanged, so the relationship between the three registers matches the standard clarinet — shifted down and, being longer, made gentler. 3. Enormous weight at the bottom, the standard way to underpin low woodwind writing. 4. Above C5 it steps forward: no longer background but a solo instrument — the low monologue of jazz lives in this register.

Playing techniques

The fingerings come from the clarinet, but three things must be relearned:

  • Spacing and linked keys. A long body places the holes far apart, hence the extra keys; "my fingers will not reach" is the newcomer's standard complaint.
  • How it is held. It stands on a peg or hangs from a neck strap, the hands only working the keys.
  • Little pressure but much air: a wide, long tube needs a steady supply to hold the low notes.

The pair worth comparing here is the bass clarinet and the bassoon: both work low, but one is cylindrical and the other conical — so their low registers feel entirely different. The bass clarinet is supple and close-grained; the bassoon is woody and grainy. That difference is exactly how orchestras divide the work between them.

Common misconceptions

  • "A bass clarinet is a big clarinet played the same way." Same fingering logic, but spacing, air pressure and how it is held all differ; it needs its own practice.
  • "It and the bassoon are the same sort of low instrument." Different bores: the bass clarinet is cylindrical, the bassoon conical — so one low register is close-grained and the other woody and grainy.
  • "Written a ninth high means it is a high instrument." Notation has nothing to do with register: its sound is low, and the page is high only for the reader's convenience.
  • "It only plays low." Above C5 its colour suddenly steps forward — jazz and contemporary music use that register constantly, and quite differently from the bottom.
  • "General MIDI has one." It does not; a clarinet sample is the only stand-in (as on this page).
