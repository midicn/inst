The bili (ancient Guanzi)

Its name is a transliteration of Kuchean; its aliases are its timbre — a pipe of sorrow from the Silk Road

The bili's name is not Chinese. It transliterates a Kuchean word; every Chinese spelling is a different hearing of the same foreign sound.

Its commonest alias means the sorrowful pipe — not a metaphor but a specification: the name states the timbre.

383: an instrument's registration moves

The earliest mention is He Chengtian's Zuǎnwen: bili is a Qiang-Hu instrument. Duan Anjie is more precise: the bili is Kuqa music, also called the sorrowful pipe. In 383 the general Lü Guang campaigned west and brought back a Kuqa ensemble; the instrument entered the Central Plains with it.

From a horn to a wooden pipe

The Bezeklik cave murals record its childhood: curved, thin below and thick above, so the first bili was horn. It switched to bamboo and straightened in the Central Plains; the Yungang murals already show the straight form. Today it is wood, eight holes, a reed whistle. Three bodies, one unchanging reed.

First of all instruments

In the Tang court's ten divisions it was a main instrument, and it appears throughout the Hu repertory. At the Tang-Song conservatory it became the first of all instruments, often leading the ensemble, hence head pipe. In the Song it was the chief melodic accompaniment, and guanpu notation became a common way to record tunes.

A foreign instrument leading a court ensemble is the highest post the Silk Road ever handed out. The suona came by the same road and ended up at village funerals.

What the poems recorded

Li Qi records its origin: cut bamboo south of the mountains, this music from Kuqa. Li Yi records its use: a reed pipe somewhere, and the whole garrison turns homeward. Bai Juyi records its physics: low notes drop like stones, high notes rise like drifting cloud. Origin, function, the two ends of its compass. He adds: what differs between Hu and Yue? Hearing this, we mourn as one.

The old form survives in the west: the Uyghur balaman is still reed, keeps the Kuqa shape, and plays the prelude of the Twelve Muqam. Modern orchestras add alto, bass and keyed pipes; the double pipe puts two reeds in the mouth and sounds two notes at once.
