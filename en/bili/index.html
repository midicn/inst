The bili (ancient Guanzi)

Its name is a transliteration of Kuchean; its aliases are its timbre — a pipe of sorrow from the Silk Road

⭐⭐ The main line: the name is a transliteration, the aliases are the timbre

The bili has a string of names: bili, bìlì, bēilì, jiāguǎn, lúguǎn, tóuguǎn — and the most important fact hides in the sources: "its name is a transliteration of the Kuchean Pi-Li."

⭐⭐ This instrument's very name is not Chinese — it is a foreign pronunciation written down in Chinese characters (compare the site's name-vs-reality lineage: the saer's mismatch was "different transliterations" — the bili is more radical: the name itself is a transliteration, and every Chinese spelling is a different hearing of the same foreign word). ⭐⭐ And the alias string is even better — "bēilì" (sorrowful pipe) is not a metaphor, it is a spec sheet: the instrument's most common Chinese alias simply is its timbre (⭐ a new extreme of alias-as-description on this site: the name admits its own sound).

⭐⭐ 383 CE: one instrument's household registration moved

The earliest document is the Southern Dynasties' He Chengtian's Zuǎnwen: "Bili is the name of a Qiang-Hu instrument." And the Tang's Duan Anjie in Yuefu Zalu said it precisely: "The bili was originally the music of the state of Kuqa; also called bēilì, akin to the jia."

⭐ In 383, the Former Qin general Lü Guang campaigned in the Western Regions and brought back a Kuqa ensemble — "the bili thus entered the Central Plains." ⭐⭐ The Silk Road was not only where caravans walked; it was a touring route for bands (the instance "Song of the Northern Frontier" — a whole band travelled that road, and the bili was the loudest and saddest of them).

⭐⭐ Form evolution: from a horn to a wooden pipe

The Bezeklik Thousand Buddha Caves' murals record its childhood: "its shape was curved, thin below and thick above — visible proof that the earliest bili was made of sheep or ox horn; after entering the Central Plains it switched to bamboo, the body straightening from bent" (straight forms appear in the Northern Wei Yungang murals).

⭐ Horn → bamboo → wood — it changed bodies three times, but the mouthpiece stayed reed (today's form: "a mostly wooden body with eight holes, a reed whistle inserted at the top"). ⭐⭐ A horn borrowed from an animal's body finally became an eight-hole wooden pipe — the materials were only the shell; the reed decided it would always be a reed pipe (compare sheng-harmony's free reeds — two reed instruments from the Silk Road: one went to the conservatory to lead, one became a harmony engine).

⭐⭐ Tóuguǎn: first of all instruments in the conservatory

A main instrument in the Tang court's ten musical divisions; in the Sui-Tang court's Hu music (Kucha music foremost, with bili in Tianzhu, Shule, Anguo and Gaochang music) — by the Tang-Song conservatory it became "the first of all instruments": often leading the ensemble, hence the name tóuguǎn (head pipe).

⭐⭐ A foreign instrument promoted to lead the court ensemble — the Silk Road's highest appointment (compare the suona: from the same road, but it went to village funerals; the bili went to the conservatory to lead — one Silk Road, two destinies). ⭐ In the Song it was the chief melodic accompaniment, and guanpu notation (marking pitches and fingerings) became an important way for musicians to record tunes — ⭐⭐ compare guqin-notation's tablature: two notation systems for the same purpose — recording an instrument in its own terms (a score is a letter an instrument writes to its own users).

⭐⭐ Three poetic perfections: a literary history of one instrument

「South of the mountains, cut the bamboo for a bili; this music first came from Kuqa.」 — Li Qi, Song on Hearing An Wanshan Play the Bili 「Where is that reed pipe blowing? All night the garrison turns homeward.」 — Li Yi 「Low notes drop like stones, heavy; high notes rise like drifting cloud.」「What differs between Hu and Yue? Hearing this, we mourn as one.」 — Bai Juyi

⭐⭐ One instrument's timbre certified by poets of three eras in three ways: Li Qi records its origin (Kuqa), Li Yi records its function (a whole camp turns homeward in one night — the switch of homesickness), Bai Juyi records its physics (low heavy as falling stone, high as drifting cloud — two textures at two ends of its compass). ⭐⭐ And Bai Juyi's last line is the footnote of all music history: "What differs between Hu and Yue? Hearing this, we mourn as one" — the barbarian's instrument and the southerner's ear grieve in the same grief.

⭐⭐ Survivors: the balaman and the double pipe

Xinjiang's Uyghur balaman (Uyghur "pipi" = reed) "still preserves the ancient Kuqa form of the bili, made of reed" — in the Twelve Muqam it carries the unmeasured prelude of the Qiongrakman (grand suite). ⭐⭐ The Central Plains rebuilt it as a wooden pipe for the conservatory; the Western Regions kept it in a reed whistle — one instrument, two conservatisms on either bank.

⭐ Modern Chinese orchestras have alto, bass and keyed pipes (range two octaves plus a sixth; keyed models play all twelve semitones); the double pipe binds two pipes side by side, two reeds in the mouth, both holes fingered at once to sound dyads. ⭐⭐ It spread east to Korea and Japan; Japan's Shōsō-in keeps no specimen — only a record in the乐具欠失物注文 of its loss — in another country's archive, the instrument survives only as a "missing item record."
