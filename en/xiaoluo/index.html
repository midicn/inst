Xiaoluo (small gong)

The smallest gong in the percussion section — and its name is one character

A scholar walks on and the gong speaks first: tai — ling — tai, unhurried. Send on a general and the stage hears cang cang cai cang, blunt and heavy. The character is identified before he speaks.

A gong pinched in the hand

Twenty centimetres across, a size below the great gong and configured differently: the daluo hangs from a cord, the xiaoluo has no holes and is pinched between thumb and forefinger while the right hand strikes with a bamboo slip.

A hung gong swings and takes a full stroke; a pinched one stops dead: weight against control.

The narrow contact line gives a bright, quick-dying sound; the boss comes in high, middle and low versions. It has a sense of height but lands on no nameable note.

Percussion written in characters

Peking-opera percussion uses neither staves nor numbers but characters: each action of each instrument has one, and saying it aloud produces the sound the stage hears.

The small gong has three — tai plain, ling light, za muted. The great gong has cang and kuang, cai means cymbals with the small gong, and yi marks a rest. Players chant the syllables before touching a stick.

The characters change with the genre: cang in Peking opera, pang in Cantonese. Transmitted by mouth, the words travel with the dialect.

It handles one kind of person

Of the four basic patterns, three contain tai or ling.

Scholars enter on tai ling tai, relaxed; generals on cang cang cai cang. Same action, tai for the civil, cang for the martial. Clowns get a light stroke per gesture.

It is the quietest member, and the pinching hand can stop the ring at any moment.

From a signal gong to a voice for roles

In Tang times a gong carried orders across a camp; in Peking opera the gongs divide by character — the great for generals, the small for scholars and comedy.
