The xiaoluo is the smallest instrument in this family — and the one with the strangest "name". Its name is a single Chinese character:

台 (*tai*)

— not the two characters for "small gong", but one standalone character that sounds like the instrument's stroke. In explaining itself, it also explains the notation of all Chinese opera percussion:

⭐⭐ Peking-opera percussion is written neither in staff notation nor in numbered notation — it is written in Chinese characters.

⚠️ It forces a question this site has not met before: a system that notates instruments with characters. We mentioned the bangu's "luogu jing" when describing how the drummer gives orders — but what that notation *is* belongs on this page: It is a table of verbal syllables. Every action of every instrument has a character. Structure: a gong pinched in the hand

Three points:

1. It is a size smaller than the daluo — and configured differently. The daluo has two holes in its rim and a cord by which it is carried; the xiaoluo has no holes at all and is pinched between the left thumb and forefinger. ⭐ The difference in how they are held *is* the difference in their jobs: a gong that hangs on a cord can swing freely (the daluo needs a full stroke); a gong pinched in the fingers can be stopped at once (the xiaoluo has to mute). 2. No beater — a bamboo slip. About 17 cm long, struck with its lower end edge-on against the face or the rim. The "edge-on" is the point: it delivers not a round impact but a narrow line of contact. 3. A slightly raised centre: a bossed gong. About 20 cm across, made in high, middle and low versions. It has a sense of pitch but no definite pitch — the same as the daluo, and unlike the yunluo.

Luogu jing: notating percussion with Chinese characters

This figure is the heart of the page.

Luogu jing is the notation of Chinese opera percussion, and it works nothing like Western notation:

  • It notates the sound itself, not a pitch. You cannot "explain" what 仓 means — but when a player says 仓, he is already producing the sound of one great-gong stroke.
  • It is an onomatopoeic syllable script. Traditionally a player memorises the syllables before learning to play — this is not a score to read from, it is a score to chant.
  • ⭐⭐ One instrument may have several characters. The small gong has three: 台 (plain stroke) · 令 (light) · 匝 (muted) — one instrument, three characters, three actions.

⭐ Which completes this site's line on Chinese notation:

One notates what is sung, the other how it is struck — and neither uses a staff; both use characters, and both are learned by saying them aloud.

⚠️ One further detail: the characters differ by genre. The same great gong is 仓 in Peking opera and 旁 in Cantonese opera; the small gong is 台 in Peking opera and 昌 or 叮 in Cantonese. It is dialect-bound, because it was always transmitted by mouth.

It only handles one kind of person

Peking opera has over sixty percussion patterns, all developed from four basic ones:

chongtou (仓七) · changchui (仓七台七) · shanchui (仓台才台) · matuier (仓另七 乙台)

⭐ Three of the four contain the small gong's 台 or 令.

But the xiaoluo's real peculiarity is its casting:

Who entersThe patternWhat it sounds like
Scholars · gentlewomen · civil officials⭐ small-gong entrance, 台令台gentle and unhurried — a leisurely walk on
Generals · grand characters⭐ great-gong entrance, 仓仓才仓blunt and forceful — building an aura of authority

⭐ The same action — an entrance — is made with 台 for a civil character and 仓 for a martial one.

Peking opera says it plainly: the small gong is used for scholars, women and comic characters (such as clowns) and for small stage movements, while the daluo serves generals and dignitaries.

⭐⭐ Rule ⑲: inside one percussion section, instruments divide up by *character type* — the instrument's colour is the character's colour.

(Which completes the other side of "volume is social radius": so far we have seen instruments matched to occasions (indoors, outdoors, signalling). This one is matched to people — whoever walks on stage, that is whose sound you hear.)

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Clear, bright, light. It is the quietest instrument in the percussion section — and precisely for that reason it is the one that suits an unhurried, gentle scene. 2. It gives a point, not a blow. The daluo gives a mass of sound, the cymbals a sheet of it; the xiaoluo gives one clean point — and with 令 and 匝 it can give a light point and a muted point as well. 3. Often it is not for you at all — it is for the character. A single stroke under a spoken line, a light stroke as a gesture lands: it is the punctuation of the stage. 4. It can combine with the cymbals into one stroke. The character 才 means cymbals and small gong together — two instruments sharing a single character.

Playing techniques

  • Pinched, not held. The left thumb and forefinger pinch the rim while the right hand wields the stick — the pinch is itself a control: press and the sound stops.
  • A bamboo slip, not a beater. About 17 cm long, struck edge-on with its lower end against the face or rim.
  • Six strokes: heavy · light · muted · covered · a thrusting stroke · an edge stroke — and the first three are already three separate characters in the notation: 台 · 令 · 匝.
  • It talks. In Peking opera it punctuates clowns and small movements — it does not beat time, it sets tone.
  • It works for dancing too. Because it has no holes, is pinched in the hand and needs no beater, it leaves a hand free in dance-and-play settings such as flower-drum folk dances — something the cord-hung daluo cannot do.

Four gongs, four sizes, four ways of holding, four jobs — and what decides the job is often not volume but the way it is held: the cord-hung one can swing, the pinched one can stop, the framed one leaves both hands free.

History

todaythe most-used gong in opera companies and folk ensembles — because it is small, light, and one hand can hold it

Its history is really the history of a division of labour: in Tang times the gong was still a single signalling device (see the daluo); by the time it reaches Peking opera it has been divided up by character type. From an order on a map to a set of voices worn by roles.

Common misconceptions

  • "A xiaoluo is just a smaller daluo." It is not a matter of scale but of configuration: the daluo has two holes and a cord and is carried; the xiaoluo has no holes at all and is pinched. Different hold → different feel → one can swing free, the other can stop dead, and therefore completely different jobs.
  • "Luogu jing is an old percussion score, much like gongche notation." Both use characters, but they notate different things: gongche notates pitch, luogu jing notates rhythm and stroke — and luogu jing is an onomatopoeic script whose characters *are* the sound; Peking and Cantonese opera even assign different characters to the same instrument (it is dialect-bound).
  • "Percussion just beats time." Those sixty-odd patterns each have a name, a structure, a technique and a use: lengchui is one sudden great-gong stroke followed by silence (a character starting, freezing); zhutou closes a section of singing or action; and the small-gong entrance and great-gong entrance are two different ceremonies for two kinds of character. They are stage language, not metronomes.
  • "The small gong has no pitch, so it is just noise." It has a sense of pitch (it is made in high, middle and low versions) — it is simply not fixed to a nameable note. What earns it its place beside scholars and women is exactly that clear colour, not the absence of pitch.
  • "The percussion section is the accompaniment." The trade has a saying: "the drum and clappers are harder than the singing." The drummer must anticipate the character's feelings and lead the actors into the scene with the percussion; before the actor moves, the percussion has already gathered the energy. One zhutou and a whole section is over — whoever holds the rhythm holds the scene.
