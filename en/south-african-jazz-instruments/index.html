South African jazz instruments

A set of instruments you can carry in the street — another kind of jazz, grown under apartheid

Related

BanjoMarimbaTrumpetAccordionThe mbira (thumb piano)A cappella singing

⭐⭐ The main line: a set of instruments you can carry in the street

「Cape jazz in the beginning featured (though not exclusively) instruments that can be carried in a street parade, such as brass instruments, banjos, guitars and percussion instruments.」

The selection criterion for early South African jazz was not compass and not headcount — it was whether the instrument could be carried in a parade: brass, banjo, guitar, percussion, a band that walked from one end of town to the other.

⭐⭐ The site's most practical instrument filter: ask whether it can go out on the street, and only then ask what it can say (against the site's portability lineage: the talk box's plastic tube · the groovebox's suitcase · the pocket-sized mbira · a whole South African band).

⭐⭐ None of the four is an 「African native instrument」, yet together they make the skeleton of African jazz —— ⭐⭐ the instruments can be swapped; the grammar cannot (against the mbira's use of pitch as a medium · the balafon's scales as a grammar —— the technique travels, the grammar does not).

⭐⭐ The banjo: home again after a lap of the earth

⭐⭐ The banjo is the site's clearest case of an instrument flowing back across the Atlantic: it grew out of the African lute family, was remade by Black Americans in the American South into the five-string banjo, and returned to Africa with the South African jazz craze of the 1930s–40s.

⭐⭐ A complete journey: West Africa → the American South → worldwide → back to Africa (against the site's migration lineage: the kora's versions travel with their people · the balafon's playing style transplanted onto a guitar · the banjo comes home after going round the globe).

⭐⭐ Born at the same time as New Orleans —— 「Being born in a similar political climate to that in New Orleans, Louisiana, at the end of the 19th century」:

⭐⭐ Two 「New Orleans」: one in the Mississippi delta, one at the Cape; the same century, two kinds of oppression, each growing its own jazz. ⭐⭐ South African music under apartheid did not become a copy of American jazz —— it became something else.

⭐⭐ Three local components

⭐ ghoema / guma —— ⭐⭐ a barrel-shaped Asian-style drum that the mixed-race carnival crowds of Cape Town turned into Goema jazz: ⭐⭐ another case of a foreign form taking root (against the Kucha instruments of bili · the independent invention of balafon and marimba · an Asian barrel drum that grew into a pulse in Cape Town).

⭐ kwela and the whistle —— 「Pennywhistle jive, or kwela, emerged from the marabi style and used a pennywhistle, a simple instrument often played by street performers」: ⭐⭐ a plastic whistle replaced the saxophone as the melodic voice —— ⭐⭐ a street player took the jazz lead with the lowest possible cost (against the site's 「cheapest instrument wins」 line: harmonica · recorder · whistle).

⭐ The marabi formula —— marabi was born in the illegal bars (shebeens) of the 1890s–1920s, ragtime piano and blues harmony over accordion, banjo and improvised percussion; ⭐⭐ its harmonic vocabulary was the eight-bar I–IV–I6-4–V cycle —— ⭐⭐ a harmonic formula passed down by ear alone: the oral tradition of harmony.

⭐⭐ A shadow outliving its instrument: the umgqokolo, a native string instrument —— ⭐⭐ the walking bass lines of South African jazz imitate its ostinato. Electric bass technique imitating an African native plucked string.

⭐ isicathamiya —— Zulu a cappella singing (against the a cappella entry's 「a cappella did not begin in the church, it began in the valleys」 —— ⭐⭐ two ends of one line: the South African highland song and the Western church polyphony); ⭐⭐ Solomon Linda's 1939 「Mbube」 — probably the first Black record to sell over a hundred thousand copies.

⭐⭐ Music and resistance: a protest song named after a place

⭐⭐ After the Sharpeville massacre of 1960 came mass arrests and exile, and musicians built their careers abroad while carrying the news of apartheid with them — Hugh Masekela · Dollar Brand (later Abdullah Ibrahim) · Letta Mbulu · Jonas Gwangwa · Caiphas Semenya · Miriam Makeba.

⭐⭐ Dollar Brand's 1975 「Mannenberg」 — named after a crime-ridden mixed-race township on the Cape Flats — became the unofficial anthem of the anti-apartheid movement. (against the site's 「music and resistance」 lineage: the talking drum banned · the atumpan needed · a protest song named after a place.)

⭐ Ladysmith Black Mambazo 1974 「Amabutho」 —— ⭐⭐ the first gold record made by Black musicians.

⭐ After apartheid: kwela → mbaqanga → kwaito; the driving trio of 1970s Cape jazz: Abdullah Ibrahim (piano) · Basil Coetzee and Robbie Jansen (saxophones) · Paul Michaels (bass) · Monty Weber (drums) —— ⭐⭐ 「Mannenberg」 is that recording.

⭐ Two ways of being alive today: the Goema Captains (Mac McKenzie · Alex van Heerden · Hilton Schilder —— ⭐⭐ a contemporary generation re-arranging South African folk song as jazz) and the Cape Jazz Band (all-star); ⭐⭐ the 「Klopse」 carnival troupes — painted faces, bright costumes, ⭐⭐ borrowing New Orleans's 「Coon Carnival」 mask tradition while growing a completely different music (against the site's 「borrowed forms」 lineage).
