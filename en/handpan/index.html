Handpan

Two steel hemispheres pressed together — each dent in the surface is a note

2 min readPercussionUpdated 2026-09-26

instrumentpercussionworld

Two hemispheres, a ring of dents

Around 2000 the Swiss workshop PANArt pressed two steel hemispheres together and hammered a ring of dents into the upper surface. That was the handpan. Each dent is a note; the large central one, the ding, is usually the tonic and the lowest note.

Tuning is a matter of shape. The bar family carves a bar to a frequency; the handpan hammers a dent into a steel shell, and the dent's local stiffness and mass fix the frequency. Both shape material into something that vibrates at a chosen pitch, one on a bar and one on a shell. A shell vibrates in two dimensions, so the sound is more complex and rounder.

One instrument, one scale

There is no fixed chromatic layout. Each instrument is built to one chosen scale, and the direction of the ring, the intervals between fields and the pitch of the ding together define it. Buying a handpan means choosing a scale, not a range.

Changing scale means changing instrument, the same trade as swapping tubes on a pan flute or crooks on a natural trumpet: flexibility exchanged for purity of tone.

Range

A common minor-scale instrument runs about D3 to D5, one and a half to two octaves, eight to ten notes. The narrowness is not a fault. It was built for improvisation and solo playing, every note belongs to one scale, and nothing you strike comes out wrong.

Fingers, tail and thickness

It is struck with fingertips, finger pads or the heel of the palm, never mallets. Different parts of the hand give different colours, and a palm laid on a field is the only way to damp it. The opening underneath is a Helmholtz resonator; it sets the low-end response and gives the tail its thickness.

The volume is small, which suits rooms, streets and small gatherings. What spread it was not composers but players' videos — an instrument travelling that way had no precedent.
