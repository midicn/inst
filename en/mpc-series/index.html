The MPC series

Sample playback, velocity-sensitive pads and sequencing for finger-played beat production

2 min readElectronicUpdated 2026-11-02

Sixteen pads, played with the palm

In 1988 Akai hired Roger Linn to design a new machine. He laid out sixteen rubber pads in a four-by-four grid, each velocity-sensitive. Drums were no longer struck; they were slapped.

Before this, making a beat was programming, pressing squares in a grid. After it, making a beat was hitting. The phrase finger drumming was coined for the machine.

The father of sampled drums, plus EMS hardware

Linn had built the LinnDrum; the hardware went to David Cockerell, co-founder of EMS. The MPC60 is a co-authorship between the father of sampled drums and an elder of British synthesis. The name started as MIDI Production Center and became Music Production Center.

It was an all-in-one machine: no extra hardware, no computer. The sequencer handled 60,000 MIDI events, 99 tracks per sequence, 99 patterns, 99 sequences. A workstation housed a studio; this housed a beat-production line.

12-bit grit, and a little sway

Sampling was 12-bit at 40kHz for up to thirteen seconds, giving a grit and punch that later 16-bit machines smoothed away. Through the late eighties it split hip-hop production with the E-mu SP-1200: one rough, one hard.

The sequencer's timing was tight and its swing quantisation became famous as MPC swing. The same inventor had tried the formula on the LinnDrum; here he turned it into a brand.

From pads to J Dilla's fingers

The 1994 MPC3000 was Linn's last design, 16-bit, 44.1kHz stereo, 32 voices. Then came the 2000, 2000XL, 4000, 1000, 500 and 2500; the 2012 Renaissance turned back to retro pads with dedicated software, and the 2017 Live and X put a touchscreen back into a standalone box.

The user list is a hip-hop hall of fame: DJ Premier, Pete Rock and Marley Marl on the 60, Kanye West and MF DOOM on the 2000XL, Dr. Dre on the 3000. J Dilla worked on the 60 before moving to the 3000 and deliberately refused to quantise, letting the beat breathe. No knob makes that sway; only hands do.
