The kora

An instrument that obeys no single classification, keeping a history that was never written down

1 min read323 characters条目World InstrumentsUpdated 2026-10-21

Twenty-one strings in two rows: eleven under the left thumb for the bass, ten under the right for the melody. Both thumbs work at once, and one instrument covers a band.

One instrument, two lines

The left hand plays kumbengo, a cycling bass and fixed accompaniment, steady as a pendulum. The right plays birimintingo, improvised runs over that cycle. The two lines move together without waiting for each other. Add satanango, the sung praise, and one player is a band.

That division explains why a single kora sounds like several instruments: a drum underneath, a melody on top, and something turning in the middle.

Taxonomy gives up

The strings run in two ranks, the mark of a double harp. They also leave the neck and cross a bridge carried directly by the resonator, the mark of a lute. Having both, the system mints a name: double-bridged harp-lute.

The body is half a calabash skinned with cowhide, drum-like; the neck is hardwood. The twenty-one strings were hide strips and are now nylon or harp string. Leather tuning rings, konso, slide along the neck and move the whole instrument into another seven-note scale. Modulation is a translation of the entire instrument, not a string-by-string adjustment.

The score is a person

Kora players are jali, and the status is inherited: you are born into it. They keep genealogies and the histories of villages and kingdoms, including the Sunjata epic; they sing patrons and ancestors at naming ceremonies and weddings; they mediate between families.

So the kora has no score. The guqin writes its music on paper; the kora maintains it in a person — one stores text, the other stores blood. The instrument is the index, and the edition lives in the player. Most West African musicians prefer jali to the French loanword griot: one is an oral historian, the other a label from a travel book.

A resonator plate and two exits

A leaf-shaped plate of tin or brass, the nyenmyemo, is clamped to the bridge or the neck, with wire loops along its edge. It rings along with the strings and lays a bed of overtones underneath. The sources describe it as an amplifier, because the sound had to carry outdoors. Modern instruments use a pickup, and the plate has retired.

The instrument grew out of the Mali Empire in the thirteenth century; legend credits Sundiata's jali Balla Fasseke with enlarging a hunter's bow harp to twenty-one strings, and Mansa Musa's 1324 pilgrimage carried it outward. Two exits today: outward, through Toumani Diabate's 72-string symmetrical kora, Mory Kante's Yeke Yeke and the plucked line in Graceland; inward, through Sona Jobarteh, the first woman from a jali family to become a kora virtuoso.
