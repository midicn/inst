Muyu

A hollowed block of wood with a single slit — no skin, no tube, only the wood itself sounding

Related

The muyu is the purest thing in the wood category: no skin, no pipe, no string, no reed — only a hollowed block of wood and a single opening.

This is the cleanest demonstration of the "what vibrates" test: from the outside (both struck, both unpitched) they are indistinguishable; you have to ask who the vibrator is.

Structure: one block of wood, one slit

Three points:

1. The vibrator is the wood. This is the mirror image of the tanggu: a drum needs a skin stretched on to make any sound, a muyu needs nothing added — its material is its voice. 2. The slit is its "mouth". It lets the body flex and vibrate freely and is where the sound comes out; the name comes from this fish-mouth opening (early muyu were carved in the shape of a fish). 3. It is of a family with the woodblock and claves: hard wood, no pitch, pure striking — except the muyu takes "hollow it out" and "cut a slit" as far as they go.

How many dimensions can an instrument control?

This table counts out a hidden thread of the batch: "which dimensions an instrument can control" is a list you can write down — pitch · timing · dynamics · timbre levels.

  • Guqin, pipa, dizi: all four in the player's hands.
  • Bianzhong: its pitch was selected in advance at the foundry (not made in the moment), and it has almost no timbre levels.
  • Tanggu: no pitch, but timing, dynamics and timbre (centre / rim / damped) are all there — which is why it can conduct a band.
  • Muyu: no pitch and very few timbre levels — what it has left is "when" and "how loud".

And that is enough. Organising time is the floor of being an instrument — the muyu stands on that floor, and one step lower there is no instrument at all.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Short, dry, tail-less. One stroke is one stroke — exactly what a time-keeping instrument needs: it does not occupy time, it marks it. 2. The hard-wood "tok". The same family as claves and the woodblock: hard material → fast onset, fast decay → clear edges. 3. Size changes the colour. Small ones are crisp, large ones fuller — ensembles keep several. 4. Position makes a small difference — the slit edge versus the side — but far less than the tanggu's centre/rim/damped.

Playing techniques

  • A single beater. In most situations that is the whole of it — one instrument, one beater, one motion.
  • It marks points, not lines. No rolls, no crescendos: its job is to give clear instants of time.
  • The metronome of chanting. In temples it keeps the recitation together — a group reciting the same text relies on that repeating "tok".
  • A rhythmic skeleton in ensembles. In the Chinese orchestra and in opera it works with the tanggu, gongs and cymbals to mark the metrical structure.
  • Nothing to tune. Pitch is irrelevant — the least troublesome of all instruments.

This struck family forks on only three questions: ① what vibrates (skin / wood / metal) · ② is the pitch definite · ③ how many levels can it give. The muyu answers the simplest way to all three — and remains an instrument.

History

Its history is an unusual road from ritual implement to instrument : it did not grow out of folk or court music but first carried the function of "organising time" inside a religious rite, which music then took over unchanged — function came before aesthetics.

*(Incidentally, the six instruments written so far come from very different places — guqin from the literati · bianzhong from court ritual · xun from ceremonial music · dizi and tanggu from opera and folk life · muyu from the monastery. "Who used this instrument" is itself another map for understanding Chinese instruments.)*

Common misconceptions

  • "A muyu is just a small drum." No. A drum sounds from a stretched skin (membranophone); a muyu from the wood itself (idiophone). Both are struck and unpitched, but the vibrator is entirely different.
  • "It is only for chanting." It is already a standard percussion instrument of the Chinese orchestra and opera bands — and its temple job was musical in the first place (keeping time).
  • "An instrument without pitch is simple and not worth close reading." The opposite: it forces the question "what counts as an instrument" — and the answer is "can it organise time".
  • "A muyu has a definite pitch." No definite pitch: size and striking point change the sense of pitch but produce no fixed degree.
  • "It is the same as a Western woodblock." Same family, different construction: the muyu is hollowed with a long slit, the woodblock is a solid bar — which is why the muyu sounds hollower and more resonant.
  • "Wood in the bayin is only a material label." It is only a material label — but here the material is everything: a muyu's sound comes entirely from that block, with nothing added.

Next

The wood category is complete (the muyu). Only two bayin categories remain: gourd · stone — and the next instrument turns to gourd: the sheng. It is both old and pivotal: China's earliest free-reed instrument, and one hole can sound several notes at once. Its question is: why can a wind instrument sound more than one note at a time?
