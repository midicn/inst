Seven entries into the percussion family, the beater finally goes away.

The previous six — the bianzhong, the bianqing, the tanggu, the muyu, the yunluo and the daluo — all rely on holding something in the hand and swinging it. The naobo does not: it is two pieces, and they strike each other.

⚠️ It forces the most fundamental question in this site's percussion group: once the beater — the middleman — is taken away, what is left for the hands to control? | | With a beater | Naobo (without one) | |---|---|---| | The contact | an instant of impact | ⭐ the contact can last | | What shapes the timbre | mainly the beater (hard / soft / heavy / light) | ⭐ mainly the hands themselves (force · angle · speed) | | What can be controlled | choose the tool + control the force | ⭐ the tool-choosing step is gone; only the hands remain | So it gains a whole extra family of actions: flat clash · edge clash · muted clash — and even scraping one across the other, and shimmering (a rapid series of tiny clashes). A beater can only strike; hands can close, slide, press and shiver.

Structure: a pair of bronze discs

Three points:

1. The dividing line is the width of that centre piece. The nao has a thin, upturned face with a small, flat-topped boss — about one fifth of the full diameter; the bo has a thick, flat face with a large, round bowl — about one half. Players put it bluntly: "the big bowl is a *bo*, the small cap is a *nao*." 2. A nao and a bo of the same size: the nao sounds lower and rings longer. ⚠️ Another source describes it in terms of colour instead — "the nao's small boss gives a *louder* sound, the bo's large bowl gives a *rounder* one". Both descriptions are recorded here as they stand. 3. It is forged, like a gong. Melting and mixing → hammering to shape → cold working and trimming the rim → drilling the apex of the bowl for the cloth loop. And its final timbre depends on alloy, area, thickness, rim curvature and boss size — two of those five are hand-tuned.

Where it sits in Hornbostel–Sachs: the first "concussion"

This figure is the heart of the page.

The first cut in Hornbostel–Sachs is not "metal or stone" (that is the bayin's question) and not "pitched or not". It is: is the sounding body struck by a non-sounding object (percussion), or do two sounding bodies strike each other (concussion)?

  • 111.1 Concussion: until this entry, this site had exactly one instrument here — the naobo.
  • 111.2 Percussion: all six earlier entries sit here — the daluo (111.241.1, individual gong), the yunluo (111.241.2, set of gongs), the bianzhong (111.242.221, struck hanging bell) and the bianqing (111.222, set of percussion plaques).

⭐ So this page does not merely add an instrument — it completes half of a map.

(A note in passing: the tanggu is not on this map, because it is a membranophone — category 2, since what vibrates is skin, not bronze or stone. "Percussion" is our everyday word; Hornbostel–Sachs has no such class — it only asks what vibrates.)

Nao and bo: two fates for one character

This page also carries a story no other instrument here has: the character 铙 (nao) has been used twice, for two completely different instruments.

⭐⭐ And the best part: a Song-dynasty writer put it in writing himself.

Ma Duanlin, *Comprehensive Examination of Documents*:

"The bronze nao is a *fuyou* vessel used by Buddhists — small, with a clear sound, which the world calls a nao. Its name is the same as the nao of the four metals, but the thing itself is different."

"Its name is the same… but the thing itself is different." Nine hundred years ago someone wrote down exactly the confusion we would otherwise have made ourselves.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. A very short attack and a very long tail. One clash gives a steep onset followed by a long metallic tail — it is naturally a sound of blend, not of points. 2. Bright, with a "watery" ring. The traditional description is *clear, with a deep watery ring*: a high sandy scrape and a low hum arrive together, so it sounds bigger than it is. 3. However you close them is how they sound. Flat clash → one complete impact; edge clash → more high frequency, more sand; muted clash → the two discs never quite meet, and the sound stops at once. 4. It partners the daluo in the percussion section. The daluo supplies weight, the naobo supplies density — the wash of sound underneath a drum-and-gong pattern is usually the naobo's.

Playing techniques

  • One disc per hand, held by the cloth. A cloth loop runs through a hole at the apex of each bowl; each hand holds one disc — the cloth is both a grip and a filter for excess high frequency.
  • Four basic actions: flat clash · edge clash · muted clash, plus shimmer (a rapid series of tiny clashes) and scrape (sliding one across the other). ⭐ In all of these, how long the contact lasts is adjustable — which a beater cannot do: a beater strikes and leaves, whereas hands decide when the two discs come apart.
  • It is used as a graded set. Peking opera has several nao — the danaos (about half a metre across, loud and strong) and the xiaojing ("cymbal nest" in the north); and several bo — the shuibo (also called pubo, gongbo, shuicha or "lotus leaf") and the chuidabo ("muted bo"). One instrument, graded into a whole set of positions from bright to thick to dry.
  • It is paired with the daluo: one gives weight, the other density.
  • It is also a Buddhist ritual instrument and an indispensable part of lion and dragon dances. In southern Fujian a whole acrobatic act — "tossing the naobo" — grew out of it, and is now on the provincial intangible-cultural-heritage list. An instrument that became a circus act.

Its history has a shape no other entry here has: first a character (the Shang nao), a thousand years later a foreign instrument (the bo), and then the foreign instrument borrowed the old character. So reading its history means reading two timelines at once — one of a *character*, one of *bronze discs*.

Common misconceptions

  • "Nao and bo are two names for the same thing." They are not: the nao has a small flat-topped boss (about 1/5 of the full width) on a thin, upturned face; the bo has a large round bowl (about 1/2) on a thick, flat face. At the same size the nao sounds lower and rings longer; the bo higher and shorter. "Naobo" is an umbrella term, and the two differ in colour and in use.
  • "The naobo came to China from Western cymbals." Only half right: the clashed bo is indeed foreign (arriving with Buddhism around the 4th century), but the character 铙 is over a thousand years older in China — there are Shang-dynasty bronze nao in the ground. An old name was lent to a new instrument; the new instrument did not bring a new name — and a Song-dynasty writer recorded exactly that.
  • "No pitch, so it sounds the same however you strike it." The opposite: the timbre of the attack is almost entirely decided by how the two discs close — flat, edge, muted, shimmered or scraped are completely different sounds. On a beater instrument that variable lives in the beater; here it lives entirely in the hands.
  • "Two discs = a louder triangle." In classification they are far apart: the naobo is two sounding bodies striking each other (HS 111.1, concussion), while the triangle is struck by a non-sounding object (HS 111.2, percussion) — the first cut falls exactly there.
  • "The bayin call it metal, so it belongs with the gongs." As with the earlier entries: the bayin records the material. In Hornbostel–Sachs it shares the struck idiophone class with the daluo but sits on a different branch — concussion, not percussion.

Next

Percussion now has seven entries, and both the gongs and the cymbals of the opera percussion section are in place. But the section is still missing one instrument — and it is the most important one: the bangu (Peking-opera drum).

It is not another loud instrument; it is the instrument that gives orders. The drummer, with two thin sticks and a vocabulary of gestures, directs everyone on stage and off.

And it asks a turning-point question: what makes a percussion instrument qualify to conduct the whole band? :::
