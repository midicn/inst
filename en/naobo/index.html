Naobo (Chinese cymbals)

Two bronze discs striking each other — the beater drops out of the picture

1 min read67 characters条目Chinese PercussionUpdated 2026-09-26

Two discs striking each other

Seven percussion entries in, the beater drops out. Six earlier entries depend on holding something and swinging it. The naobo does not: two pieces striking each other. One disc is a nao, the other a bo, and the difference is the boss: a small flat cap about a fifth of the width, or a large round bowl about half.

It is forged like a gong: melt, hammer to shape, cold-work the rim, drill the cap, loop a cloth through. Alloy, area, thickness, rim curvature and boss size decide the sound.

The first cut: concussion

The first question is not metal or stone, nor pitched or unpitched, but whether a sounding body is struck by something silent, or two sounding bodies strike each other. The naobo sits at 111.142, concussion; the six earlier entries under percussion. Bayin records material; the HS class records who strikes whom.

One character, used twice

The Shang nao was a bronze bell: short and broad, a hollow handle, mouth upward, struck with a beater, three or five in a graded set. The Rites of Zhou says the metal nao stops the drums; Zheng Xuan describes a bell without a clapper, held and struck.

After the Song the same character meant a pair of clashed discs, and Ma Duanlin wrote it down: the name is the same as the nao of the four metals, the thing itself is different. The clashed bo came in with Buddhism around the fourth century; five of the nine Sui court musics used it, seven of the ten Tang.

However you close them

One clash gives a steep onset and a long metallic tail: a sound of blend, not of points. The old description is bright with a deep watery ring, a sandy scrape and a low hum, so it sounds larger than it is.

How you close the discs decides the rest: flat clash is one complete impact, edge clash adds high frequency and sand, muted clash stops the sound at once, and then shimmer and scrape. A beater strikes and leaves; hands decide when the two come apart.

On stage it partners the daluo: the gong supplies weight, the cymbals density. Peking opera grades them into danao, xiaojing, shuibo and chuidabo.
