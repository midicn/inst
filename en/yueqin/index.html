Yueqin (moon lute)

Shaped like a full moon — and the year it was finally declared its own instrument

Related

PitchRuanPipaSanxianJinghu

The yueqin's name stacks two things together: "round as the moon in shape, a qin in sound."

It evolved out of the ancient ruan — its soundbox made into a full moon, its neck shrinking shorter and shorter — until it became the small, bright plucked instrument on opera stages.

⚠️ And its most instructive fact is about when an instrument counts as an instrument: in the Song *Yue Shu* it still stands in a sentence ending "made by Ruan Xian" — one of the ruan's family; by the Qing its neck had shortened and its shape differed greatly; yet it was only in 1950 that it was formally established as an independent instrument. ⭐⭐ Rule ㊶: the body split first; the name was acknowledged later — classification lags behind sound. And lovelier still: in Taiwan, China, Japan and Vietnam the long-necked type may still be counted as a "yueqin" — one name, different borders in different places.

A round one: a moon in shape, a qin in sound

Three points:

1. Round is its name. "Named for a soundbox shaped like a full moon" — thin paulownia face and back for lightness, hardwood rim and neck for stability, and two sound bars and two posts hidden inside: the interior of an empty "moon." 2. Short is its temper. A short neck, 8–9 frets — "its short, stubby build suits high notes especially well," which is why opera bands chose it. 3. The plectrum is its gesture. Four strings in two pairs, fifths tuning, plucked: strikes, rolls, sweeps, presses, shakes and slides — half of them serving grain.

From ruan to yueqin: four notches of separation

⭐ Of the four notches, the first three are changes of body; only the fourth confirms identity:

PeriodWhat it was
Tang–Songthe name "yueqin" is recorded; "round, long-necked, four strings and thirteen frets" — "made by Ruan Xian," written into the ruan's family
Qing"the shaft shortened into a neck" → stubby, eight frets → "short build suits high notes" → into opera and narrative music
1950⭐ formally established as an independent instrument — the shape had long differed; independence came from classification
after1950s wide-neck (18–24 frets, equal temperament, modulates) · 1980s long-shaft (30 frets)

Range

Its seat in Peking opera: three pieces, then four

Its most famous post is the Peking-opera pit:

  • "In the Peking-opera band the yueqin joins jinghu and jing-erhu as the 'three pieces,' one of the chief accompanying instruments";
  • and the sources preserve an earlier version: "before the jing-erhu was invented, the three were jinghu, yueqin and sanxian; with the invention of the jing-erhu these became the 'four pieces.'" ⭐ What happened here is addition — the jing-erhu joined the line-up — the exact opposite of the yu (where the ensemble shrank and the largest died first): one rule of "ensemble history," verified in both directions.
  • The Peking-opera yueqin is stingy with strings: "usually one string, sometimes two" — it wants no chords, only a clear struck point at the right moments.
  • Other operas use it too: Yu opera's "large octagonal fretless yueqin" forms a trio with erxian and sanxian.

⭐ One line for the table: it is the yueqin of the stage and the yueqin of the mountains — the stage wants it to "point clearly," the mountains want it to "say plainly."

Common misconceptions

  • "Yueqin was just the ruan's old name." ⚠️ More tangled: they were one family (the Song text says "made by Ruan Xian"); the Qing split the shapes; 1950 declared the yueqin independent — while in Taiwan, China, Japan and Vietnam the long-necked type may still be called yueqin.
  • "The name comes from the shape, so it must be round." ⚠️ The common form is round, but flat-hexagonal and octagonal forms exist — the name records the most typical shape, not every one.
  • "In Peking opera it plays melodies." ⚠️ Usually one string: its job is the point — a bright strike in the right place, alongside jinghu and jing-erhu.
  • "It only accompanies." ⚠️ It solos, ensembles and accompanies; the 1950s reforms gave it free modulation and three-plus octaves.
  • "Folk instruments and opera instruments are separate worlds." ⚠️ The yueqin lives in both: Yi opera, Bai opera, Yu opera, Peking opera — one instrument, two ecologies.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Three cues:

1. Hear the point. Its notes are short and bright — ink dots on the singing, not lines. 2. Hear the moon. In solos or in Yi and Hani dance-songs it *sings*: in those gatherings it is one way for young people to talk to each other. 3. Hear the two voices. The same instrument is an accompanist on stage and a lead in the hills — before listening, ask where it is standing.
