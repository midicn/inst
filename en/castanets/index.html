The castanets are percussion's most underrated — and hardest to play in time — instrument: they are just two pieces of hardwood. But the two pieces are not flat: they are hollowed into shell-shaped cavities, and those cavities are the key to the sound.

Cavity versus solid is the essential difference from the claves.

⚠️ One conceptual point: the same gesture — two pieces of hardwood struck together — can be two different instruments, because shape decides colour. - Castanets: hollowed shell-shaped wooden shells → the cavity lets them vibrate more freely → crisp, penetrating, a little ring. - Claves: solid cylindrical sticks → damped by the material itself → dry, short, blunt. Both are "two hardwoods struck", with similar material, but one hollow and one solid — completely different colours. Proof that shape decides colour at the smallest scale.

Structure: hollowed shells and how they are held

Three points:

1. The cavity is its soundbox. Hollowing the shell lets it vibrate more freely → a crisp tone with a little ring. 2. A gap between the two, set by the cord's tension. Too tight is dull, too loose is slack. 3. Held in the palm, with palm and thumb opening and closing the pair — so hand shape directly shapes the sound, which is what makes the technique hard: the hand plays while also controlling the instrument.

Range

The castanets are unpitched, so no range chart is generated. Their means lie in hand shape and strokes:

StrokeColour
Single stroke (finger pad and palm heel)a clear single point
Double stroketwo quick clicks
Carretilla (roll)a continuous five-finger roll — the heart of Spanish technique
Thumb strokeduller, lower
Finger strokebrighter, higher

The carretilla is the technical threshold: five fingers falling in turn (or thumb alternating with fingers) give an even string of clicks — the dense rhythms of Spanish dance depend on it.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Crisp, woody, slightly penetrating — the cavity makes it louder than solid claves. 2. Mostly high frequency, so it is heard in a band without being harsh. 3. A short but audible tail, a little longer than claves. 4. Hand shape matters enormously — unlike drums or gongs, the player's hand is at once the stand, the control and the damper.

Playing techniques

  • A pair per hand is the Spanish tradition; both hands may hold pairs (four shells).
  • Carretilla: the five-stroke roll by fingers in sequence.
  • Danced while played: in Spanish dance the castanets coordinate with footwork and body movement, so the player must move and control hand shape at once — far harder than playing seated.
  • In the orchestra they appear mostly in Spanish or exotic passages, played standing by a percussionist or a dancer.

Common misconceptions

  • "Castanets are just two bits of wood — simple." The roll takes years, and holding a pair per hand while dancing is genuinely demanding.
  • "They are the same instrument as claves." Similar material but different shape (hollowed versus solid) → different colour, ring and use.
  • "They have pitch." Unpitched. Different striking spots change colour but do not form a scale.
  • "They are only used in Spanish music." Orchestral music on Spanish subjects and some popular music use them too.
  • "Any grip will do." Hand shape (gap size, position) directly decides the sound — a classic case of the hand being part of the instrument.
