Pengling (bells)

A pair of tiny goblet-shaped bells whose old name is "star"

Related

TimbreRhythmNaobo (Chinese cymbals)BianzhongBianqingYunluoBronze drumMuyu

The pengling is a pair of tiny goblet-shaped bells struck against each other — and its old name is "star".

A name borrowed from the sky, given to something only 5.5 cm across. And what this page is really about is the apparent contradiction in it:

⚠️ It is a pair of bells, and it has no definite pitch. This is the entry where two threads finally stand side by side: | | How it sounds | Pitched? | |---|---|---| | Bianzhong · Bianqing · Yunluo · Bronze drum | struck (hit) | ⭐ yes | | Cymbals · Bangzi · ⭐ Pengling | clashed (collided) | ⭐ no | ⭐ And within the "clashed" column, the pengling is the odd one out — because it is bell-shaped. A bell (a closed shell) is the easiest shape to give a definite pitch (the bianzhong proves it) — yet two bells colliding pushes it back into the unpitched camp. ⭐⭐ Rule ㉖: "being pitched" is not a property of an instrument but a result of "being repeatable" — the shape gave it the possibility of pitch; how it is struck decided it would not use it.

Structure: a pair of goblet-shaped bells

Three points:

1. A pair of "wide at the mouth, narrow below" bells. The mouth flares open at the top and the body narrows to a hemispherical bowl (cap) — that is the fixing point, and the hole for the cord is there. ⭐ Note the word: "bowl" — the same word-family as the cymbal's bowl and the daluo's boss. When Chinese instrument makers name "the fixed centre of the vibrating body", they reach for the vocabulary of vessels. 2. Hollow, with no clapper. It is a pair of small bells with nothing inside — it is not rung by shaking; it is sounded by collision. 3. It must be a pair. Two make a set, joined by a silk cord, leather thong or rope; the player holds one in each hand, mouths upwards, and strikes the rims together — though there is also a version with a single bell on a stick, struck with a metal slip, and some temples use a single bell only.

"Hit" versus "collide"

This figure is the heart of the page.

Line up every percussion instrument on this site by how it is set sounding, and a clean line appears:

  • Struck (by a beater): bianzhong · bianqing · yunluo · bronze drum — all pitched;
  • Clashed (two pieces colliding): cymbals · bangzi · pengling — none with definite pitch.

⭐⭐ This site's observation is that a pitch has to be repeatable before it can be fixed.

  • The bianzhong specifies its striking points (centre and side) — which is why it can even deliver two pitches;
  • the yunluo gives one pitch per gong, always struck in the same place;
  • the bronze drum has two measurable fundamentals at centre and edge, at known positions;
  • but when two objects collide, where they meet, which one leads, and how they couple afterwards are different every time.

⚠️ Note clearly: the sources do not explain this line — the paragraph above is this site's own observation. But with six instruments laid out side by side, the split between "hit" and "collide" is strikingly tidy, and worth recording.

⭐ And the pengling is the subtlest case on that line: it is bell-shaped — and a closed shell is the easiest shape in which to lock a pitch (the bianzhong proves it). The shape gave it the possibility of pitch; how it is struck decided it would not use it.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Bright, ringing, long-sustaining, piercing. The sources say that though it has no definite pitch, its sound carries, and that it suits elegant, lyrical tunes — it is an instrument of "clarity". 2. ⭐ Two bronzes, two tails. Resonant bronze rings long; brass rings shorter. On an instrument this small, the material is the whole of the tonal design. 3. It is far smaller and deeper than the cymbals. Same family — one a large thin disc, the other a small deep goblet — and the colours are as different: one sandy, one bright. 4. It often pairs with a wooden fish. The sources single this out: it is often used with the small wooden fish — ⭐ one wood, one metal: a matched pair of "points".

Playing techniques

  • One bell in each hand, mouths up, raised in front of the chest, struck together. What meets is the rims — the thinnest and most freely vibrating part.
  • ⭐ But its "subtraction" is interesting: it can also be used as a single bell — mounted on a stick and struck with a metal slip; and some temples use only one bell, struck with the right hand. ⭐ So it can collide, or it can be struck — and once it is struck, it is closer to being an ordinary idiophone.
  • ⭐ Its place in the band is unusual: it is usually played by someone who doubles — small instruments tend to hang off someone else's hands (the same pattern as the pedal bangzi, played by the man who also plays the fiddle).
  • It has a precise post in Peking opera: in the civil band it is used for the dan role's fan-erhuang arias — marking the beat inside the singing, and adding ornaments with the drum in the interludes.
  • ⭐ It is often played by non-professionals: in Tibetan Toe and Nangma song-and-dance it is played by women singing as they play; the Naxi use it in Dongjing music; temples use it in chanting. It is an instrument anyone can pick up — 5.5 cm, one hand.

History

PeriodState
Northern and Southern Dynasties (386–589)⭐ already current in China. A "Six Dynasties star-striker" figure is in the Palace Museum in Beijing; the Northern Wei murals at Dunhuang show the instrument being played; Yungang and the Sima Jinlong tomb reliefs both show players
Tangenvoys from Piao (modern Myanmar) brought music to court, where it was called bronze cymbals; the New Book of Tang records its form and playing: "four lingbo, made as in the Qiuci section, three cun across, threaded on leather, struck to keep time"; the Tang lexicon of Buddhist terms describes it as "like a small bottle cap, with a nose and a mouth; strike two together and they sound"; an important part of court music; a Tibetan name, dingxia, appears in ninth-century documents

Its history has a thread no other entry has: its identity keeps swinging between two things — in the documents it is sometimes "bronze cymbals" and sometimes "bell cymbals", and the sources explain why: "its shape lies between a bell and a cymbal." Its name records other people's uncertainty about what to call it.

Common misconceptions

  • "It is a pair of bells, so it must be pitched." ⚠️ It has no definite pitch (five sources agree). It has a bell's shape, but two bells colliding costs it a repeatable pitch. ⭐ The shape gave it the possibility; how it is struck decided it would not use it.
  • "The pengling and the cymbals are different things." The sources are direct: it "can be counted a variant of the cymbal", and "belongs among the cymbal family — its shape lies between a bell and a cymbal, hence lingbo." ⭐ One family: a large thin disc, and a small deep goblet.
  • "It can only be clashed." It can also be struck: a single bell on a stick, hit with a metal slip; some temples use one bell struck with the right hand. ⚠️ And note: once it is struck, it comes closer to being an ordinary idiophone.
  • "It is a bit-part instrument." It has a precise post in the Peking-opera civil band (the dan's fan-erhuang arias), it is used across Jiangnan sizhu, Cantonese music, Fujian nanyin and Xi'an drum music, and it is a rhythmic instrument of Tibetan Buddhist chanting. It is small, but its post is specific.
  • "In the bayin it is 'metal', so it belongs with the gongs and cymbals." By material, yes — metal. But in Hornbostel–Sachs it is concussion (with the cymbals and the bangzi), on a different branch from the struck gongs — same material, different excitation, different branch.

Next

Percussion now has fourteen entries. One thing is worth looking back at:

four instruments here are already on the "carried" line — the daluo (carried on its cord), the cymbals (struck while walking), the elephant-foot drum (slung and danced), and the pengling (one goblet in each hand).

The last small instrument takes a different branch: the hand drum — it is not carried, it is turned in the hand; it uses no beater either, but changes position and hand-shape on the skin.

So it asks the batch's last question: when pitch is no longer needed at all, how many sounds can one skin still hold?
