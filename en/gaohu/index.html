This page is about a "new" instrument: born in the 1920s — among the youngest of all Chinese instruments.

Its most distinctive feature first: it did not "add an instrument"; it changed an ensemble's character.

Guangdong music's early line-up was called the "hard bow": erxian, zhutihu, sanxian, yueqin and transverse flute — a "five-piece stand" whose bright, high, ringing sound earned it the name "hard-bow combination." After the gaohu arrived: gaohu, yangqin, qinqin, dongxiao and yehu formed the "soft-bow five," "better able to express delicate shifts of feeling."

⭐⭐ Rule 51: a new instrument turned a whole ensemble from hard to soft. The sources put it plainly: "behind Guangdong music's evolution from 'hard' to 'soft' lies musicians' spirit of blending and embracing change." — it did not simply displace the erxian; it gave the whole genre a new way of breathing.

How it was "built": new strings, new posture

⭐ Its birth took two moves — and both came from another instrument:

MoveFrom whereResult
New stringsthe violin"erhu's silk strings changed to the violin's steel" → brighter, more penetrating
New posturegripped playing"played with the box gripped between the legs", held vertically on the right thigh
New tuning—"with the fourth-up tuning" → G4–D5, a fourth above the erhu

⭐ And two people made the connection: "Lü Wencheng, an erhu player, met the violinist Situ Mengyan, and each influenced the other" — one played erhu, one played violin; the gaohu is what those two hands produced.

Why it is gripped

⭐ The most physical — and cleverest — detail on this page:

"The thighs grip the skin and sound window to control tone and volume, and to reduce the wolf tone."

  • Gripping = pressurising the box: skin partly covered, resonance damped → volume and colour become adjustable by hand — or rather by leg;
  • ⭐ The "wolf tone" is an old string-instrument problem: a pitch that hits the body's resonance and squawks — gripping the skin presses that resonance peak down.
  • So the posture is not a habit but a functional design: other huqins colour the tone with the hands; this one also has a leg to do it with.

Its timbre was grown from a dialect

⭐ The line worth remembering:

"Delicate slides and rich ornamentation make the gaohu's sound fit the rise and fall of Cantonese tones, holding Lingnan's warm everydayness."

— Cantonese is a tonal language, and the gaohu's slides and ornaments play exactly that contour. Unlike the banhu, born to "roar," or the erhu, long on "lament": here a dialect's tones were turned into a timbre.

⭐ Compare rule ㊽ ("an instrument can have dialects"): the dombra is one instrument growing different shapes in different places; the gaohu goes further — a local accent grew an instrument outright.

Range

Common misconceptions

  • "The gaohu is just a higher erhu." ⚠️ It is not an erhu tuned up: smaller box · round skin and window · violin steel strings · gripped posture · tuning a fourth higher — a redesigned instrument.
  • "It is an old instrument." ⚠️ It dates to the 1920s, created by Situ Mengyan and his student Lü Wencheng, replacing the erxian — one of the youngest Chinese instruments.
  • "Gripping the box is just a habit." ⚠️ It is a function: tone and volume control, and wolf-tone reduction.
  • "It only plays cheerful music." ⚠️ It is famous for bright, clear playing, but *Autumn Moon over the Calm Lake* is a moonlit reverie — it excels at "bright," not "noisy."
  • "Orchestral arrangements would switch it to erhu." ⚠️ On the contrary: Peng Xiuwen's arrangements of *Step by Step* and *Colourful Clouds Chasing the Moon* "kept the gaohu part, without substituting the erhu."

Timbre, and how to hear it

Three cues:

1. Hear the brightness. "Clear and bright" — steel strings and a small box make a natural pair with the erhu's warmth. 2. Hear the slides. "Delicate slides and rich ornamentation" — its flavour lives in the left hand, which is exactly where it "fits Cantonese tones." 3. Hear the "point-line dialogue." In *Step by Step* the woodblock taps points while the strings draw lines — points and lines are the skeleton of its most famous piece (the woodblock has its own page here).
