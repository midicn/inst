The bronze drum has the word "drum" in its name and not one scrap of skin on it.

It is an instrument cast in bronze: head and body are one single casting — no skin, no tacks, no hoop, nothing that can be tightened or loosened. So although it is called a drum, it is classified as an idiophone: what vibrates is the bronze itself, not a membrane.

⚠️ It forces the same question as the last few entries, and gives the most direct answer yet: when an instrument looks like a drum, is named like a drum, and is used like a drum — on what grounds do we say it is not one? Structure: a drum with no skin

Three points:

1. Five parts, one casting. Head · chest · waist · foot · ear — flat rim, curved waist, one head, hollow, open at the base, four ears. The body carries mould seams: the evidence that it was cast. 2. ⭐ Its shape is an acoustic structure. The sources put it plainly: the head and the swollen chest form an arched sound-storing and resonating chamber; the sound passes through the cylindrical waist and leaves through the foot, which flares like the mouth of a horn. It is a sound pipe from head to foot. 3. Two orders of magnitude in size. From a handspan to over a metre across, from tens of kilos to hundreds; the largest surviving example is about 1.65 m across and 300 kg. It is the largest instrument on this site.

The patterns on the head

This is the page's second central figure.

A bronze drum's head is a picture. And every picture on it starts at the centre:

  • The sun motif: a disc of light with rays. ⭐ It began as the mark left by the casting gate — ground and polished into a raised disc, then combined with sun worship into art. It is the earliest ornament on bronze drums and the only one found throughout their history.
  • Frogs: cast in relief, four, five or six of them; ⭐ "stacked frogs" — a small frog on a big one's back, up to three or four deep. (Frogs and rain-making belong together.)
  • Figurative scenes: on Shizhaishan-type drums the chest carries feathered dancers, boat races, fishing and sacrifice — not decoration but a two-thousand-year-old record of southern life.

⭐⭐ And the rings may be more than ornament. The tuning research raises a striking suggestion: one of the ways a bronze drum vibrates is that the head moves in concentric bands, alternately up and down along nodal circles; and the dense rings on the head may show that the makers were already using the principle of nodal lines. In other words: those circles may be marks cut for tuning.

It was never only an instrument

Of every entry on this site, the bronze drum has the smallest share of "instrument" in its identity:

  • It is power. Monopolised by chiefs and nobles, treated as a precious treasure or ritual object, and even an object of sacrifice itself — hence "where the drum is, the authority is."
  • It is wealth. The plainest evidence is in the *Later Book of Han*: "Ma Yuan, on campaign in Jiaozhi, took the bronze drums of the Luoyue and cast them into horses." ⭐ It was worth melting down. (And that sentence is the first appearance of the word "bronze drum" in Chinese.)
  • It is a signal. "Striking it could gather people to deliberate, and it served as a military command" — and the sources list it alongside "ritual object" and "treasure" as a means of communication.
  • It is a ritual object. Early drums were hidden in caves or buried, not to be struck at will — ⭐ once an instrument is forbidden to be played casually, it is no longer only an instrument.

⭐⭐ Rule ㉔: in the Bronze Age, an instrument was never only an instrument. And the bronze drum and the bianzhong are exactly parallel cases:

How it is tuned: a trade of its own

The pitch of a bronze drum is largely settled at the casting — but it is not unadjustable. On the contrary, tuning a bronze drum was once a profession.

Period / typeThe method
the original method⭐ quenching with chemicals · cold hammering · scraping the walls — three steps
Lengshuichong typea pair of half-ring ears added between the main ears, and attachments hung from them to control the sound ⭐ tuning by hanging weights
Beiliu · Lingshan typeschanging the thickness of the head — gouging the back of the head thinner (hence the tuning gouges on those drums)

⭐⭐ And a Qing writer, Qu Dajun in his *New Talks of Guangdong*, describes it as a whole trade:

The ordinary founders of bronze drums could not tune them. Whenever a drum came out of the mould, a "bronze-drum master" had to be invited to tune it. He would quench the drum's navel (the sun motif at the centre) and its surroundings with a little chemical, and give it a few strokes of a cold hammer — seemingly with no effort, a dozen strokes or so and it was done. It was a highly skilled craft.

⭐ Points worth carrying away:

1. Tuning moves in two directions: adding and subtracting. Gouging is subtracting (the same family as grinding a bianqing thinner); hanging attachments is adding mass — and adding is something neither the bianzhong nor the bianqing can do. (Nor is there a screw to turn as on a paigu: a bronze drum has no mechanism, only material to add or remove.) 2. The tuner and the founder were two different people. The caster could not tune; the tuner was sent for — the "last mile" of an instrument is completed by another trade. 3. On a bronze drum, a note can literally be stuck on. Which is the same idea as the next entry, the elephant-foot drum, tuned with sticky rice.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Low, thick, very long-ringing. The old books outdo one another: Song writers say it "rings so loud it rivals the alligator drum" and that its "sound startles the village"; Qing writers say it is "heard for miles" and "shakes the clouds". And one lovely line: "hung and struck over water, its sound is neither bell nor drum." 2. ⭐⭐ Each drum gives two notes. The general reference works say a bronze drum "has no definite pitch" — meaning it has no standard pitch — yet on any single drum the centre and the edge give two definite notes. The 1982 tuning study measured them: on Wanjiaoba-type drums the two are a minor second apart (109 cents, within 3 cents of the just-intonation semitone), while Shizhaishan-type drums favour perfect fifths and minor thirds. ⚠️ "No definite pitch" does not mean "no pitch" — a point that is unusually easy to misread here. 3. Centre low, edge bright — and this direction is sourced. A soft beater on the centre gives a thick, low sound; a bamboo slip on the edge gives a crisp, short one. 4. The length of the ring can be handled by hand — by a second player with a resonator (below).

Playing techniques

⭐⭐ A bronze drum takes two people.

  • The first holds a soft beater in one hand and a bamboo slip in the other — the soft beater on the centre (thick and low), the slip on the edge (crisp and short). ⭐ One person carrying two completely different strikers, because they must produce two different colours.
  • The second holds a resonator, aimed at the open foot, and slides it back and forth — ⭐⭐ to reinforce the resonance and to control how long the ring lasts. That is "adjusting the tail in real time" — the same need as muting a daluo with the hand, except that here it is delegated to a second person.
  • The drum is struck while hanging, so that the open foot can speak.
  • Its most common "performance" is a ritual, not a stage. Sacrifice, festival, wedding, funeral — it is used, not admired.
  • It is still playing today. The Zhuang, Yao, Miao, Dong, Bouyei, Sui, Gelao and Wa still use it; the Zhuang bronze-drum custom is on the national intangible-heritage list.

One thing in this history no other entry has: its casting technique was once lost, and then learned again. A two-thousand-year-old instrument was "re-learned" in the 1990s.

Common misconceptions

  • "A bronze drum is a drum." ⚠️ It is not a membranophone: it has no skin — the head is a plate of cast bronze. There is one test: what vibrates? The bronze body (idiophone). The name comes from how it looks; the classification comes from what vibrates.
  • "The books say it has no definite pitch, so it has no pitch." ⚠️ Those are two different things. "No definite pitch" means no standard pitch (you cannot say "this drum is in C"); but on any single drum the centre and the edge give two definite notes — measured at a minor second (109 cents) on Wanjiaoba drums and mostly perfect fifths and minor thirds on Shizhaishan drums. "No standard pitch" ≠ "no definite note."
  • "It looks like a drum, so the bayin puts it under 'hide'." ⚠️ No: the bayin classifies by material, and a bronze drum is bronze — so it is metal. The "hide" class holds only skinned instruments (tanggu, bangu). ⭐ Which is exactly why the bayin and Hornbostel–Sachs ask two different questions: one asks what it is made of, the other what vibrates.
  • "It is cast, so the pitch is fixed at the mould and cannot be changed." ⚠️ It can — and tuning it was a trade: quenching, cold hammering, scraping the walls, hanging attachments, gouging the back of the head thinner. The Qing *New Talks of Guangdong* even names the specialist — the "bronze-drum master" — and notes that the founder himself could not do it.
  • "The bronze drum is a museum object." It is still in use: Zhuang, Yao, Miao, Dong, Bouyei, Sui, Gelao and Wa communities use it in festivals and rituals today; the Zhuang custom is national intangible heritage; and a 6.68 m record drum was cast in 2018.
