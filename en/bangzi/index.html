Bangzi (wooden clapper)

Two hardwood sticks that gave a whole vocal genre its name

1 min read66 characters条目Chinese PercussionUpdated 2026-09-26

Two hardwood sticks, one twenty-five centimetres long, one twenty, and a whole vocal genre is named after them: bangzi qiang.

Two sticks

The long one is cylindrical, about four centimetres across. The short one is rectangular, twenty by five or six by four. The rectangular sits in the left hand, the round in the right, crossed at right angles and struck together. The wood is usually rosewood, mahogany or jujube heart, solid and thoroughly dry, free of knots and cracks.

The instrument has two parts and nothing else. A bianzhong must be cast and tuned, a yunluo needs ten gongs and a frame, a paigu five drums and tuners. The bangzi has nothing to adjust; everything rests on the density and dryness of one piece of wood. It gave up complexity and bought being usable anywhere.

One name, four shapes

Bangzi is not a shape but a position: any hardwood noisemaker taking over the clapper's job can be called a bangzi. In four genres it grew into four objects.

The Hebei type is two sticks struck together. The southern bangzi, or Guangdong board, is a hollow rectangle with a slot, hit with a bamboo slip and never clashed; Peking opera's gaobozi uses it too. The pedal bangzi is egg-shaped, body and hammer in one, tied to a table leg and worked by the foot in Henan opera, usually by the zhuqin player. The Qin bangzi is oval, twenty centimetres, for Qin opera.

They diverged over whose hands were still free. Those hands were needed for the fiddle, so the beat went to a foot.

First a signal

Its history as a signal is far longer than as an opera instrument. Its first two jobs had nothing to do with music: marking the night watches, and mustering people for the yamen.

In Water Margin the tenants hear the bangzi and come running with spears and staves. Ba Jin's Shading is more precise: the bangzi of the second watch begins, its clear wooden sound answering the dogs in the quiet night. Nor was its earliest form two solid sticks; it was bamboo, or wood hollowed out.

The name goes to the steadiest part

In the late Ming and early Qing the bangzi spread with bangzi opera and became its time-keeper. Li Diaoyuan's Ju Shuo has it: with the bangzi as the clapper, the yueqin answers it, there is slack and tight; commonly called bangzi qiang.

A lead can be swapped; a time-keeper cannot. Names go to the steadiest element, which is why Peking opera is not called jinghu opera.

Today the four types coexist. Tongzhou bangzi entered the second national intangible heritage list in 2008 — the genre, not the two sticks.
