The rebab

The ancestor of nearly every European bowed string, displaced by the violin in its own homeland

1 min read431 characters条目World InstrumentsUpdated 2026-10-21

The rebab stands on the floor. Not under the chin, not in the lap: an iron spike under the body holds it up, cello-fashion, and the player crouches to bow it.

The body is half a coconut shell or a small wooden box, faced with sheepskin or parchment. The neck is long and thin, ending in a pegbox; one to three strings run down it with no fingerboard, so pitch is wherever the finger lands.

A posture set by one spike

The spike sets how close it sits to the floor and the legs. The skin face radiates at the body: few overtones, a hard attack, a sound that arrives close. The price is that shifting position is awkward; the upper positions barely exist.

The bow is far more curved than a violin's, strung with horsehair. Whether the hair runs between or outside the strings varies by school, and this instrument sits on that divide.

A little over an octave

With no fingerboard the finger stops the string directly; there are only so many places to stop it. Instead of a bigger body and a wider compass, it made its timbre resemble a voice — the quality the sources praise. Narrow is a division of labour.

More names than shapes

Arabic says rebab, Iraq joza, Persian kamanche, meaning little bow; Afghanistan ghichak, Egypt kamanga, Thailand saw sam sai.

The gamelan rebab is the strangest branch: two strings, a coconut body with a skin face, and bow hair left slack so tension comes from the wrist — every stroke retunes the note a little. A Javanese ensemble keeps two, in pelog and slendro, never played together.

The ancestor shown the door

In the eleventh century the pear-shaped rebab reached Western Europe as the rebec; the Byzantine lira shares that root. Part of the violin's blood is rebab blood.

Then it lost at home. Across the Arab world it gave way to the violin and the kemenche. Turkey is stranger still: in the second half of the eighteenth century the viola d'amore arrived from Europe, the local kemence fell out of favour and survived only in the Mevlevi lodge as rebab. The old name was forgotten; the borrowed one stuck.

When an instrument is displaced, what survives is rarely the shape. It is the position. The lodge wanted a bowed string, not a violin.
