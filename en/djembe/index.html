The djembe

The name is a proverb, the instrument is a gathering — a drum anyone may play

In Bambara there is a saying: Anke djé, anke bé — everyone gather together in peace. The djembe takes its name from it: djé is to gather, bé is peace. Few instrument names are invitations.

The name is an invitation

The first half says gather, the second says peace: together they describe the drum's job in a village — weddings, naming ceremonies, harvest, festivals, people coming together with the drum in the middle.

It appears in the Mande world around the thirteenth century and is now found from Guinea to Senegal. The shell is one block of hardwood carved into a goblet, covered with goat skin and tuned by a rope weave.

Blacksmiths made it; anyone may play it

The origin is credited to the Mandinka blacksmith caste, the Numu. A smith's craft lives in wood and fire, not in blood, so it can be taught outward: anyone may become a djembefola, literally one who plays the djembe.

The neighbouring instruments are not like this. Balafon, kora and ngoni belong to the hereditary griot caste; the djembe is not a griot instrument. A drum made by blacksmiths can be played by anyone; a kora raised by griots belongs to griots.

Three spots on one drum

Played by hand, the drum gives three landing places and three textures. The heel of the hand in the centre is the bass; the fingers slightly off-centre give the tone; the fingertips snapped near the rim give the slap. Those three carry almost every traditional rhythm.

The Mandinka say a good player can make the drum talk — telling a story, not sending a message. The Malinké never used the djembe as a signalling drum; that was the talking drum's work. Two drums, two trades.

Ensemble, and the road out

The djembe almost never plays alone. Its home is a percussion ensemble: a lead player, three bass drums (dununba, sangban, kenkeni) and dancers. The lead plays breaks between sections, signalling dancers and band at once.

Traditionally men play the djembe and dunun, while women usually play the shekere, karignan and kese kese. The road out came in two steps: national folkloric companies took the drum to the world stage in the 1950s and 60s, and tours by masters such as Mamady Keïta and Famoudou Konaté widened it. What travels is the drum; what stays is the context of the rhythm.
