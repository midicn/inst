The taiko

Japan's family of drums — shrine ritual, Noh and Kabuki, and the modern taiko ensemble

1 min read137 characters条目World InstrumentsUpdated 2026-10-28

In Japanese, "taiko" is not one instrument but the word for drums as a class: the drum at a shrine, on the Noh stage, or in a festival procession. Treat it as "a kind of Japanese bass drum" and you miss the structure — one class, several institutions.

The main line: a class, not an instrument

The long-bodied nagado-daiko has its skins held by tacks; the shallow hira-daiko is a related form; the shime-daiko pulls two skins together with ropes and leather tabs and can be tuned; the katsugi-daiko is portable. Sizes run from one hand to a storey. What separates them is not shape but who plays them, and where.

Form: tacks and rope

On a nagado, the edge of each skin is nailed with a row of tacks (byou), and the pitch is largely set at the workshop. On a shime, tightening the ropes raises the pitch, so it can agree with flute and shamisen. Tacks fix the sound, rope makes it adjustable. Only one head is struck; the body acts as a resonator.

Occasions: shrine, Noh, Kabuki, kumi-daiko

The shrine drum is played to be heard by the kami. The Noh hayashi is built on flute, small and large shoulder drums, with a taiko joining only some plays; Kabuki's geza uses shime-daiko and ōdaiko for entrances, battles and weather. In a festival procession the drum hangs from the shoulder and is struck while walking. In the 1950s Daihachi Oguchi arranged the drums into an ensemble, and kumi-daiko took shape; Ondekoza and Kodo carried it to world stages, turning drummers into dancers. In the Kojiki, Ame-no-Uzume dances on an overturned tub before Amaterasu's cave — that is myth, not drum history; it says only that the drum's first job was to call the kami.

Against the bass drum and the djembe

The Western bass drum is double-headed and aims at a mass of low frequency; a taiko is struck on one head, and the body carries the sound, so its low end has a clear centre. The djembe is a single-headed goblet drum, played by hand with three tones, led by a soloist and grounded by dunun; taiko uses sticks and separates registers by head size and body length. Two easy mistakes: taking taiko for one large drum, and taking kumi-daiko for an ancient tradition.

Three things to take away

1. A class, not an instrument: nagado, hira, shime and katsugi each have their own institution. 2. Tacks fix, ropes tune: two mountings produce two kinds of musical life. 3. From festival to kumi-daiko: the listener changes from kami to audience, but the strokes remain the same body work.
