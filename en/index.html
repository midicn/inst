Instruments

An illustrated atlas of instruments — Western, Chinese, electronic and world — with range charts, how each one makes its sound, and hand-drawn diagrams.

Strings

  • BanjoA plucked instrument whose resonator is a membrane stretched over a circular rim, with a short high drone string
  • CimbalomThe Hungarian concert hammered dulcimer — legs, a damper pedal, fully chromatic, four octaves, engineered in 1874
  • SanturA Persian struck zither with a trapezoid box, two sets of nine bridges and seventy-two strings, played with two light hammers
  • ViolA fretted six-string bowed instrument played with an underhand grip — the chamber instrument of the Renaissance
  • HarpForty-seven strings and seven pedals on a triangular frame — the strings carry only the diatonic scale
  • LutePear-shaped with a ribbed back and several doubled courses — the solo instrument of the Renaissance and Baroque
  • MandolinFour doubled courses tuned exactly like a violin, played with a pick — tremolo is its signature
  • UkuleleA small four-string nylon instrument whose standard tuning is not in pitch order
  • Acoustic GuitarSix steel strings over X-bracing, with the neck joining the body at the fourteenth fret
  • Classical GuitarSix nylon strings, fan bracing, and a plucking hand that uses the fingernails
  • Double BassThe largest and lowest of the string family — sloped shoulders, fourths tuning, and written an octave above sounding pitch
  • CelloThe baritone of the string family, held between the knees — its voice is the closest to a human one
  • ViolaThe violin's family sibling a fifth lower — darker in tone, and the glue of the inner parts in an ensemble
  • ViolinA bowed instrument whose four strings are tuned in fifths — the highest standing section of the orchestra

Woodwinds

  • Pan FluteSeveral closed tubes in a row, one note each — an ancient instrument invented worldwide
  • OcarinaA closed-vessel flute whose pitch comes from cavity volume and hole area — hence its free shape
  • RecorderThe old duct flute whose air is shaped by a fixed channel — the Baroque's standard solo wind
  • Baritone SaxophoneThe largest common saxophone — folded twice and written a major thirteenth above sounding
  • Tenor SaxophoneThe sax written a major ninth above sounding — the signature voice of jazz soloing
  • Alto SaxophoneA single reed driving a conical metal tube — where the two axes of woodwind classification cross
  • Soprano SaxophoneThe straight soprano sax — written exactly like a clarinet, but built on a conical bore
  • ContrabassoonThe double-reed an octave below the bassoon — nearly six metres of tube, lower than a double bass
  • BassoonA double-reed conical tube folded into a U — the widest-ranging woodwind
  • Bass ClarinetA folded low clarinet, written a major ninth above its sounding pitch
  • E-flat ClarinetA small clarinet a minor third above the standard one — the brightest spike in the band
  • ClarinetA cylindrical tube with a single reed — it overblows a twelfth, which splits it into three distinct registers
  • English HornThe F alto oboe — a name that has nothing to do with England, and a bulbous bell
  • Oboe d'amoreThe Baroque oboe a minor third lower than the standard one, with a bulbous bell
  • OboeA conical tube driven by a double reed — the instrument an orchestra tunes to
  • Alto FluteThe G transposing flute — longer, wider and notably softer than the standard flute
  • FluteThe only woodwind with no reed — its sound comes from an air jet cut against the edge of the embouchure hole
  • PiccoloThe top of the flute family — written an octave below its actual sound

Brass

  • Wagner TubaA horn mouthpiece on a tuba-like body — built for The Ring of the Nibelung
  • OphicleideThe keyed brass low instrument that held the bass before the tuba
  • SerpentA wooden, leather-covered low brass in a snake shape — stopped by finger holes, not valves
  • Natural TrumpetThe valveless Baroque trumpet — its notes come from the harmonic series alone
  • SousaphoneA tuba coiled to wrap around the body with the bell facing forward — built for marching
  • EuphoniumThe brass register between trombone and tuba — the wind band's tenor-bass
  • Bass TromboneThe wider-bored trombone with valve attachments — the bass of the trombone section
  • TubaThe largest common brass — the bass foundation of orchestra and wind band
  • TromboneThe brass whose slide changes the tube continuously — the only one that can truly glide
  • French HornThe coiled horn with a funnel mouthpiece — the hardest brass to keep in tune
  • FlugelhornThe trumpet family's softest member — the extreme of conical bore and deep cup
  • CornetThe more conical valved brass — the mainstay of wind bands and early jazz
  • TrumpetThe piston-valve brass driven by vibrating lips — the most agile high brass in orchestra and jazz

Percussion

  • PandeiroLike a tambourine, but not one — a tunable frame drum, the heartbeat of Brazilian rhythm
  • HandpanTwo steel hemispheres pressed together — each dent in the surface is a note
  • Latin PercussionSeveral instruments, one per player, organised by a shared rhythmic pattern
  • Drum KitKick, snare, toms and cymbals assembled into one set — played by a single person with four limbs
  • WoodblockA hollowed hardwood block borrowed from East Asia — with a clearer sense of pitch than its cousins
  • RainstickA shaken idiophone of internal spirals and grains — its shape of sound set by how fast you tilt it
  • MaracasHollow gourds filled with seeds — the underlay of the Latin rhythm section
  • Wind ChimesHung metal or bamboo tubes that ring in sheets — and when the wind triggers them, nobody controls the rhythm
  • AnvilA real or imitation anvil as an instrument — for smithies and machinery on stage
  • WhipTwo boards slapped together for one sharp crack — a marker of downbeats
  • ClavesTwo solid hardwood sticks struck together — the timekeeper of Latin music
  • CastanetsTwo hardwood shells struck together — the rhythmic core of Spanish music
  • CelestaSteel plates struck by a keyboard — a glassy idiophone played by a keyboard player
  • Tubular BellsA row of hung metal tubes, one note each — the orchestral imitation of church bells
  • GlockenspielSteel bars — the brightest, most transparent idiophone
  • VibraphoneMetal bars over resonators with rotating vanes — a shimmering idiophone whose "vibrato" is really amplitude
  • MarimbaSofter wooden bars over long resonators — warm, resonant, and capable of polyphony
  • XylophoneHard wooden bars over resonators — a dry, brittle, clearly grained idiophone
  • GongThe alloy-disc idiophone — unpitched, with an extremely long tail
  • CymbalsBronze idiophones struck in pairs or hung singly — an extremely rich, noise-like spectrum
  • TambourineA frame drum with a ring of jingles — one instrument straddling both percussion classes
  • TriangleA steel rod bent into a triangle — the brightest, most focused idiophone
  • Bass DrumThe two-headed membranophone with the deepest low end — in orchestra and drum kit alike
  • Snare DrumThe membranophone with snares across its lower head — pitchless, and the heart of military music and the drum kit
  • TimpaniThe tunable membranophone drum — the core of orchestral percussion

Keyboards

  • HarmonicaA breath-driven free-reed instrument that sounds on both blow and draw, with bends for semitones
  • AccordionA portable free-reed instrument whose hand bellows give direct dynamic control
  • HarmoniumA keyboard instrument whose foot bellows drives free reeds — an aerophone with a different vibrating body from the organ
  • OrganKeys open valves that admit air into pipes — an aerophone whose notes can sustain indefinitely
  • ClavichordThe keyboard instrument whose brass tangents strike the strings — dynamic, capable of vibrato, and extremely quiet
  • HarpsichordThe keyboard-plucked chordophone — plucking fixes the volume, so colour comes from stops and string sets
  • PianoA keyboard-struck chordophone with touch-sensitive dynamics — one of the few full-range instruments

Early Instruments

  • Mechanical InstrumentsDevices that store a performance on pinned barrels and paper rolls — they record actions, not sound
  • BugleThe valveless military horn — its calls use only the notes of one triad, with all the information moved into rhythm
  • Hunting HornA metal horn with no valves and no holes — it can only reach the few points of one tube's harmonic series
  • Hurdy-GurdyA crank turns a rosined wheel against the strings — one player, two hands, an unbroken drone plus a melody
  • Portative OrganThe pocket pipe organ pumped by the player's own left hand — buying portability with range, volume and polyphony
  • TheorboThe lute with an absurdly long neck, built for the continuo bass — low notes from longer strings, not thicker ones
  • RacketThe bass double reed whose long conical bore is folded into a column — a bass voice from a pocket-sized body
  • CrumhornThe Renaissance woodwind that seals its double reed inside a wind cap — suddenly quiet, undynamic, played in consorts
  • ShawmThe Renaissance double-reed ancestor of the oboe — loud, brilliant and built for outdoors
