The yunluo is a "gong", but it is built the opposite way round from every other gong.

An ordinary gong is one instrument for one note, and has to be made big to be heard; the yunluo instead packs ten pitches onto one frame. It is not a louder gong — it is an instrument you can play melodies on with a pair of beaters.

⚠️ It forces the last question in this site's percussion group: once a percussion instrument has been laid out as a scale, is it still a percussion instrument? We have seen this move before, in the bianzhong and the bianqing: write the pitch into the material in advance, and let each instrument own a single note. The yunluo uses the same trick — but what it buys is different. It did not invent a new instrument. Structure: ten notes on one frame

Three points:

1. The frame has three leaves — three cells on each outer leaf, four in the middle: ten cells. One gong hangs in each. That is the whole secret of its being melodic: ten pitches, fixed in advance, laid out within reach. 2. ⭐ The topmost gong is called the *gaogong luo* — and it is rarely used. So of ten gongs only nine are in daily use, and the instrument is also known as the "nine-note gong" (there, "nine" means *many*, not exactly nine). Its name does not come from how many gongs it has; it comes from the notes actually used. 3. It has to hang free. Each gong is suspended in its cell by three silk cords and touches nothing; the face is flat with no boss, and the rim extends outward as a flat ring into which the cord holes are drilled. This is the exact opposite of the great gong (which has a raised boss at the centre). Any contact with a hard body drains vibration away and muddies the pitch — to get a melody you first remove the blur: the shape loses its boss, and the mounting loses its contacts.

From a dot to a line

This figure is the heart of the page.

An ordinary percussion instrument can only handle time: it tells you *a sound happened now*. Lay ten pitches out in a row, though, and pitch joins the list of dimensions it can control — and a rhythm tool becomes a melody tool.

What is really worth noting is how it was done: not by inventing a new instrument, but by copying the same instrument ten times.

That separates it from the other way of tying instruments together:

⭐ Two combination instruments, two opposite goals: one spreads out across timbre, the other up through pitch. (And a third route entirely is the marimba: one instrument carrying a whole scale on its own. Same result; the yunluo gets there by joining ten instruments together.)

One knob left: thickness

All ten gongs of a traditional yunluo are about 8 cm across — not because nobody could make gongs of different sizes, but because the dimension of size was deliberately switched off.

Two things worth carrying away:

  • "Thick, and the note is clear; thin, and the note is muddy." Those eight words come from Jia Gongyan's Tang-dynasty commentary on the *Kao Gong Ji*, and they state exactly what plate vibration does: frequency is proportional to thickness. The ten gongs of a yunluo are that sentence practised in its purest form: with size frozen, only one variable is left. (The same rule appears on the bianqing page as "if too high, grind the faces; if too low, grind the ends.")
  • ⭐ The reformed yunluo unfroze size. The 29-gong version of 1963 was built on the model of the bells in the Confucius Temple at Qufu, with gongs of different sizes as well as thicknesses; by the 38-gong version the largest bass gong is 22 cm across and the smallest treble gong only 7 cm — it went all the way round and came back to the bianzhong's logic. Which dimension you freeze is a choice; change it and you have changed the instrument.

⚠️ One point of disagreement in the sources: most say the ten gongs are all 8 cm across, while others record them as 7–8.5 cm, slightly differing in size and thickness (practice varies by genre). Both are given here; what they agree on is that thickness is the main means.

Range

  • Ten gongs are ten notes. Its "range" is not a continuous band but the points plotted above — hence the discrete-note figure here (the same drawing used for the hunting horn).
  • About an octave and a fourth — far narrower than the zheng or ruan — but it does not need to be wide: its job is to pick out the skeletal notes of a melody inside an ensemble.
  • The reformed yunluo changed targets: 29/36/38 gongs tuned to equal temperament, three octaves, fully modulating — from "a fixed set of notes" to a complete melodic instrument.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Clear, round, long-ringing — and not loud. It is the variable-pitch percussion of the metal idiophones: it wins not by volume but by clarity of pitch. 2. Its ring is longer than a chime's and shorter than a bell's. In an ensemble it often marks neither the beat nor the pulse but draws the outline of the melody. 3. Hard and soft beaters are two different instruments. Hard (horn, hard rubber) is firm and bright, at home in the treble and in fast melodies; soft (soft rubber) is gentle and singing, close to a lightly struck bell. 4. The reformed yunluo has three registers with three characters: the bass dark and thick, slow to speak, long-ringing; the middle pure and transparent — the most expressive melodic register; the treble quick, very short-ringing, crisp and sharp.

Playing techniques

  • Two ways of holding it, for two situations: ① handle in the left hand (or braced against a table), right hand striking with a single beater — fewer ornaments, suited to processional playing (walking while striking); ② handle removed, the three leaves propped up at an angle, one beater in each hand — more ornaments, suited to seated playing. ⭐ Whether you have to walk decides how complex the part can be — the same family of facts as this site's "volume is social radius": the size of an instrument is set not only by sound but by whether it has to be carried (which is why processional sets usually stop at ten gongs).
  • Single strokes, double strokes, rolls, light and heavy strokes, plus double stops and arpeggios; two beaters in each hand, struck simultaneously, give a four-note chord — close to how the marimba is played.
  • Xi'an drum music uses paired yunluo: two frames joined in the middle with no handle, standing like a pair of folding screens, struck by one player — when one frame is not enough, join two.
  • Its double stops can be combined with a sheng to imitate a bell.

1950s–60sreform: in 1963 Zhao Xingru and the Zhoucun Ludong instrument workshop in Shandong built a 29-gong yunluo on the model of the bells in the Confucius Temple at Qufu (range g–f3); the Beijing workshop made a 36-gong set; in 1964 Tian Dezhong with the same workshop produced the 38-gong version (smaller bodies, thicker faces for the top register; range fixed at c1–c4, three octaves; a shuangguang gong added above the frame). Todaya colour instrument in wind-and-percussion bands, shifan drumming, Hebei and Shandong wind music, Shanxi drum music and Buddhist/Taoist ritual music, and a melodic percussion voice in the modern Chinese orchestra in its own right (works such as *Molten Steel Flows* and *Song of the Reservoir* are led by the yunluo)

Common misconceptions

  • "A yunluo is just a scaled-down gong." The opposite: it is many gongs plus a frame, and it is flat-faced with no boss, hung on three cords, and tuned almost purely by thickness — apart from "bronze, struck with a beater" it differs from a great gong in nearly every respect.
  • "Ten gongs means ten usable notes." It is ten gongs, yes — but the top one, the *gaogong luo*, is rarely used, which is why it is also called the nine-note gong; and the layout differs by region and genre, so there is no single national set.
  • "It is an unpitched colour percussion instrument." It has definite pitch, and in wind-and-percussion bands it carries the skeletal notes of the melody; after the reform it is a full melodic instrument that can modulate. ("Has pitch" and "is percussion" do not conflict — the class asks what vibrates, not what the instrument plays.)
  • "Bigger gongs are lower, so a ten-gong set must be ten sizes." A traditional yunluo freezes size: all ten are about 8 cm across, and the pitch differences come mainly from thickness — the central point of this page.
  • "The bayin call it metal, so a yunluo is a 'metal instrument'." As with the guqin: the bayin records the material, while in Hornbostel–Sachs it is also an idiophone (answering *what vibrates*) — the two systems ask two different questions.
