Ruan

China's round-bodied, straight-necked lute — a name it only received after "pipa" was taken from it

7 min readChinese PluckedUpdated 2026-09-26

instrumentstringpluckedChinesebayin

One thing about the ruan is unique in this batch: its name did not grow on it — it was taken over from somebody else.

⚠️ A name can only go to one branch — and "pipa" was taken. In the Han and Tang, "pipa" was a generic term for a family of held plucked instruments (as noted on the pipa's page). That family had two branches: | Form | What it came to be called | |---|---| | round body · straight neck | after the other branch took the name → "ruanxian", today the ruan | | pear-shaped body · bent neck | wildly popular in the Tang; it monopolised the name "pipa" | In the Tang the bent-necked, pear-shaped pipa arrived and took over the three characters 「琵琶」; so this straight-necked, round-bodied pipa had to use a person's name instead — Ruan Xian, the member of the third-century "Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove" who played it best. A name is a scarce resource. When one class of instruments divides, one side must change its surname. Set the batch's three naming stories side by side: ① a name can describe an action (the pipa: pi = forward pluck, pa = backward) ② a name can change places (the xiao and the di: "today's xiao is the di of antiquity") ③ a name can be taken away (the ruan: the pipa was seized, so it took "ruanxian")

Structure: a round box and a straight neck

Three points:

1. A round soundbox. This is the plainest difference from the pipa: one round, one pear-shaped — and that is exactly the mark by which the two branches divided. 2. A straight neck. No bent-back headstock as on the pipa. 3. Twenty-four frets in equal temperament. Frets are fixed bars crossing all four strings — so one string can produce many notes, the exact opposite of the zheng's one-bridge-one-note.

Bridge, fret, or neither

This figure closes the batch's survey of "how a pitch is taken from a string" — three instruments, three answers:

And the trade-offs can be totalled up: a bridge trades the decision about pitch for volume and speed; a fret keeps many notes per string but turns pitch into steps (slides then need string-bending); neither leaves pitch continuous and puts everything on the left hand.

In the band: the same role as the sheng

The ruan's tone is centred, full, mellow and poetic — and its place in a Chinese orchestra is very much like the sheng's among the winds:

It earns its seat through "blending power" and "easy chord-playing" — not the most attention-grabbing instrument, but the one that fills out the whole section.

This lines up with the "volume is social radius" thread: it is neither the loudest nor the most private — it is the middle one, the one that glues the band together. Modern ruans form a family by register (soprano, small, middle, large, bass), and the middle and large ruans are standard in Chinese orchestras.

Range

  • About G2–D5 on the middle ruan (indicative). It is an instrument divided by register: want lower, take a large ruan; higher, a small one — one instrument does not cover the whole compass.
  • It fills the "middle" slot. Chinese orchestras lacked a middle-low register, and that is the direct reason for the ruan's revival.
  • Besides the bass ruan there was once a bowed "la ruan" (modelled on the cello), little used because it did not work well — an attempt to build a bass instrument, and another probe at "what vibrates".

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Round, full, soft. Four strings across a round box make it rounder and less angular than the pipa. 2. It does not shout. Medium volume, ideally suited to filling in chords and thickening a section. 3. A fretted grain. Of the same family as the pipa: clear beads of sound, though darker. 4. It can play chords. Four strings and twenty-four frets let it sound several notes at once — a practical ability among Chinese plucked instruments.

Playing techniques

  • Seated, held to the chest. The body rests on the thighs and against the chest; the left hand stops strings and the right uses a plectrum (before the 1980s finger picks were standard; the higher string tension pushed players toward plectrums).
  • Over thirty right-hand techniques: plucking, picking, hooking, wiping, flicking, sweeping, rolling, tremolo and more.
  • Over ten left-hand techniques: harmonics, tapping, sliding, pushing, pulling, vibrato and others.
  • Slides by pushing. With frets, slides require bending the string to change its tension — the same method as the pipa.
  • No settled method yet. The ruan's revival is still young and teaching methods vary.

This family forks on two questions only: what shape is the body · is the pitch taken by a bridge, by a fret, or by fingers. The ruan answers "round + frets" — while its name reminds us of a third thing: classes divide, and after a division the names have to be handed out again.

History

PeriodState
Han (c. 2nd–1st c. BCE)records mention a held plucked instrument with a round body, straight neck, four strings and twelve frets, then called Qin pipa (origins have several legends, from Central Asian introduction to a princess's dowry musicians)
Wei–Jinnamed after Ruan Xian of the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove, who played it best; it becomes fashionable and a leading instrument for solo, ensemble and song accompaniment
Tangthe bent-necked pear-shaped pipa becomes hugely popular and monopolises the name "pipa" → this straight-necked round-bodied one is renamed "ruanxian"; it remains a principal instrument of the Qingshang and Xiliang repertoires
Songshortened to "ruan" (one account has a Song emperor adding a fifth string); afterwards ruan and yueqin remain two names for one instrument for centuries
Yuan–Qingin folk use, but by the Qing the old ruan's range shrinks to barely an octave, and folk versions degrade to two or three strings
since the 1950srebuilt as 4 strings, 24 frets, larger box and steel strings; a family modelled on the string family takes shape — soprano, small, middle, large, bass; ruan and yueqin now become two different instruments
1987Liu Xing's concerto Yunnan Memories establishes the ruan as a solo instrument

One historical fact stands out: all of the ruan's old scores are lost, and the modern instrument differs so much from the old that it has almost no traditional repertoire. Its repertoire is entirely modern. A two-thousand-year-old instrument with a brand-new repertoire — not an isolated case: the sheng's modern reform and the bianqing's replicas belong to the same category.

Common misconceptions

  • "The ruan is a young instrument." Its ancestor goes back to the Han (round body, straight neck, four strings, twelve frets); only the modern rebuilt form (4 strings, 24 frets, steel strings, a full family) is young.
  • "The ruan and the pipa are unrelated." They are deeply related: in the Han and Tang both belonged to the class called "pipa"; when the bent-necked branch took the name, the straight-necked branch became "ruanxian". They are siblings after a division.
  • "Its name comes from its shape." No — it comes from a person: Ruan Xian, who played it best.
  • "The ruan is just a sideman." It does carry the middle register and chords — which is exactly the sheng's role among the winds: the ones that earn their seat by blending are often the least dispensable.
  • "It has a rich traditional repertoire." The opposite: lost scores plus a rebuilt instrument mean almost no traditional pieces; the repertoire is contemporary.
  • "The bayin says silk, so only silk strings will do." As with the guqin: the bayin records the material of its own time; steel strings are now standard, louder and more stable.

Next

One plucked instrument remains: the sanxian — filling the last cell of the family's table: no bridges, no frets, and a small drum for a body. Its question is how few parts an instrument can have: three strings, one drum, and a neck with nothing on it — so how did it hold down the lead role in opera and narrative song for a thousand years?
