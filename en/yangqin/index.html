The yangqin is an instrument that was *edited twice* — and both edits left a paper trail.

⚠️ The first edit was the tool. The 1744 gazetteer of Macao, China records a "bronze-stringed zither" there, "struck with split bamboo, ringing and chiming" — and the sources state plainly that the book records the change of the striking tool "from the imported wooden hammer to the bamboo *qinzhu*." ⚠️ The second edit was the name. "Qiu Heshou's *Qinxue Xinbian* records changing the imported 'yangqin' (洋琴, foreign zither) to 'yangqin' (扬琴)." ⭐⭐ Rule ㊸: to be accepted, a foreign instrument must be edited twice — the tool first, the name second. Wooden hammer to bamboo (easier in the hand), 洋琴 to 扬琴 (easier on the ear). And this is the first instrument in the batch whose rename has a named author — compare the guanzi: its name was simply *said* into being; the yangqin's name was *changed* by someone.

Two notes on one string

⭐ Three points:

1. It is struck. Bamboo hammers (the *qinzhu*) hit the strings — the odd one out in this plucked section: strings, yes, but the sound comes from a blow (the sources call it "grain and ring" at once). 2. Two peak-and-valley bridges. "The peaks carry strings; the valleys let the other bridge's strings pass" — two rows interleaved into a pitch map; treble left, bass right. 3. ⭐ Two notes on one string: early yangqins placed a bridge at two-thirds of each string so it "sounded two notes a fifth apart" — the same cleverness as this site's two-pitch bell (rule ㉙): the bell splits two notes by its shape; the string splits two notes by its bridge position.

⭐ And "洋" was the era's general prefix for imported things — 洋片 · 洋火 · 洋琴 (this batch even has a song called 《拉洋片》, "peep-show slides"). The yangqin swapped its own 洋 out: same sound, different character. (Names-history item ⑳: homophone re-charactering, erasing the foreign feel — the twin of item ⑮: one changes the word, the other changes the character.)

"China's only world instrument"

The source's own words: "the yangqin is the only world instrument among Chinese instruments." It has two routes:

  • Westward: "originating in ancient Assyria in Mesopotamia," reaching Europe via Persia by the 15th century — where its kin is the dulcimer; in West Asia it is still the santur; in Hungary, the cimbalom.
  • Eastward: "about 1580, during the Wanli reign, entering Guangdong by the maritime Silk Road" (or, by another account, overland via the Pamirs) — and once landed, its first step was to be *edited*.

⚠️ Also recorded: some Chinese scholars hold a "Yangzhou origin" view; this page sets both accounts side by side without adjudicating.

Range

Where it works hardest: *qinshu* and opera

⭐ One sentence from the sources carries weight:

"At first the yangqin was used mostly to accompany narrative singing and local opera. Among these, every region's *qinshu* ('zither books') took the yangqin as its chief accompanying instrument."

— the very name of the *qinshu* genre comes from it. (Shandong qinshu, Sichuan qinshu, Henan qinshu, Xuzhou qinshu… the "qin" is the yangqin.)

⭐ So this batch's thread of "genres named after instruments" now has three examples:

Each wrote its most essential thing into its name — for one it was the beat, for another the accompaniment, for the third the instrument itself.

Its roster is crowded: Guangdong music · Jiangnan silk-and-bamboo · Yangzhou qingqu · Guangxi wenchang · Changde sixian · Sichuan / Henan / Shandong qinshu · Yulin xiaoqu — few instruments in this batch span so many genres.

Common misconceptions

  • "The yangqin is plucked." ⚠️ It is struck, with bamboo hammers — the odd one out in the plucked section, like the piano and the bells: strings or metal, but sound by blow.
  • "It is native Chinese." ⚠️ The mainstream view is imported (Mesopotamia–Persia line, entering Guangdong about 1580); a "Yangzhou origin" account coexists.
  • "It was always called yangqin." ⚠️ It was first 洋琴 (foreign zither), also *daqin*, *hudieqin*, *tongsqin*, *shanmianqin* — and Qiu Heshou's book records a rename: 洋琴 → 扬琴.
  • "It only accompanies." ⚠️ It has solos (*Spring Dawn at Green Lake*, *Thunder in Drought*, *Yi Dance*) and concert works; the 401 / 402 / 501 reforms carried it from "a sixth over an octave, badly out of tune" to free modulation and a wide range.
  • "A foreign instrument stays foreign." ⚠️ It had its tool, name, bridges and layouts all altered — "sinicisation" was not a gesture but a series of concrete technical edits.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Three cues:

1. Hear "grain *and* ring." Contradictory words, both true: a strike is a dot first, then a line. 2. Hear the "horizontal" habit. The sources say it favours "horizontal" melodic ornamentation — it embellishes the tune rather than owning it (exactly its role in qinshu and opera). 3. Hear the damping. Post-reform models added a damper — too much ring muddies; knowing when to stop is a skill.
