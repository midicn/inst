Maracas

Hollow gourds filled with seeds — the underlay of the Latin rhythm section

2 min readPercussionUpdated 2026-09-26

instrumentpercussionworld

Related

Two balls, one in each hand

A pair of maracas is two hollow shells filled with dry seeds, each on a handle. Shake them and the seeds rattle inside; the sound comes from shell and seeds together. Gourd, wood and plastic all change it, and so does grain size: coarse grains give a loose, rough shiver, fine grains a dense one.

The two have to match in volume and colour. Hand-made objects never match exactly, and that small difference widens the rhythm layer a little.

A surface, not points

Maracas have no definite pitch and offer no note, only a fine sheet of noise. The claves give points, the maracas give surface, and the two fill each other's gaps.

Amplitude and speed are separate variables: amplitude sets loudness, speed sets density. A struck instrument has one control for force, a shaken one has two, which is how a crescendo can happen without a break in the shake.

Wrist, not arm

The motion comes from turning the wrist. Swing the whole arm and you tire quickly and the sound coarsens. The hands need not do the same thing: one can shake steadily while the other adds weight on the strong beats. In folk music the maracas usually run alongside singing and dancing, where evenness matters far more than accuracy.

The underlay of a Latin groove

Take this layer away and the whole groove sounds dry. It competes with nothing, but remove it and the gaps between congas and bongos open up at once.

The history is longer than the sound suggests: gourd shakers were already used in ritual and dance in the Americas before Columbus, and only after the sixteenth century did they blend with African rhythm into what they are now.

To hear what it does, find a Latin groove and put your attention on the high shiver, then try to ignore it. If you cannot, it has been holding the thing up all along.
