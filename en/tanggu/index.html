Tanggu

The double-headed Chinese barrel drum — the first membranophone here, and the first instrument whose pitch does not come from any length

Skins at both ends

The body is a wooden barrel lying on its side, a cowhide stretched over each end. A bigger head gives a lower sound; skin tension can be set before the show; the striking point is chosen in the moment. Those three things are the whole of what players call its pitch. It never gives you a note, only a sense of height.

Patterns are orders

In opera the drummer uses patterns to start a song, close a phrase, change metre, push the tempo. He deletes pitch entirely and keeps only time and colour, and still runs the whole show. A bugle sends orders with a few pitches and rhythmic figures; the tanggu sends them with colour and density. Both work on the same dimension: time. Which is why the person who does the talking in the band sits behind a drum.

Centre, rim, damped

The centre is thick and low, near the rim tight and dry, and a hand pressed on the head cuts the sound short — the standard way to punctuate. Two beaters alternating make the roll that builds tension in luogu music. Let go and the tail rings; press and it stops, the exact opposite of a gong, which is hard to silence once it starts.

From the battlefield to the stage

Clay drums exist from the Neolithic; the Shang and Zhou used drums for sacrifice, war and feasting, and the Rites of Zhou files them under hide. When opera rose in the Song and Yuan, the drummer became the most senior player in the band. Armies signalled with it, stages directed with it, festivals built momentum with it — and all three used the same material, the rhythmic pattern.

Next time a percussion passage goes by, stop following the melody and watch where the beater lands. The breathing of the whole drama is in those two sticks.
