The plucked-string section opens with a name — and three instruments stand beneath it.

Wogonghou · shukonghou · fengshou konghou: one native (a small se-like zither, with frets), one that travelled all the way from Mesopotamia (the "barbarian konghou" Emperor Ling of Han loved), one crowned with a phoenix and bodied like a boat (from India by way of Central Asia). One name, three origins — and in the Sui–Tang court's nine ensembles, all three played at once.

⚠️ Its most astonishing fact comes from Qiemo, Xinjiang, 1996: before that year, nobody had ever seen a real konghou — it lived only in poems, murals and reliefs. The Zaghunluq cemetery yielded three (two about 2,500 years old). The researcher called them "a key… filling a blank in the history of Chinese music." ⭐⭐ Rule ㊵: what travelled was not the object but the method — Xinjiang has since produced 24 konghou, and the conclusion is that it spread eastward "not as objects, but made locally from local timber" — poplar for the body, tamarisk for the string-rod. What arrived was not a harp; it was a craft.

⭐ Its naming is equally lovely: "konghou" and "kanhou" — "because it is hollow inside it was called *kong*hou; because it sounds in clacking, marked beats it was also called *kan*hou." (The *Tongdian* adds a third explanation: the maker's surname *Hou* may be in the sound itself.)

"Nobody had ever seen a konghou"

⭐ The line worth carrying away is the researcher's conclusion about how it travelled:

"In spreading from west to east, the konghou did not travel as objects — locals made it themselves from local timber."

  • From Ur (real objects from about 2500 BCE) eastward through Mesopotamia, the Altai, the Pamirs and Hami, the structure stays "basically similar" — but the materials are local: the Qiemo harp's body is carved poplar, its string-rod tamarisk;
  • hence "localisation": "the poplar and tamarisk materials and some of the craft show local character" — "an embodiment of Chinese culture's inclusiveness."
  • ⭐ And one lovely conjecture: "the konghou is thought to have developed from the hunter's bow." Slacken a bow, and you have a harp.

What kept it alive for 1,400 years

In the Han and Tang it was a star — star enough that:

  • it entered folk poetry: *Southeast the Peacock Flies* praises its heroine with "thirteen, she wove; fourteen, she cut clothes; fifteen, she played the konghou; sixteen, she read the poems";
  • ⭐ it entered the most expensive poems: Li He's *Li Ping Plays the Konghou* begins "Wu silk and Shu paulownia, taut in high autumn; empty hills hold their clouds, unwilling to drift" — silk strings and paulownia body, right there in the first line;
  • it entered the murals and the tomb figurines: Kizil, Kumtura, Simsim, Subashi, Bezeklik; and among the eight Sui-dynasty musician figurines from Zhang Sheng's tomb, a woman plays the upright konghou alongside pipa, five-string, panpipes, flute, cymbals and a double-reed pipe.

Then it vanished.

  • "By the Song there were still konghou, small konghou and phoenix-head konghou. After the Ming it was used less and less, until it was lost."
  • "It peaked in the Tang, and fell out of fashion after the 14th century";
  • Ming court records preserved its specifications (four *chi* eight *cun* long, dragon head, 20 strings) — but that reads like the instrument in a file cabinet, not on a stage.

⭐ It left three living lines:

LineWhat
PoemsLi He, Gu Kuang, Wang Changling — in textbooks ever since. The name survived in verse.
ImagesMurals, figurines, paintings — the shape survived in pictures.
ObjectsQiemo, 1996 (three) · a Tang lacquered upright konghou fragment in the Shōsōin · 24 in Xinjiang — the wood finally matched the drawings.

⭐ Then three reconstruction stages: 1930s copies → 1959, Beijing Musical Instrument Research Institute → 1980, the modern konghou ("combining the Western harp, zheng, pipa and guqin"): double courses, a pear-shaped soundbox, bridges; the *Yanzhu* konghou stands 175 cm tall, 85 wide, with two rows of 44 strings. China's first konghou soloist, Cui Junzhi, put it plainly: "Very few people know the konghou — most mistake it for a harp."

⭐ And the loveliest homecoming happened where it was dug up: in 2015 the artist Lu Lu brought a rebuilt modern konghou back to Qiemo; in 2016 the county and her team founded Xinjiang's first public konghou school — "seed teachers" and "konghou children" — so local kids could learn the instrument that came out of their own soil. The words "homeland of the konghou" returned from an excavation report to children's hands.

Common misconceptions

  • "The konghou is a harp." ⚠️ The modern one borrows the harp's shape but has double courses, a pear-shaped box and bridges, and can bend, press and shimmer. "Mistaken for a harp," as its first soloist said — a mistake, not a definition.
  • "There is only one konghou." ⚠️ Three: flat (native), upright (Western Regions, 22 strings), phoenix-head (India via Central Asia) — all three in the Sui–Tang ensembles.
  • "It came from the Western Regions." ⚠️ Only the upright and phoenix-head ones did; the flat one is native, listed in Han times as "proper Chinese sound" in the Qing-shang repertoire.
  • "It died completely." ⚠️ Three lifelines (poems, images, objects) and a 1980 rebuild; Qiemo is still raising "konghou children" today.
  • "Instruments spread as objects." ⚠️ The konghou is the clearest counter-example: it travelled as a method, not an object — wherever it arrived, it was rebuilt from local wood (poplar, tamarisk).

Timbre, and how to hear it

Three cues:

1. Hear the "clear and bright." The sources' word for it: "clear-toned, softly cadenced" — silk strings are softer than metal, and closer to a voice. 2. Hear the "double." The modern konghou has two courses: corresponding strings in unison — one walks the melody, the other can bend and press — the biggest difference from a harp. 3. Hear the poem's version. Li He wrote "jade of Kunlun shattering, phoenixes crying" — ⚠️ but remember: he wrote it while the instrument still lived; for the next millennium, people could only imagine it by reading.
