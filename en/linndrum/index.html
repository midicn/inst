The LinnDrum

When machines began to sound like records — real drum samples, swing, and a musician who taught himself to code

In 1980 a drum machine with no drumheads sold for $5,000. The LM-1 had no oscillators, only a handful of recorded drums. Every machine before it imitated a drum; this one played the drum back.

A songwriter who learned to code

Roger Linn was not an engineer. He wrote songs, worked with players, and learned to code and build circuits because he needed the tool to exist. That decided what the product was: he treated it as an instrument for writing songs, not a replacement for a drummer. A tool does not have to beat a player; it has to make writing easier.

The money was borrowed — $20,000 from his father — and the circuit-board designer was paid with a Porsche he had owned for twelve years. Five hundred and twenty-five LM-1s were made, and only top producers could afford one.

Quantisation is a fault; swing is the patch

The LM-1 was programmed by playing, not by step entry. Linn found that the machine flattened everything he played onto sixteenth notes — the limit of its resolution, not of his timing. He added shuffle, which delays every other sixteenth slightly, and the delay is the feel.

Quantisation plus swing became the formula every later drum machine used. The shuffle knob on a 909 traces back here.

Fifteen outputs and swappable chips

The 1982 LinnDrum halved the price to $2,995. Its sounds sat in socketed EPROM chips, so a studio could buy and swap them; fifteen individual outputs gave each drum its own track at a time when machines had one. Eight-bit storage with mu-law encoding reached the equivalent of 12 to 13 bits of dynamics. It shipped without MIDI, added later by a third-party modification.

Thriller was cut that way: an LM-2 body, LM-1 snare and conga chips, and an 808 clap chip. Three rivals worked inside one machine. Prince used it from 1999 through Purple Rain; Kate Bush built Running Up That Hill on a LinnDrum pattern.

The death of missing MIDI

In February 1986 Linn Electronics closed. The Linn 9000 that was meant to follow was unreliable and drained the money; MIDI was standard on rival machines and the LinnDrum had none. The man who wrote the formula lost to the port he never fitted.

Linn moved to Akai and designed the MPC60 in 1988, where velocity-sensitive pads and the next thirty years of beat production began. In 2012 he built the Tempest with Dave Smith. In 2024 Behringer released a cheap LM Drum clone, and Linn said publicly that nobody had consulted him and he would rather have been asked.
