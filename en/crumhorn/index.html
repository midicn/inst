The crumhorn and the shawm are two forms of one double-reed principle: both use a double reed and a conical bore, both are keyless with holes under the fingers. They differ in exactly one thing — and that one thing remakes the instrument: the reed is sealed inside a wooden cap, so the player's lips never touch it.

⚠️ What a wind cap is A narrow slot is cut in the top of the cap. The player presses their mouth to that slot and blows into the cavity; the air inside sets the reed vibrating and the sound travels down the bore. It looks like a small modification. It has three large consequences: 1. Volume collapses — the lips can no longer press on the reed, and the cap limits both airflow and amplitude. 2. Dynamics become impossible — the lips are a double-reed player's only dynamic control, and here they are kept away; the instrument can barely play soft or loud. 3. The tone buzzes — the cap cavity is itself a small resonating chamber, adding a covered, nasal colour. In short: a wind cap turns an outdoor instrument into an indoor one.

So its other name is apt: it is a double-reed instrument that can only sing, never shout. Its existence shows that Renaissance wind ensembles were organised by volume — loud instruments on the outer parts, soft ones inside — and the crumhorn stands squarely on the soft side.

Structure: cap, folded bore, no keys

Three points:

1. The wind cap — the instrument's key part, and the reason its "horn" sounds covered. 2. The bore folds into a J. Down, then back up with the bell pointing upward: a physically short instrument with a much longer bore, hence a far lower pitch than its size suggests. The same idea is taken to its extreme in the racket, folded repeatedly into a column. 3. Keyless holes, so semitones come from forked fingerings, as on the shawm.

The wind-cap effect: one extra shell

Notice how small the difference is: the same reed, the same bore, and the only change is where the sound enters — from "lips driving the reed" to "lips merely feeding air into a small cavity".

The rule generalises: any extra cavity on an instrument is never just a shell. It changes volume, dynamic control and timbre at once. The pirouette (on the shawm) is the mild version; the wind cap is the extreme one.

Range

  • About a ninth (C4–D5 on the soprano size).
  • It cannot overblow: the cap limits the pressure in the bore, so a second octave is out of reach. To go lower you do not blow harder — you make the bore longer.
  • Made in consorts: soprano, alto, tenor and bass occupy separate registers, exactly as the recorder family does.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Buzzing, nasal, covered — cavity resonance from the cap is its fingerprint. 2. Quiet — an inner voice in an ensemble, never an outer one. 3. Almost no dynamic range. Its most counter-intuitive property: a phrase comes out at much the same volume however it is played. 4. Clean attack without a sharp edge. The tongue against the slot can stop the sound — the only articulation available, and the source of its characteristic brisk, even rhythm.

Playing techniques

  • Tongue the slot for articulation. The lips take no part in tone production, so the tongue is the only articulation control — which makes its tonguing even and slightly clicking.
  • No lip tuning. Double-reed players normally fine-tune with lip pressure; here that is impossible, so intonation rests on fingering and the maker's accuracy.
  • Circular breathing is very common: inner consort parts are often long held notes.
  • Consort playing is its main use; solo repertoire is comparatively small.
  • It must be emptied. Condensation collects in the cap and the player shakes it out — a practical detail of every wind-cap instrument.

Two questions sort out this whole family: ① who drives the reed (lips or cap)? ② how is the bore laid out (straight, folded, folded to a column)? The first decides whether dynamics are possible; the second decides how size relates to register.

History

PeriodState
Late Middle Agescapped double-reed instruments appear in Europe; one practical reason for the cap is protecting a fragile reed
14th–15th c.the crumhorn takes shape, made in consorts, an inner voice of court and town bands
15th–16th c.its heyday: combined with the shawm (outer voice), the racket (bass) and the recorder in an ensemble layered by volume
17th c.gradually displaced by newer, more expressive instruments
18th–19th c.survives mainly in pictures and documents, with a few folk forms
20th c.revived and newly made by the early-music movement, and still played and composed for

Its rise and fall tracks the idea of expressiveness. The Renaissance wanted layers of volume, hence an ensemble layered by volume. The Baroque and after wanted layers of dynamics and line, and an instrument that cannot play soft and loud was edged out of the centre. "No dynamic control" was no problem in the 16th century; by the 18th it was fatal.

Common misconceptions

  • "A crumhorn is a badly made shawm." The opposite: the cap is deliberate, partly to protect the reed. Its quiet, undynamic character is the price of the design, not a failure of craft.
  • "The tube is short, so it must be high." The J-fold is precisely so that a long bore fits a short body — which is why it can sound low.
  • "It is an oboe-type, so it can play soft and loud." It cannot vary its dynamics; that is the single most important thing to remember about it.
  • "The bent tube is what makes the odd tone." The colour comes from the cap cavity, not from the bend.
  • "The cap is decoration." Remove it and the instrument instantly becomes louder and dynamically controllable — it dictates the whole character.
  • "It is extinct." Since the 20th century the early-music movement has brought it back to the stage.

Next

The crumhorn and the shawm have shown the variable how the reed is driven. The next variable is how the bore is laid out — the racket folds a very long conical bore back and forth into a "column", producing a bass voice from the volume of a dictionary. Read together, they demolish the assumption that physical size determines register. :::
