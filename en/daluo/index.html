The daluo is the loudest of the Chinese gongs, and the first thing it gets right is not playing melodies.

It is a bronze disc with a boss: a slightly raised circular plate at the centre, and a sloped rim running down and outward from it. It has no definite pitch — and that is not a defect but a job description: what it needs is to carry, and carrying far and having a clear pitch are two different things.

⚠️ It asks the previous entry's question in reverse. The yunluo wanted melody, so it packed ten pitches onto one frame and sat at a table. The daluo wants distance, so it keeps one disc and gives up pitch — and that is why it can be carried at the waist, or struck from a city gate. | | Yunluo | Daluo | |---|---|---| | Face | flat, no boss | ⭐ bossed at the centre | | Pitch | definite (one note per gong) | ⭐ no definite pitch | | Sounds available from one piece | ten pitches | several timbres (by striking point and hand) | | Sit or walk | sits (seated ensemble) | ⭐ can be carried (procession · clearing the way) | Put those two rows together and one fact falls out: "bossed or not" and "pitched or not" are two independent axes. Structure: a bronze disc with a boss

Three points:

1. Three concentric zones, each with its own job. At the centre is the boss (also called *luoguang*; in Peking opera, *luomen* or *luoxin*) and it is the main sounding part; outside it is the face (also called the "second position"); outermost is the sloped rim. The boss's size and thickness, as a proportion of the face, decide how high or low the gong sits. 2. The rim is drilled for a cord — it is *carried*, not hung. A medium gong has leather or silk cords through two holes in the rim; the left hand holds the gong, the right hand strikes. The way it is held is itself evidence of its job: something you can carry is something you can use for clearing a road, marching, or a temple fair. 3. The body is "resounding copper", which is why a gong is also called a "resounding vessel". It is a bronze alloy, hammered over and over — a different route from the bianzhong, which is cast; the gong is beaten into tune.

One disc, three actions

This figure is the heart of the page.

On a single bronze disc, "where you strike" is already part of the playing:

  • The centre: sounds lower — low, thick, with a long tail.
  • The rim: sounds higher — thinner, with a sandy edge.
  • In between: striking between rim and centre gives yet another timbre and pitch.

And one more action: press a hand on the face right after the stroke — the sound stops dead (the techniques called muted and covered).

⭐ It buys several usable sounds by dividing up one bronze disc.

That is the exact opposite route from the yunluo:

RouteMethodExample
Copy outwardmake the same instrument ten times, one pitch eachYunluo (ten gongs, one scale)
⭐ Divide inwardone instrument, with different timbres across its surfaceDaluo (one disc, several sounds)

Both end up with "more sounds available" — one reaches outward, the other looks inward.

Volume is the job

The daluo's first post was not musical at all:

  • "Strike the gong and withdraw" — in the classical phrase *ming jin shou bing*, the "metal" that is struck is the gong. It was a military signal.
  • It still is, in a sense: a 32 cm flood-warning gong was measured carrying 800 metres in the open air.

But carrying far is not only about being loud: the suona carries far too, even further. The difference is what it carries. The suona carries a tune; the daluo carries a signal.

⭐ And a signal only needs a shape that is recognised instantly — it does not need pitch. So the daluo grew into the "distance" set of features: large, thick, bossed at the centre, and very long ringing. Giving up pitch is what bought it that entire social radius.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Low, loud, hard. It is not *a note* but a mass of sound — its partials are so dense that you hear no pitch, only a weight. 2. A long, lingering tail. After one stroke the sound hangs in the air — which is precisely why it carries so far. 3. Centre and edge are two characters. The centre is thick and low, the rim thin and higher; so when you hear a daluo, noticing which part was struck tells you more than listening for a pitch. 4. The hand changes the timbre too. Heavy strokes, light strokes, edge strokes, and *press-and-hold* (the muted stroke) — how long the hand stays on the face is how long the tail lasts.

Playing techniques

  • One hand holds the gong, one hand strikes. Leather or silk cords run through two holes in the rim; the left hand holds the cord so the face hangs vertical, while the right hand wields the beater — a beater whose head is wrapped in cloth or leather, which is what makes the sound round and full rather than sharp. ⭐ Holding rather than hanging is direct evidence of the job: only something carried can travel.
  • Six strokes: heavy · light · muted · covered · a thrusting stroke · an edge stroke.
  • In Peking opera it is graded: accompaniment mainly uses six gongs of different pitch — low, middle, upper-middle, large bright, small bright and high bright. If one instrument gives only one note, you use six instruments to get six characters.
  • It is one of the Peking-opera *wu-chang* trio (with the single-skin drum and the small gong). There it is in charge of scale: generals entering and leaving, battle scenes, a sudden turn of feeling — one stroke and the whole scene has changed.
  • Different genres and occasions use different gongs: the *suluo* (low, deep and lingering), *huyinluo*, *fang-suluo*, *fengluo* (high), *heiluo* (the "road-clearing gong"), and the huge *dashailuo*. Which gong you pick is itself a dynamic marking for the line that follows.

The line worth remembering on this page was written down for us by Europe: once the daluo travelled west, it became the only Chinese instrument in the symphony orchestra. And what it took there was not pitch — it was the weight of that one stroke.

Common misconceptions

  • "A daluo is the same thing as a Western tam-tam." Both the build and the use differ: the daluo is bossed, carried by hand, can be muted or covered, and takes part in drum-and-gong rhythmic patterns; the tam-tam is unbossed, hung, played with soft beaters, and normally given a single stroke. One is a rhythmic and signalling instrument; the other is a colour instrument.
  • "A boss means a pitch." ⚠️ No: the Chinese daluo is bossed and still has no definite pitch, while the yunluo is flat with no boss and does have pitch. "Bossed or not" and "pitched or not" are independent axes — what decides pitch is the whole geometry of diameter, curvature and thickness.
  • "It has no pitch, so it can do anything." The opposite: it can supply no pitch at all. It can only take part in rhythm and atmosphere, never in melody — which is exactly the problem the yunluo was built to solve.
  • "A daluo is just one big whack." It has six strokes — heavy, light, muted, covered, thrusting, edge — and Peking opera selects among six gongs of different pitch. "Where you hit, and how" together make up its entire "range".
  • "The gong is Chinese." More precisely: the gong originated in southern China and Southeast Asia (there are Western-Han Baiyue gongs in the ground to prove it), and there the "set of pitched gongs" became a melodic system — the gamelan. At home it took the signal-and-rhythm road. One ancestor, two roads — see Gong.
