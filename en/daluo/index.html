Daluo (great gong)

A bossed bronze disc that gives up pitch and buys distance instead

2 min readChinese PercussionUpdated 2026-09-26

instrumentpercussionidiophoneChinesebayinmetal

A general steps on stage, the gong sounds, and the house knows where to look. Melody was never its job. A bronze disc with a boss: a raised plate at the centre, a face sloping away, a rim drilled for a cord. No definite pitch — a job description, not a defect: it needs to carry, and carrying far is not sounding a clear note.

A bronze disc with a boss

The boss is the main sounding part; outside it the face, then the sloped rim. Boss size and thickness against face area decide how high the gong sits. Resounding copper, hammered into shape: the bianzhong is cast, the gong is beaten.

Where you strike is which timbre

One disc gives no pitch, but it does give several layers of colour.```svg

Low and thick at the centre, thinner and higher at the rim, a third colour between. Press a hand on the face and the sound stops dead: muted and covered.

The yunluo repeats one instrument ten times and reaches outward; the daluo divides one disc and reaches inward.

A boss does not mean a pitch: the yunluo is flat and pitched, the daluo bossed and unpitched.

## Eight hundred metres

Its first post was not musical. In the old command to strike the metal and withdraw, the metal is the gong. It still is: a 32 cm warning gong was measured carrying 800 metres in open air.

The suona carries far too, but it carries a tune. A signal needs only a shape recognised at once, so the gong grew large, thick, bossed and long-ringing.

Qin and Han bronze discs served military command; a Han Baiyue gong came from Luobowan. In 1791 Gossec introduced it to the orchestra.

## The hand that carries it

Cords through two holes in the rim: left hand holds, right hand wields a cloth-wrapped beater, which keeps the sound round. Holding rather than hanging is evidence of the job.

Six strokes: heavy, light, muted, covered, thrusting, edge. Peking opera uses six gongs of different pitch, since one instrument gives one note; one stroke changes a scene.

Which gong you pick is a dynamic marking for the next line. It is not the tam-tam: one is carried and muteable, the other hung and struck once.
