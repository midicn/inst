The qanun

Its name means rule — a ruler for measuring intervals

2 min readWorld InstrumentsUpdated 2026-10-21

instrumentworldmiddle-eastarabicpluckedzither

Greek kanon means rule, law. Arabic took the word as the name of an instrument — a zither built to be a ruler.

A ruler for measuring intervals

It was a theoretical object before a performing one. The name is no metaphor: it was conceived as an acoustic ruler for measuring intervals.

In the ninth century al-Farabi described it in the Great Book of Music. An instrument in a philosopher's book has its origin in interval theory, not repertoire. The oud's is the other kind: al-Kindi recorded its dimensions in fingers. One measures size, the other interval.

A line older than Greece

Its prehistory reaches further: a stringed instrument of the Old Assyrian Empire, nineteenth century BC, in Mesopotamia. The evidence is an engraving on an ivory box from the old capital Nimrud.

A nineteenth-century-BC instrument survives as a scratched line.

One brass tab moves one note

Under each course sits a small brass lever. Press it and it bears on the string, shortening the length and raising that note by about a quarter tone, at any moment. One qanun offers hundreds of combinations.

Rauf Yekta noticed the device in use on ordinary diatonic qanuns some thirty years before his monograph of 1922: the practice of correcting pitch came before the idea that it needed standardising.

The picks are zihaf, two tabs of horn or plastic ringed onto the index fingers, leaving the others free for the levers. One hand does two things: pluck, and move the pitch.

Theory grown into an object

In the twentieth century Turkish conservatories, under Arel and Ezgi, produced the AEU system, fixing tuning and lever configuration together — a theory and an instrument's setup bound into one thing. In 2007 Ozan Yarman applied a seventy-nine-tone scheme to a Turkish qanun: an instrument rewriting its hardware for a theory.

The body is a thin trapezoid, one long bridge on arching pillars padded with fish skin: five pads in the Arabic form, four in the Turkish, the extra leaving room for extreme strings. The decorated holes, kafes, are not ornament alone; they are part of the timbre.

Istanbul fused Arabic, Turkish, Byzantine and Persian practice into the form still played; in the fasıl, the Ottoman chamber suite, it sits at the centre.
