The human voice

A whole band inside the body — singing came before language

1 min read297 characters条目VoiceUpdated 2026-10-21

instrumentelectronicelectrophonewesternvoicesinging

The same greeting, said with a smile, welcomes you; said coldly, it shows you the door. The words are the same, the pitch the same; what changes is timbre and tone.

Lungs, folds, tract

The lungs supply airflow and pressure. The folds are the reed: air pushes them apart, they vibrate, and muscles stretch or slacken them to set pitch. Above the larynx, pharynx, mouth and nose filter; tongue, palate and lips shape vowels and consonants.

That division of labour is a subtractive synthesiser: pump, oscillator, filter, resonator. The synth was named in 1948; the body's version has run for tens of thousands of years.

Male folds measure 17 to 25 mm, female 12.5 to 17.5, three layers, able to shorten and bulge. Men usually have a longer tract too, which lowers the colour — that half owes nothing to the folds.

Two octaves

Choirs divide voices into soprano, alto, tenor and bass, with mezzo and baritone between, and male falsetto reaching the alto's range. A trained singer has about two octaves, and most Western song is written inside them.

Half that compass was spent by composers; the other half was given by the voice. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries adult male alto and soprano parts were recruited into the choir of St Peter's: an institution that wants a sound finds a way.

Timbre is written by culture

A tight nasal tone is accepted as beautiful in many West Asian, South Asian and Arabic traditions; opera and gospel singers want chest resonance and audible vibrato. Which counts as good is decided by convention, not by the physics of the folds.

The rise and fall of speech is already a narrow melody; singing only widens it.

It arrives with words

The voice is the only instrument that comes with words. Instrument entries are classified by form; vocal music by text — sacred or secular, according to what is sung.

The Diva Dance in The Fifth Element was written beyond human range: each note recorded separately and assembled in an editor, and Zhang Liangying later sang it live. Assembly first, live after — the road pitch correction would take.

Whether singing or speech came first, a hypothesis puts music before language, in the Middle Palaeolithic or earlier. It cannot be settled, but it suggests the instrument predates writing.
