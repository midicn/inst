Guqin

The seven-string fretless literati zither of China — three ways to sound one note, and thirteen studs that are simply the string's division points

The guqin is the heart of Chinese literati music and the natural entry point to the bayin system of classification. It puts two very different things on one instrument:

⚠️ First: two systems of classification meet here. The previous eighty-odd entries used the Hornbostel–Sachs scheme, whose test is "what vibrates". China had an earlier system — the bayin (metal, stone, earth, hide, silk, wood, gourd, bamboo) — whose test is "what material it is made of". The guqin's answer in the bayin is one word: silk. And that immediately raises a thoroughly modern question: the bayin sorted instruments by the materials available more than two thousand years ago — and most guqins today use steel-nylon strings. The material changed; the classification becomes an inherited name. This is not a flaw in the bayin, but the common fate of every material-based system: it describes the world as it was when it was invented.

Structure: seven strings, no frets, thirteen studs

Three points:

1. Fretless. This is the essential difference from the pipa: frets make pitch a set of steps; no frets make it a continuous line. So the guqin's left hand can slide up and down the string — one of its most recognisable idioms. 2. Thirteen studs. Not decoration but division points of the string — the next figure is entirely about this. 3. Seven strings, played flat, quiet. It was never designed for a concert hall: small sound and long, slack strings mean guqin music tends to move slowly.

One string, three ways to sound

This figure explains the guqin's most important technical trait:

1. The studs are physics, not decoration. Touch a division point and that stretch of string stops vibrating, leaving a harmonic. The thirteen studs are where they are because that is where the nodes are. (The same fact as the harmonic series — the guqin just marks it visibly.) 2. Three colours: open (whole string, full and deep) · stopped (left hand pressing, rounded and flexible) · harmonic (light touch, bright). Traditionally likened to heaven, humanity and earth: harmonics are heaven, stopped notes are the human voice, open strings are the earth. 3. One pitch, three characters. The same note can be produced on different strings, at different studs, in three ways — very rare among instruments: elsewhere it is one note, one colour; on the guqin it is one note with three personalities.

Range

  • About C2–D6, four octaves plus a major second — very wide for a seven-string fretless instrument.
  • The bass comes from open strings and the lower division. Seven open strings give seven notes from C2 up, so its bottom register is one string per note, the same logic as the harp's strings lengthening step by step.
  • Working register C3–D5, traditionally the middle division (studs four to seven), where the melodies live.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Quiet. By design: it is an instrument for oneself and a few friends, not for a hall. 2. Long decay and living slides. With no frets the left hand slides, carrying tiny pitch inflections — the hardest thing to imitate. 3. Bright, hollow harmonics. Whole sections played entirely in harmonics are not unusual (as in *Three Variations on the Plum Blossom*). 4. Three colours to tell apart. Deep (open) · rounded (stopped) · bright (harmonic) — distinguishing these three is the first thing to learn about the guqin.

Playing techniques

  • The right hand plucks, with nail or flesh and at varying angles, which is where much of the colour comes from.
  • The left hand lives between pressing and not pressing. Press and you get a stopped note (which can slide); touch a stud lightly and you get a harmonic — half the instrument's expression is in that left hand.
  • The music tends to be slow. Long, slack strings make fast intervallic runs on one string impractical, so guqin music favours unhurried lines and slides.
  • Notation records actions only. Since the mid-Tang tablature (jianzipu) records string, stud and finger but not rhythm — which is why the same piece differs greatly between schools. (Note the contrast with the mechanical instruments of the previous batch: those recorded actions with perfect precision and lost the intention; jianzipu gives only actions and leaves the intention to a teacher.)
  • Da pu. Because the notation omits rhythm, present-day players must re-create a playable text from old scores — a discipline with its own name.

Common misconceptions

  • "The guqin is the guzheng." Two entirely different instruments: string count, bridges, playing method and sound all differ. The shortest test: the guqin has no bridges; the guzheng has one per string.
  • "It is so quiet because it is badly made." It is deliberately built for private settings: literati played in a study or among hills and streams, and had no need to be loud.
  • "The thirteen studs are decoration." They are the string's division points, exactly the harmonic nodes — without them there is no way to place a harmonic.
  • "Jianzipu is a backward notation." It records actions and leaves rhythm to transmission — a deliberate choice, whose price is that one piece differs between schools (and which is precisely why schools exist).
  • "The bayin says silk, so only silk strings will do." The bayin sorts by material and describes the material of its own time. Steel-nylon strings are now normal — the name is inherited, and that does not diminish the historical value of the system.
  • "The guqin is solo only." There is a qinge tradition of singing while playing, and it often plays with the xiao; solo is merely the most common setting.
