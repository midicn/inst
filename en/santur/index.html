Santur

A Persian struck zither with a trapezoid box, two sets of nine bridges and seventy-two strings, played with two light hammers

2 min readStringsUpdated 2026-09-26

instrumentstringsworldstruck

Two light hammers, held like a pen between thumb and two fingers. The tip lands on a steel string and the note does not stop: the other three of its course, and distant unisons, ring too. One note sounds like a patch of sound.

The body is an isosceles trapezoid, about 90 cm across the player's side, narrowing to 35 cm, 6 to 10 cm deep. The taper makes bass strings longer without heavier wire.

Eighteen bridges, seventy-two strings

Seventy-two strings in eighteen courses of four; struck together they double volume and sustain. Bass courses are copper or bronze wound, upper ones steel. Two rosette openings are both acoustic holes and ornament.

The bridges, kharak, look like chessmen and slide on the board. Move one and the speaking length changes, and with it the pitch: apart from the pins this is the only tuning, and the reason quarter-tones exist.

One bridge, three segments

A course of four strings is not one pitch end to end. It crosses two bridges and is cut into three segments of different lengths, each a different note. You strike whichever segment you want.

Few strings, many positions: eighteen courses give twenty-seven, twenty-five pitches once the top repeats. Tuning means placing bridges as much as tuning strings.

Three registers

Bass E3–F4, middle E4–F5, treble E5–F6, each overlapping the next by more than an octave. A pitch is often in two registers, and moving a melody between them changes its weight. The lowest octave is incomplete: some modes cannot be played there, hence the twelve-bridge versions.

Long sustain is its clearest trait and main nuisance: fast passages smear. So santur music leans on ornament, repetition and tremolo, using density where others use staccato. The hands can also work both bridge sets and carry two lines.

The ancestor of a branch

The Hungarian cimbalom, the Chinese yangqin, the Indian santoor and the Western hammered dulcimer all descend from this line. In 1874 Schunda gave the cimbalom legs, a pedal and full chromaticism; the santur kept a box, strings, movable bridges.

Habib Hasan Touma argued that the instrument on Babylonian reliefs, held flat and struck, is the common ancestor of the harp, yangqin, harpsichord, qanun and cimbalom. The details are debated; what he got right is that flat strings being struck matters more than how many there are.

HS class

HS class: Chordophone · struck · box resonator, no neck or fingerboard · Bayin: not applicable, the eight categories are a Chinese system.
