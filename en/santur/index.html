Santur

A Persian struck zither with a trapezoid box, two sets of nine bridges and seventy-two strings, played with two light hammers

The santur is the oldest branch of the box zither family: a trapezoid wooden box with strings stretched over the soundboard and struck with two light hammers. Not plucked, not bowed — struck.

Its place in the family tree matters: the Middle Eastern santur, the Hungarian cimbalom, the Chinese yangqin, the Indian santoor and the Western hammered dulcimer are all descendants of this one line.

Structure: a trapezoid box, eighteen bridges, seventy-two strings

Four points:

1. An isosceles trapezoid. The wide side is about 90 cm (the player sits there), the narrow side about 35 cm, the box 6–10 cm deep. The taper is not decoration: it makes bass strings longer and treble strings shorter without changing gauges. 2. Eighteen bridges, in two sets of nine. The bridges (kharak) look like chessmen and can be slid along the soundboard — moving one changes the string's speaking length, and therefore its pitch. That is the santur's only tuning mechanism besides the pins, and the reason it can produce the quarter-tones of Persian music. 3. Seventy-two strings, four to a note. Four unison strings struck together roughly double volume and sustain — the same idea as the mandolin's courses, except struck instead of plucked. The bass courses are copper- or bronze-wound; the upper ones are steel. 4. Two rosette openings on the soundboard — acoustic openings and ornament at once.

How it sounds: one bridge makes three segments

This is the shared secret of every hammered-dulcimer family, and the most interesting thing about the santur.

A course of four strings is not one pitch from end to end: it crosses two bridges and is therefore divided into three segments, each with a different speaking length — so each segment is a different note. The player strikes whichever segment they want.

Hence few strings, many positions: 72 strings (18 courses) yield 27 positions (25 distinct pitches, since the top note repeats). It also explains why tuning a hammered dulcimer is a craft: you are not only tuning strings, you are placing bridges.

One layer up, the santur's sound has a feature few string instruments share: very long sustain, and sympathetic resonance in the strings around it. Strike one string and the other three in its course, plus every other string at the same pitch, start ringing — so the sound is thick and continuous, close to the way a harp layers up, except the attack is hammered, harder and more definite.

The cost: it is poor at short, sharply separated notes — the tail is too long and fast passages smear. So santur music leans on ornaments, repetition and tremolo (rapid alternation of the two hammers), using density where other instruments use staccato.

Range

The range divides into three registers, one per group of courses:

RegisterString materialRangeWhere
Basscopper / bronze woundE3–F4outside one set of bridges
MiddlesteelE4–F5centre
TreblesteelE5–F6outside the other set

The three registers overlap by more than an octave, so a given pitch is often available in two registers — which is exactly the instrument's tone-colour layering device: move a melody to another register and its weight and brightness change.

Note that the lowest octave is incomplete: bridge layout means the bass register only carries some degrees, and a given mode may not be playable down there. That is a real limit — and the reason 12-bridge variants exist: a slightly wider range, but more importantly the ability to hold the notes of several modes at once, so you do not have to retune to change tonic.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. A hammered attack. Steel strings hit by wooden hammers give a very definite onset with a metallic edge — between plucking (guitar) and striking (piano). 2. Enormous sustain with rich resonance. Strike one note and three more in its course plus distant unisons respond — a single note already sounds like a patch of sound. 3. Registers differ sharply. The bass courses are wound, thick and dark; the upper ones are steel, bright and fine. The same melody in another register sounds like another instrument. 4. Microtones. Persian classical music uses quarter-tones (koron down, sori up), and the santur obtains them by sliding its bridges. They sit between the piano's black and white keys, and are the most immediate way to recognise Persian music by ear.

Playing techniques

Everything turns on two light hammers:

  • Grip: the mezrab are feather-light and held between thumb, index and middle finger — closer to holding a pen than a drumstick. Tips may be bare wood, felt or cotton: harder tips for clarity, softer for atmosphere.
  • Alternating strokes: the two hands alternate on one note to make a tremolo — the santur's signature sound, used to sustain long notes (the same survival strategy as the mandolin's tremolo: one note will not hold, so repeat it).
  • Hands across registers: bass and treble sit outside the two bridge sets, so the two hands can work in different registers simultaneously, producing two voices. A santur cannot truly hold a chord, but it can build polyphonic lines.
  • Ornament: trills, grace notes, glissando-like runs — the core vocabulary of Persian classical music and the densest part of the repertoire.
  • No short notes: with such a tail, the player controls blur by damping and by switching registers, rather than by playing staccato.

The family

This branch spread very widely — the santur is effectively the ancestor of struck box zithers:

So "what is a santur?" has a clean answer: it is the closest thing in this family to the prototype — no legs, no pedal, no mechanism, just a box, strings and bridges, with one adjustable variable: where the bridges sit.

One name worth singling out: Habib Hasan Touma argued that this Babylonian instrument is the common ancestor of the harp, the Chinese yangqin, the harpsichord, the qanun, the cimbalom and the hammered dulcimers. The details are still debated, but the point stands: "strings lying flat and struck" matters far more than how many strings there are.

Common misconceptions

  • "The santur is plucked." It is struck. Two light hammers, not fingers — which is why its attack and sustain differ from a guitar's or a harp's.
  • "One course is one note." A course crossing two bridges is cut into three segments, each a different note. That is how 18 courses give 27 positions. The bridges are the core mechanism, not the strings.
  • "More strings means more range." With only 72 strings the santur covers over three octaves — because "one string, one note" does not hold here. Compare the harp: 47 strings give six and a half octaves by one note per string plus pedals. Two completely different trade-offs.
  • "It is uniquely Iranian." The same branch runs across Eurasia: the Chinese yangqin, the Hungarian cimbalom, the Indian santoor and the Western hammered dulcimer are all close relatives. It is the ancestor of the struck box-zither family.
  • "The bridges only support the strings." The kharak slide, and moving one changes the speaking length and therefore the pitch. Tuning means tuning strings and placing bridges.
  • "Microtones mean out of tune." Quarter-tones are a deliberate modal device (koron down, sori up), set by moving bridges. Heard with an equal-tempered ear they sound "off"; that is a different tuning system, not a fault.
