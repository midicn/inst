The archaeology and revival of the xun

The history of its finger holes is a fossil record of Chinese music — an instrument that blew itself for seven millennia

In 1979 Chen Zhong handed a player an eight-hole clay xun. The last person who knew how to play one had been gone for centuries.

The hole count is the timeline

Archaeologists read xun holes like tree rings. The Hemudu piece has no finger holes and sounds one note. Banpo added one, measured as a minor third. Two-hole specimens from Jingcun and Yijing gave three notes. The fish-shaped three-hole xun from Huoshaogou, found in more than twenty ordinary graves, gave four framework notes. Late Shang pieces have five holes; three from Fu Hao's tomb play a full seven-note scale. Han instruments have six or seven. One hole to five took three thousand years; five to eight took a few decades.

Clay remembers intervals, not pitch

Pitch measurement finds no common standard. Every xun sits at its own absolute height; what stays stable is the distance between holes. Makers had no notion of absolute pitch, only of relations, and the minor third at Banpo has been called the oldest known scale form. Jingcun gives three notes, Huoshaogou four, late Shang five holes and seven notes. That staircase came from potsherds, not texts, and it runs beside the bianzhong on another track.

Two ends of a range

The court kept two sizes. The ya xun was large, deep and low; the song xun was small as an egg and sat higher. They occupy the ends, and the middle was filled by changing instruments, not fingerings. Excavation gives not a continuous scale but a series of unrelated pitches. Only the modern eleven-hole xun joins those dots into a line.

Losing the line, and finding it

The Book of Rites calls the xun the sound of early autumn, flat-based, six holes, the number of water. A clay timbre was written into a book and bound to a season.

By the Qing the line had snapped. Wu Xunyuan copied a Shang five-hole pear-shaped xun and left the Tanghu Xunpu. In the late 1970s Lü Ji, Huang Xiangpeng, Cao Zheng and Chen Zhong measured excavated pieces and relearned the craft. Chen's playable eight-hole xun came in 1979; by the late 1980s Zhang Ronghua was moulding resin-sand-clay instruments sold in sets. To hear it now, start with Zhang Weiliang's 1997 Wentin. To hear what it first was, look at a clay egg in a case that cannot change its note.
