Triangle

A steel rod bent into a triangle — the brightest, most focused idiophone

1 min read29 characters条目PercussionUpdated 2026-09-26

The whole band is playing, and the ping is still there

At the last bar of a rehearsal the conductor stops everyone but the triangle. A steel rod bent into a triangle, one corner left open, hangs from a frame on a string; a small metal beater touches it and the sound crosses the orchestra. It is the smallest thing on stage and one of the most penetrating.

Hanging it is harder than hitting it

It must hang on string. A metal hook carries the vibration away and the tail dies at once, the tone turning dull; a wooden frame is kinder than a metal one for the same reason. The open corner matters too: a closed triangle cannot flex freely. The beater is a small metal rod; a drumstick will sound it, but takes away part of the high end. Many rehearsal rooms hang it off a music stand, and half the sound is lost there.

Small does not mean quiet

Its energy sits in a few high frequencies, which makes it cleaner and sharper than a cymbal and much harder to mask. The volume is routinely underestimated; in a full tutti one stroke floats on top. An arranger who judges it by size will write too many.

Open and closed are two instruments

Cup it in the hand and the ring disappears, leaving a tick; let it hang and the sound runs on. Strike the inside near the opening for the brightest tone, the top corner for the driest, roll the beater around the frame for a continuous shimmer. It entered European bands in the eighteenth century with Turkish military music; Mozart and Haydn already used it, and it moved from exotic colour to standard tone.
