Pipa

China's pear-shaped four-string lute — a name that describes a playing action, not an instrument

Related

Three different systems of naming and classification sit on one instrument — a good illustration of this whole batch:

⚠️ Three logics, one instrument | Name / system | Its basis | The pipa's answer | |---|---|---| | the name "pipa" | a playing action | "pi" is a forward pluck, "pa" a backward one — per the Shiming, the two characters began as two strokes | | the bayin | what it is made of | silk (丝) | | HS classification | what vibrates | chordophone | So action, material and vibrating body all land on the same instrument. And in the Han–Tang period "pipa" was a generic term for a whole family of held plucked instruments (straight or bent-necked, four or five strings); only later did it settle on this one. A name describes an action, a material system describes materials, a scientific taxonomy describes physics — which is exactly why the same objects produce such different maps.

Structure: a pear-shaped body and a ladder of frets

Three points:

1. The 相 and frets are one deliberate design. The 相 sit on the neck (large blocks) and the frets on the soundboard (thin bars); together they cut the strings into fixed lengths, so pitch becomes a set of steps — the exact opposite of the continuous pitch of the guqin and erhu. 2. Four strings, four colours. Traditionally finely distinguished: the first bright, the second mellow, the third full, the fourth deep — so the same melody changes character on a different string. 3. It is a distant relative of the lute. Both descend from one branch of ancient short-necked, held plucked instruments, and even the bent-back pegbox is a shared piece of technology.

Fret density is the freedom to change key

This figure is the technical core of the page, turning "key change" into something countable:

  • The traditional twelve-fret layout produces two "neutral tones" at the seventh and eleventh frets (a slightly raised 4 and a slightly raised 7 — not on the equal-tempered scale). The result: a traditional pipa could only play in D, and could not freely change key.
  • Liu Tianhua added one fret between the sixth and seventh, breaking the neutral positions and bringing the layout fully onto equal temperament; it was later extended to six 相 and twenty-four frets, taking the range to nearly four octaves.
  • The rule to take away: the number of frets directly sets how far an instrument can travel — between "one key only" and "any key" lie nothing but a few extra slats of wood.

*(This is the other half of the erhu story: Liu Tianhua reformed both instruments — fixing the erhu's tuning and positions, and filling in the pipa's semitone frets. One goal: let Chinese music play together and change key.)*

Range

  • About A2–E6, nearly four octaves — and that width comes from the frets, not the string count. Four strings cover four octaves because each string can be stopped very high.
  • Tuning A2 · D3 · E3 · A3 (thickest to thinnest): the bass is leapy (A2 to D3 is a fourth, D3 to E3 only a tone), so its low register is a set of points rather than an even spread — a little like the hunting horn's, though here the cause is string tuning, not physics.
  • The bass lives on the fourth string, melodies usually on the first and second — which is what "four strings, four colours" is for.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Extremely grainy. Every note is a distinct bead: the pipa's notes start fast and decay fast, so it excels at points. 2. But it can also make lines. Lunzhi — the five fingers rolling in turn on one string — joins the beads into a line. This is its signature technique and the moment it stops sounding like a plucked instrument. 3. Four strings, four colours: first bright, second mellow, third full, fourth deep. 4. Two faces, civil and martial. Its repertoire divides into civil suites (delicate, as in *Flute and Drum at Sunset*) and martial suites (fierce, as in Ambush from All Sides).

Playing techniques

  • The right hand is the protagonist. Pipa right-hand technique is unusually rich: pi, tiao, gou, mo, ti, gun, lun, yao, zhi, fen, sao, fu — such a fine division of labour on one plucked instrument is its hallmark.
  • Lunzhi turns points into lines with five fingers rolling on one string — a technique that only became possible once softer silk strings arrived (see below).
  • The left hand adds vibrato, pushes, pulls, slides, crossing strings and harmonics. Frets do not forbid slides — the left hand can push a string sideways to change its tension.
  • Finger picks. Modern players wear picks to get a bright, crisp, hard-edged tone.
  • Held upright, resting between the knees, which frees the left hand to travel over the body — a posture settled only after the Ming.

A comparison axis you can use directly: whether it has frets decides whether pitch is a set of steps or a line — and that divides this family more tellingly than "what material it is made of". Which is precisely the division of labour between the two systems: the bayin handles "what it is made of", HS handles "how it sounds" — while a player cares most about "how you get the pitch".

History

PeriodState
Qin–Han"pipa" names a whole family of held plucked instruments (the straight-necked, round-bodied ones later became the ruan); the name comes from two strokes
Wei–Jin–North/South dynastiesthe bent-necked pipa arrives from Central Asia (the Kucha region) and becomes dominant; played horizontally with a plectrum
Sui–Tanga golden age: played at court and in the streets; the Tang Dunhuang pipa score shows a mature solo repertoire; five-string pipas also flourished
Song–Yuana shift toward upright holding and finger plucking
Ming–Qingthe form settles; silk replaces animal-gut strings — softer and more elastic, which makes finger plucking possible and calls forth the lunzhi; schools form (Wuxi, Pinghu, Pudong, Chongming, Shanghai)
1818–1819Hua Qiuping and others publish the Pipa Score — the first printed pipa collection in Chinese history
1920s–30sLiu Tianhua argues for laying out the frets in equal temperament: one added fret between the sixth and seventh removes the neutral tones → later standardised at six 相 and twenty-four frets, range extended to nearly four octaves; the pipa can now change key freely
mid-20th c. to todaystrings change from silk to nylon-wound steel; the layout extends further (25, 26, 30 frets); many new works

Its technical history has a clear causal chain: material (silk strings) → technique (finger plucking) → language (lunzhi: points become lines) → and back to material (steel strings, brighter tone). Change the string and you change the technique; change the technique and you change the language.

Common misconceptions

  • "The pipa has always looked like this." Its ancestor was a family of instruments; horizontal to upright, plectrum to fingers — all of that happened over several centuries.
  • "Frets rule out slides." They do not — the left hand can bend the string to change its tension, a fundamental technique.
  • "More strings means a wider range." The pipa has only four strings yet spans nearly four octaves — width comes from the frets, not the strings.
  • "Changing key is a given." Before Liu Tianhua a traditional pipa could play in D only, because the neutral tones at frets seven and eleven shut the other keys out.
  • "Lunzhi is just fast repetition." Its point is turning points into a line — giving a grainy instrument the ability to sustain.
  • "The bayin says silk, so steel strings are wrong." As with the guqin and erhu: the bayin describes the material of its own time.

Next

The silk category closes here: guqin (fretless, plucked) · erhu (fretless, bowed) · pipa (fretted, plucked) — three instruments that between them exhaust the ways of getting a pitch out of a string.

Next we step out of silk into bamboo: the dizi changes the whole discussion — no strings, no frets, no stopping, only the air column inside a bamboo tube and a few holes opened by fingers. The same job — changing the speaking length of the vibrator — is done completely differently in air than in string.
