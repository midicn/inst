Double Bass

The largest and lowest of the string family — sloped shoulders, fourths tuning, and written an octave above sounding pitch

6 min readStringsUpdated 2026-11-02

instrumentstringswestern

The double bass is the last and largest member of the string family: a body around 1,070 mm, a string length around 1,050 mm — played standing up or from a high stool.

It is also the family's odd one out. Every other violin-family instrument follows one set of rules: symmetrical round shoulders, fifths tuning, and notation at sounding pitch. The double bass breaks all three:

Structure: three departures from the family

1. Basses have different body shapes, including sloping and rounded shoulders; they are not generally made with only the left shoulder cut away. 2. Geared tuning machines make fine adjustment of heavy strings practical and are common on modern basses. 3. Fingerboard, string and body dimensions vary by model. The ratio of string length to body length does not measure fingerboard length.

Why fourths tuning

This is the most frequently asked question about the instrument, and the answer is not musical. It is about hands.

The family's string lengths differ enormously: violin 328 mm, cello 690 mm, double bass 1,050 mm. String length decides one thing above all — how far a hand must travel for a given interval. Working from the string-length-to-frequency relationship, a whole tone (two semitones) lands at:

Starting from an open string, raising pitch by a whole tone in equal temperament shortens the vibrating length by about 10.9%. For lengths of 328, 690 and 1,050 mm, the stopping distances are about 36, 75 and 115 mm. These are distances from the nut to the whole-tone stopping point, not the minimum spacing between adjacent fingers. The same interval takes less distance higher up the fingerboard.

  • Standard fourths tuning E1–A1–D2–G2 helps organise fingering and string crossing on long strings.
  • Lower positions often use 1–2–4, with the third finger supporting the fourth. The third finger is not discarded throughout the instrument; higher and thumb positions use other patterns.

So a bass player's left hand is not really "stopping notes" — it is shifting constantly. That slightly gliding continuity in fast bass lines comes from exactly this.

Range

A little over three octaves. Note the two rows on the chart: the double bass is the only member of the family notated as a transposing instrument — an E2 on the page sounds as E1. The reason is practical: writing sounding pitch in bass clef would push a great many notes below the staff, beyond any sensible number of ledger lines.

The standard four-string low E1 is about 41.2 Hz and has a definite pitch. Five-string instruments or extensions can reach C1 or B0. Upper partials also help listeners identify a low note, so the whole 20–40 Hz region cannot be treated as sensation without pitch.

The clef is bass clef; high passages switch to treble clef, with the same octave relationship to sounding pitch.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Recognising a double bass works differently from the rest of the family:

1. Low notes have both weight and recognisable pitch; upper partials help the ear identify the lowest notes. 2. The D and G strings and higher positions serve melodic and solo writing with a different colour from the low accompaniment register. 3. Pizzicato has a clear attack and decay, while bowing can sustain a level and connect long lines. Jazz and orchestral writing can use either as required.

Playing techniques

The right hand has two bow grips, unique in the family:

  • French bow (overhand): palm down, structurally like the cello's French bow.
  • German bow (underhand): palm up, cradling the frog — the older tradition inherited from the bass viol.

The two schools are complete systems (weight distribution, wrist action, spiccato all differ), and both survive among professional players today.

The left hand follows the previous section: 1-2-4 fingering plus constant shifting. The rest of the technique is shared with the family, with a few things that need separate work on this instrument:

  • Pizzicato: thick strings and high tension mean the whole hand, not just a finger. In jazz it is a solo language — walking lines, double stops, slaps — not decoration.
  • Harmonics: the longer the string, the further apart its nodes, and the easier they are to hear. A double bass's natural harmonics are clearer than a violin's.
  • Bow hair tension: thick strings need more pressure, but too much chokes the string and kills the harmonics. Controlling that line is the hardest part of the right hand.
  • Hold and endpin: most basses use an endpin, but some players — especially in jazz and folk — brace the instrument against the body to pluck it, and both the posture and the sound change.

The family

The double bass’s development involves both viol and violin traditions rather than one simple descent from the bass viol. Modern fourths tuning, shoulder shapes, backs and bow types should be described separately. Historically, violone did not always name one particular instrument.

History

Since the sixteenth century, European low bowed instruments have used varied sizes, string counts, tunings and names, gradually leading to the modern double bass. This was not a single replacement that preserved one earlier instrument’s complete set of rules.

Eighteenth-century basses usually had three strings (A1, D2, G2). The fourth string (E1) came later and only then gave the instrument the depth an orchestral bass part needs. Dragonetti is the figure of that period: his technique and his works made the double bass a plausible solo instrument in the late 18th century.

In the 19th century Bottesini did for the double bass what the great virtuosi did for the violin — called "the Paganini of the double bass", he pushed solo technique to its limit and left concertos and character pieces. The solo tradition is essentially this line.

In the 20th century the instrument split across two stages: the orchestral bass part (bowed, long notes, foundation) and the jazz bass (plucked, walking, improvising). One instrument, two almost unrelated techniques — also a first in this family.

Common misconceptions

  • The double bass is not simply an enlarged cello: its historical forms, usual fourths tuning and transposing notation differ.
  • Pitch depends jointly on vibrating length, tension and mass per unit length: f = (1/2L)√(T/μ). Gauge and tension are not merely secondary adjustments.
  • Fourths tuning helps organise fingering. Lateral distance between strings comes from string spacing, not from the interval between open strings.
  • 1–2–4 is a common lower-position pattern; shifting, thumb position and other fingerings extend its reach.
