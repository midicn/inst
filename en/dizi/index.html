The previous three entries all belonged to silk — guqin, erhu, pipa, all questions about strings. The dizi takes a step out: it has no strings at all.

⚠️ The same job, done completely differently in string and in air Strings and wind instruments are in fact solving one problem: making the *speaking length* of the vibrator shorter or longer, and so changing the pitch. | | String (guqin, pipa) | Air (dizi) | |---|---|---| | Changing the speaking length | press a finger down (cut the string short) | lift a finger off (open a hole) | | Getting the high notes | touch a node → harmonic | overblow → excite a higher mode | | The hidden logic | where the finger stops, the string "starts" | which hole is open, the air column "ends" | Note the second row: the string's *harmonic* and the pipe's *overblown note* are two incarnations of one physical principle — both say "do not excite the lowest mode; make it vibrate in a higher one". The guqin's thirteen studs and the dizi's overblowing are talking about the same thing.

Structure: one bamboo tube, seven small holes

Three points:

1. The membrane hole is what makes it unique. A thin membrane from inside a reed stalk is pasted over the hole; the air stream sets it vibrating too, brightening the tone and adding a buzz. This is the single most important difference from a Western flute. 2. No keys. Six holes opened and closed directly by the fingers — which is why the spacing is even (easy to make), at the cost of intonation, compensated by forked fingerings and embouchure. The same problem as any keyless woodwind, such as the shawm. 3. Transverse. The air strikes the edge of the blow hole (edge tone) rather than passing a duct — a different drive from the recorder's whistle mouthpiece, and the reason it sounds "breathier".

One reed membrane is what separates it from the flute

This "extra part" logic has now appeared three times on this site:

The rule to take away: an added vibrating part changes timbre and expressiveness but never an instrument's class — because the HS test remains "what vibrates", and these parts are coupled into the sound, not the primary vibrator. Which is why "what vibrates" is more reliable than "what it looks like".

The number of holes is the freedom to change key

This is the most important parallel rule in this batch:

"Can it change key?" is decided, in both media, by the same quantity: how many positions it can change its length to. That is a rule you can carry away from the instruments themselves.

Range

  • About two and a half octaves. The low register comes from opening and closing the finger holes; the top is reached mainly by overblowing.
  • It is a *family*, not one instrument: made in C, D, E, F, G, A and B; by use it divides into qudi (accompanies Kun opera; long, thick tube, mellow) and bangdi (accompanies bangzi opera; short, thin tube, bright and piercing), plus a lower xindi (da di).
  • One flute, several keys by fingering — how many, though, depends on whether the fingering system can cover them (see the previous section).

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Bright, clear, with a buzz on top. That buzz is the membrane vibrating. 2. Very breathy. With edge-tone drive, every note carries the sound of the air itself — it sounds "airier" than a duct flute. 3. Two regional characters. The southern school (qudi) is elegant and clear; the northern (bangdi) is firm and rough. 4. Membrane tension changes pitch. Looser in summer, tighter in winter — the player's way of offsetting the effect of temperature on pitch (and something to manage whenever it plays with others).

Playing techniques

  • Embouchure is everything. On the same flute, lip position and air angle produce changes in pitch, colour and dynamics — the most "alive" part of the instrument.
  • Forked fingerings. With evenly spaced holes, semitones and key changes are covered by cross-fingerings (opening a middle hole while covering the outer ones).
  • Overblowing. The top register is reached by pressure, exciting higher modes — the same principle as touching a node to get a string harmonic.
  • Circular breathing is common in long phrases, especially among folk players.
  • The membrane must be fitted by the player — its age and tension adjusted, a routine before every session.

This family forks in two places: ① what drives the air (edge tone / duct / reed); ② whether there are keys or added parts. The dizi occupies the plainest corner: one person, one tube, a few holes and a piece of membrane.

History

PeriodState
c. 9,000 years agobone flutes from Jiahu, Wuyang, Henan: made from crane wing bones, with several finger holes and able to sound multiple notes — among the earliest playable instruments known
pre-Qinin the texts, "di" mostly means an end-blown pipe (not today's transverse flute); the name "di" has denoted more than one instrument
Hanthe transverse flute arrives from Central Asia and spreads (traditionally linked to Zhang Qian's mission westward), blending with native pipes
Tangthe transverse flute is well established, but has no membrane — at that time only the xiao bore one
Song–Yuanthe membrane moves to the flute and the xiao stops using it — the modern dizi takes shape
Ming–Qingit becomes the mainstay of opera accompaniment: the qudi for Kun opera, the bangdi for bangzi opera; southern and northern styles separate
20th c.layout improvements: seven-, nine- and ten-hole dizi, more keys and semitones; a large solo repertoire appears (*New Shepherd's Song*, *Urging the Horse to Carry Grain*, *A Stroll in Suzhou*)

One detail in its history is revealing: the name "di" has pointed at different instruments over two millennia. The pre-Qin "di" was end-blown (the later xiao); today's transverse flute is the product of an imported transverse pipe blending with native ones — and the membrane only moved onto it after the Song. An instrument's identity is often much younger than its name — the same problem as the pipa's "name that describes an action".

Common misconceptions

  • "A dizi is the Chinese version of a flute." Outwardly similar, but two key differences: bamboo, keyless and membraned versus metal, fully keyed and membraneless. The membrane alone is enough to hear the difference.
  • "It is a membranophone, because it has a membrane." No. The test is "what vibrates": the air column (aerophone). The membrane is an added part coupled into the sound, not a change of class.
  • "More holes must be better." More holes buy key freedom at the cost of more complex fingering and less even tone and volume. The traditional six-hole dizi remains the mainstream.
  • "The dizi is simply out of tune." Its holes are evenly spaced, so it genuinely needs forked fingerings and embouchure to compensate — a division of labour between structure and player, not bad craft.
  • "It has always looked like this." End-blown became transverse, bone became bamboo, and the membrane only moved onto it after the Song.
  • "The bayin says bamboo, so only bamboo will do." The same as the guqin's silk: the bayin describes the material of its own time. And the bone flute of nine thousand years ago is not in the bayin at all — the bayin is one era's, one region's classification of materials, not an exhaustive list.

Next

Silk and bamboo are both done: in the bayin they are two neighbouring categories, and each stands for a whole set of ways of changing a vibrator's length.

The last category turns to metal: the bianzhong — which is neither pressed by a hand nor blown by a mouth, but has its pitches cast into the bells themselves. Once an instrument can only sound "as built", its pitch has left the player's hands entirely. :::
