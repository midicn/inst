The DX7 and FM synthesis

No instrument owned a decade so completely — digital won, presets ruled

Ten minutes

FM synthesis modulates the frequency of one sine wave with another, and the spectrum that comes out is far wider than what went in. Chowning worked it out at Stanford, the university patented it and looked for a licensee.

Hammond and Wurlitzer passed; Yamaha did not. One technology, unintelligible on one side and understood in ten minutes on the other. The ones who understood were not cleverer; they were already standing in that world.

From fifty chips to two

The first commercial FM synthesizer was the GS1 of 1980, expensive because of chips: one instrument used fifty integrated circuits.

By the time the DX7 appeared in 1983 the same synthesis needed two. At $1,995, with sixteen-voice polyphony and a touch-sensitive keyboard, it sold more than two hundred thousand units. Democratisation here did not mean a price cut; it meant the machine got smaller.

The sound is computed

Analog polysynths were warm and slightly smeared. The DX7 was harsh, glassy and cold — brighter and richer. It excelled at electric pianos, bells and plucked sounds, because FM can compute the complex transient of an attack.

Subtractive synthesis does sustain, with a filter sweeping slowly; FM does the moment. The architecture differs too: six sine operators modulate one another through thirty-two algorithms, each a wiring diagram. The spectrum is computed, not filtered.

The decade of presets

FM programming was famously miserable: membrane buttons and a two-line display, and with that many parameters almost nobody programmed their own. The world ran on presets.

The most famous was E.PIANO 1, an electric piano that effectively replaced the Rhodes on record. In 1986 it was used on forty percent of America's number-one singles — one factory preset, forty percent of the top of the chart.

The DX7's unprogrammability later became the reason it dated, and the reason analog synths returned with one knob per function. Three lessons fit together: digital can win on character; presets are not cheating but distribution; interfaces decide history. A chip derived from its engine, the YM2612, went into the Sega Genesis. Eno programmed his own sounds and treated FM as a language; Enya used the Tubular Bells preset and treated it as furniture. Both were true on the same instrument.
