Cymbals

Bronze idiophones struck in pairs or hung singly — an extremely rich, noise-like spectrum

One sheet, one band of noise

A cymbal does not give high frequencies one at a time; it spreads them. The snare also lives up there, but what vibrates is completely different: a stretched head in one case, a whole sheet of bronze alloy in the other. That is the line between membranophone and idiophone, and between a hiss and a crackle. Its overtones never form a harmonic series, so there is no pitch to hear, only a band of noise filling the top.

One sheet as a colour map

Near the bell the sound is dry and controlled; at the edge it is open with a very long tail. Position is colour. Craft decides the rest: the copper-tin ratio, the pattern of hammering and the lathe grooves together fix the final sound, so brands differ, and so do batches within a brand. Hammering leaves uneven stress in the metal and lathing cuts the grooves; both are tuning the same sheet.

Three ways to stop it

Same physics in all three forms, different damping. A pair is stopped by hand, a suspended cymbal almost cannot be stopped, a hi-hat is closed by the pedal. Because the stopping differs, the jobs differ: the pair marks accents, the suspended one makes space, the hi-hat is the clock of a rhythm section. Two plates brushed together hiss; slammed together they burst, and the difference is contact area and speed rather than force.

The little pitch that size gives

Unpitched. Size and weight only give a relative sense of height: a hi-hat is usually 14 inches, tight and short; a crash is medium-small and explosive; a ride is often 20 to 22 inches, clear, with a ping definite enough for jazz drummers to build a continuous line on it. China has its own nao and bo, similar in form and independent in origin. Resemblance is not descent. Turkish military music carried cymbals into European bands, and the jazz kit systematised them into hi-hat, crash and ride, each with a single job.
