The dap is the only drum on this site that hangs entirely in the hands.

No handle, no cord, no stand — the drum rests on the webbing of two hands alone. The Qing court record describes it with a phrase worth keeping: "shaped like a hand drum, but without a handle."

It has no beater either — everything a beater would do belongs to the hands: where to strike (centre · edge · rim), how to strike (finger · palm · palm-heel · "cuo" · "tan" · roll). And inside the frame hangs a ring of small iron rings: shake it, and there is another sound.

It gives the fullest answer yet to the question left by the pengling:

⚠️ When pitch is not needed at all, how many sounds can one skin hold? The traditional technique gives six named strokes: centre tone · edge tone · palm tone · finger tone · "cuo" tone · "tan" tone. Line the six names up and something shows (an observation of this site) — they come from three different dimensions: named by position (centre · edge) · by hand part (palm · fingers) · by motion (cuo · tan). ⭐⭐ Rule ㉗: pitched instruments arrange their notes in a line; an unpitched instrument arranges its timbres as a map — and the coordinates on this map have names. Named things can be taught, learned, and grown into schools — which is why an instrument "without pitch" can still be a solo instrument.

Structure: one skin, one frame, and a ring of iron hidden inside

Three points:

1. One skin and one frame. The frame is turned from mulberry wood into a flattened round hoop (regional woods differ: Dolan daps glue up elm or Russian olive), with a single skin — from sheep, pony or donkey hide to the python skin that became common after the 1950s, said to be the best-sounding choice. ⚠️ It is single-headed: the back is open, unlike a two-headed barrel drum such as the tanggu — every sound it makes leaves through this one skin. 2. It has no handle. "Shaped like a hand drum, without a handle" — no handle means nothing ties it to the player's body. The nearest exception is an Uzbek cord loop (the right thumb tied to the frame) — already the most "attached" this family ever gets. 3. A ring of iron hangs inside the frame. Small hooks are driven into the inner wall, holding some thirty small iron rings. They take no part in the skin's vibration — they are a sound reserved for shaking. ⭐ Compare the tambourine: to add a metal layer to a frame drum, the tambourine sets little cymbals into slots in the hoop; the dap hangs rings inside it.

A map on one skin

This figure is the heart of the page.

Back to the opening question: how many sounds does one skin hold? The sources are strikingly plain: "because the fingers strike different parts of the head with different force, the sounds vary — dong and da — with a thousand changes of colour and enormous expressive range."

In other words — the sense of high and low comes from *place and force*, not from a scale. A museum record puts it even more precisely: it "produces different pitches and colours according to the part and the force".

And the six traditional techniques are precisely the "place names" of these variables:

NameWhat the name records (this site's observation)
centre toneposition — struck at the middle of the head

⭐ Two further practices extend the map:

  • Rolling on the rim: the dap placed between the two legs, both hands rolling on the rim, giving a sound "like a bell" — at that moment it is a flat table, not a drum held in the air.
  • Shaking the frame: no skin involved at all — the iron rings against the frame.

⚠️ The boundary of the sources: how "cuo" and "tan" are actually played, the sources do not explain — they left only the names. (As with any "the bigger the X, the lower the Y" claim: no source, no detail supplied on its behalf.)

⭐ And the fact that the strokes have names is itself worth recording: a skin's positions have no note names — but they do have place names. Named things can be taught, learned, recorded — which is why an instrument "without pitch" can hold solo pieces (Harvest) and grow regional styles and schools.

"Soul" and lead

For its role in the band the sources reach for three heavy words: "soul", "conductor", "lead".

  • It is "hailed as the soul of the Uyghur band"; also called "the player of souls."
  • "It unifies rhythm and tempo — a veritable conductor."
  • "It is constantly used as a leading instrument, balancing high, middle and low, unifying the rhythm."

⭐ And one folk saying that beats every adjective:

"The dap can cover what the other instruments lack." — it carries everyone.

Its way into the great suites is equally concrete: it is the main time-keeping instrument of the Uyghur muqam, "almost indispensable" there. And it does not merely accompany: there are solo pieces such as *Harvest*, and famous players such as Abliz Akhsh.

⭐ Set beside this batch's bangu, two ways of "conducting" appear: the bangu works by a radius of attention (an effective sounding area of 5–10 cm commanding a whole opera troupe — it gives orders); the dap works by rhythm and tempo themselves (it sets the pace and carries the rest). Neither works by volume; both work by position.

Three sizes, three posts

The sources offer two slightly different sets of measurements, but the division of duties is identical:

SizeWho uses itPost
Small (about 20–28 cm; also called nägmän dap)professional folk singers · muqam ensembles⭐ beating time for the Twelve Muqam and meshrep
Medium (about 32–40 cm)farmers, townspeople, the stagedance accompaniment · ensemble · showpiece solos
Large (about 44–52 cm)⭐ the folk shaman, the bakshi⭐ ritual — "driving out spirits", healing (Tajiks also use it for dance tunes)

⭐ This site's observation: for the same three sizes, the elephant-foot drum divides by tone (large = majestic, medium = excited, small = bright); the dap divides by post — one instrument grows different sizes because it serves different social roles.

And carried across a mountain, it keeps changing:

  • Dolan dap (Bachu, Makit): many drums together, played in unison, "no free improvisation" — a school that puts individual display away; it accompanies Dolan sanam and Turpan's milis.
  • The Tajik dap: a wide frame (often cut from an old poplar sieve), wolf or cow hide; no iron rings, sometimes three pairs of small cymbals instead; a deep, thick sound. ⭐ And the players differ too: "the Tajik dap is played almost entirely by women" — the reverse of the Uyghur and Uzbek rule that it is usually played by men. (Rule ㉒ said an instrument divides along "whose hands work it" — the dap gives the plainest case yet: cross a mountain, and the player's gender changes.)
  • The Uzbek dap: form, making and playing similar to the Uyghur (held in the left hand, struck with the right).

⭐ And a final piece of evidence for how central it is: it can accompany a dance alone — and the dance is named after it. "Dap ussuli", that is, "the hand-drum dance." (Rule ㉑ said what a genre is named for shows what it considers essential — here the version is: a dance simply took the drum's name.)

⭐ History of names, item ⑩: a name can travel the Silk Road with its instrument. The nine entries before this one were all about how an instrument gets named at home; the dap adds the cross-cultural one: when the instrument travels, the name travels with it. (And the muqam it accompanies is distributed across 19 countries and regions of Central, South and West Asia and North Africa, with Xinjiang at the far east — one name and one music sharing the same line on the map (this site's observation).)

⚠️ And a wink inside the legend: flaying the python to make drums — the legend happens to explain why the dap "sounds best with python skin" and why python became "widely used after the 1950s." Legend and craft meet here.

⚠️ One more naming trap to clear up: in Chinese writing the word "shougu" (hand drum) is not used consistently — this site's tambourine page already flags "hand drum" as a misnomer; and the Uyghurs also have a smaller jingled hand drum (called "nägmän dap", "the hand drum for singing", closer in form to a Western tambourine). This page is about the most typical one: no cymbals, no handle, iron rings inside the frame. (Amusingly, the dap and the tambourine share one cell in Hornbostel–Sachs — 211.311, single-skin frame drums, whose given example is the tambourine — two answers in the same cell.)

⭐ Two details in the Qing record are worth pausing on:

  • "shaped like a hand drum, without a handle" — by the Qing, "hand drum" had become a *shape description*: an instrument defined as "like a hand drum, but handleless."
  • "painted with coloured lions, lacquered yellow" — the court dap was a painted ceremonial object; the same instrument is a sheepskin and rings in the village and painted regalia in the palace.

⚠️ The conversion is a modern approximation: the Qing measure (1 *chi* 3 *cun* 6 *fen* 5 *li*) comes to about 44 cm at 1 *chi* ≈ 32 cm — matching the modern large size.

Common misconceptions

  • "The hand drum *is* the tambourine." ⚠️ No. They are of one family — both single-skin frame drums (H-S 211.311, whose example is the tambourine) — but the dap has no handle, hangs iron rings inside, and is played by two bare hands; the tambourine mounts jingles, has a grip, and its hallmark is the thumb roll. Two answers in one cell. (This site's tambourine page already flags "hand drum" as a misnomer.)
  • "No pitch, so nothing to play." ⚠️ The reverse: solo pieces such as *Harvest* exist, the techniques have six formal names, and regional styles and schools have formed. No pitch is not no content — the content moved elsewhere: position, hand, rings, rhythm.
  • "The rings are decoration." They are a second voice: without touching the skin, shaking the frame sets them against it; the Tajik variant swaps them for three pairs of small cymbals. Compare the tambourine's jingles in slots — three ways of adding a metal layer to a frame drum.
  • "It belongs to the Uyghurs alone." The Uzbeks and Tajiks share it (Uzbek playing is much the same; the Tajik form differs and is played almost entirely by women), and it has been taken into Kazakh, Kyrgyz and Tatar bands.
  • "Is it pitched?" No definite pitch. But note what that means here: not "it cannot produce definite sounds" but "there is no unified pitch standard" — different parts and forces change the sense of height (dong, da). (The bronze drum page has already set out this wording.)
  • "It must be slung on the body to be played." ⚠️ The reverse — no handle, no strap: the drum hangs in the hands. This completes the batch's "carrying" line in three ways: carried (the luo on its cord, the cymbals struck while walking, the elephant-foot drum slung) · held (the pengling, one goblet in each hand) · ⭐ simply cradled (the dap).

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Bright. "A crisp, ringing tone," with "a wide range of dynamics" — it can work the space between whisper and thunder (which is why it is called the soul). 2. Two layers, one source. The skin (struck: dong / da) and the rings (shaken: a jingle) belong to one instrument — a single motion can fire both, and a shake can fire the second alone. 3. The skin *is* the tone design. "Python skin sounds best"; a python head is "bright and ringing." Change the skin, change the instrument. 4. Material follows the place. From mulberry, rosewood and apricot to Dolan glued elm or Russian olive, to the Tajik poplar sieve frame — a folk instrument has no "standard material", only "the best material at hand."

The percussion line closes here — fifteen instruments.

The materials have come full circle: bronze (bianzhong · bronze drum) · stone (bianqing) · hide (tanggu · bangu · paigu · elephant-foot drum · the dap) · metal (daluo · xiaoluo · yunluo · cymbals · pengling) · wood (wooden fish · bangzi).

And the last instrument in this class is the plainest thing on the whole site: no skin, no metal, no resonator — only a few slats of wood. Yet the entire rhythmic system of Chinese opera is named after it:

the "board" of "board and eye" — it is the paiban, the clappers. :::
