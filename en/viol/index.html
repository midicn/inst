Viol

A fretted six-string bowed instrument played with an underhand grip — the chamber instrument of the Renaissance

It was the cello's colleague for two hundred years

In a sixteenth-century English hall a chest of viols stood ready: one treble, one tenor, two basses, played together after supper. The cello's ancestors were being rebuilt in Italian workshops at the same time. The viol is not the cello's ancestor; the two ran in parallel for two centuries, and the cello won on volume.

Frets you can move

Seven gut frets are tied around the neck and can be pushed up or down. Fixed metal frets only give equal temperament; movable ones let a player place the tuning where the music needs it, and Renaissance music is most consonant in meantone. Seven frets make one chromatic octave on the top string, so the whole compass from D2 to A4 follows from six strings and seven frets. The tuning runs fourth, fourth, third, fourth, fourth, the same layout as the lute, which is why the two repertoires circulated together.

Palm up, and therefore quiet

The bow is held palm upward, the stick curving away from the hand, the fingers on the hair. Tension can change while playing, so a pianissimo can be extremely fine, but the ceiling is low. When concert halls grew at the end of the eighteenth century, a low-tension design could not fill them. That is physics, not a judgement on the players.

One line, not a whole compass

Viol music is written by part. In a consort the instrument sings one independent line; frets let the left hand stop chords with reliable intonation, and the writing sits closer to the lute than to the cello. Vibrato was an occasional ornament, not a continuous polish, and the early-music revival restored that restraint. The Viola da Gamba Society was founded in 1948, and the 1991 film Tous les matins du monde brought the sound back to a wide audience. Listen for how four lines make room for each other, not for cello weight.
