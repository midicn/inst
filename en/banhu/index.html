Banhu

The erhu's cousin, with its skin resonator replaced by a wooden board — a board that ended up in its name

Related

The difference between the banhu and the erhu is the easiest — and the most startling — in this batch: same family, same bowing, same two strings, with one material on the resonator swapped out.

Structure: the erhu's frame, a different face

Three points:

1. No skin on the front — a thin paulownia board is glued on. This is the only essential difference: bow, strings, stopping and the hair threaded between the strings are all the erhu's. 2. A waist nut instead of a qianjin. The erhu uses a qianjin; the banhu uses a waist nut, of horn or rosewood — a different name and material for the same job. 3. A thicker, longer bow. Thicker stick, more and tighter hair, longer than the instrument itself — room for "fiery" playing.

Skin, or board?

This figure is the heart of the page: two faces, two timbres, two jobs.

  • Skin (a membrane): soft vibration, heavy damping → a tone close to the human voice → lyrical, singing lines (the erhu).
  • Board (wood): harder vibration, more high frequency → a high, bright tone → "high, fierce, hot, fiery" emotions (the banhu).

And that one material difference fixed its social post: the banshu leads bangzi opera — Hebei bangzi, Qin opera, Jin opera, Pu opera, Henan opera, pingju, Lü opera, Shandong bangzi… the high, intense singing of bangzi music needs exactly a high, intense voice.

(Qin opera shows it twice over: its original lead was the erguxian*, and both have paulownia board faces — the difference was string material (gut versus silk). Same board, different strings, timbre changes, and the lead chair changes hands. In one story, "material decides the post" happens twice.)*

Range

  • A full octave above the erhu — one reason it sounds so sharp (the high register is its home ground).
  • Working range about two octaves and a fourth (high banhu).
  • Tuning follows the genre and the singer. In the same genre, a different voice means a different tuning — it is an instrument built to serve a sung line, so it must be able to follow a person (the same reasoning behind the sanxian's fretless neck).

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Loud, crisp and bright. The easiest way to tell it from the erhu. 2. High with a "blast". A wooden board is harder than skin, with more high frequency and very strong penetration. 3. It can also sing softly. It does not only shout: the low register is deep and full, the top fierce and high — two faces. 4. A rustic flavour. Its colour is unmistakably folk — which is also why it never entered the regular roster (see below).

Playing techniques

  • The basics are the erhu's: stopping and shifting with the left hand, bowing with the right.
  • Left-hand finger-glides and pressing are especially characteristic — the inflections of operatic singing depend on them.
  • Right-hand staccato, spiccato, tremolo and double-string playing are all distinctive.
  • The bow is longer than the instrument, which is what allows large, fast strokes — in the service of "fire".
  • Shifting positions is a story in itself: the Qin-opera banhu began with a single position, and when the range ran out players used "borrowed notes" (dropping an octave for high notes, raising one for low notes) — only in the 1930s did it reach two positions, and later players extended it to a third.

A counter-intuitive rule hides in this table: instruments with too much personality do not enter the regular roster — the erhu family blends with itself, while the banhu "does not easily blend with erhu-family instruments" and is therefore used only as a special section. It is left out not because it is worse, but because it is too much itself. The exact opposite of the ruan: the ruan earns its place by blending; the banhu earns its place by refusing to.

Its history in one sentence: it was built by a way of singing — not an instrument in search of a use, but a high, fierce kind of singing that needed a high, fierce voice.

Common misconceptions

  • "A banhu is just another name for an erhu." Two instruments: the erhu is skinned with python, the banhu boarded with paulownia — one word's difference, and both timbre and post follow.
  • "Being part of the huqin family, it must be a membranophone." No: the strings vibrate (chordophone); python skin and paulownia board are both only resonators. Changing the resonator's material does not change the class — further proof that "what vibrates" beats "what it looks like".
  • "It only accompanies opera." Since the 1950s it has been a solo instrument, with works such as *Da Qi Ban* and Qin Qiang Pai Zi Qu.
  • "A louder instrument must be more welcome." The opposite: because its colour has "too much personality", it is not in the Chinese orchestra's standard roster and serves only as a special section.
  • "Being higher, it must have a narrower range." It is an octave above the erhu, with a range of about two octaves and a fourth — and the high register is precisely its home ground.
  • "The bayin says silk, so only silk strings will do." As with the guqin: the bayin records the material of its own time; in Qin opera's history, "the same board with different strings" once changed who led the band.

Next

The bowed family now has two here: the erhu (python skin) and the banhu (paulownia board). The next instrument, the jinghu, takes it to the limit: a smaller body, skinned with snake, an extremely bright and penetrating tone — it is the "voice" of Peking opera, and it asks: how can an instrument reduced to almost a single point hold up an entire national theatre?
