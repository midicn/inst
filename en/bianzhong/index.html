Bianzhong

Bronze bells whose pitches are cast into the metal — the player holds no pitch, only the choice of when to strike which bell

Pitch poured into bronze

When the Zeng Hou Yi bells came out of the ground in 1978 they were still hanging on their frame, and still in tune. Bronze cast 2,400 years ago lines up with today's C major. The fundamental difference from almost every other instrument is that the player holds no pitch at all.

A string player stops a string, a wind player opens a hole; a bell's pitch is fixed when the bronze is poured. Afterwards the only way to change it is to grind the inside wall, which is a founder's method, not a player's. The player stops being a maker of pitch and becomes a chooser of it: all he decides is when to strike which bell.

Shaft, crown, studs, faces

Strike the centre and one note sounds; strike near the edge and another does. Two pitches per bell. The studs on the body are not decoration; they take part in how the bell vibrates. And the set hangs in pitch order, which is what bian, arranged, means.

Why the shape gives a second note

The almond section is not a circle but two arcs meeting at two edges. Strike the centre or the side and the body enters two different modes of vibration, so it gives two notes. Cast it round, as a Western bell is, and it gives one note wherever you hit it. This is not luck; it is a design the founders of the pre-Qin period made on purpose.

A scale cast into bronze

How wide a set reaches was settled at the foundry. The Zeng Hou Yi set has 65 bells in three tiers and eight groups, spans five and a half octaves, carries all twelve semitones and can change key. Its bells, frame and hooks hold 3,755 gold-inlaid characters recording numbers, pitch labels and the correspondence of regional names. Playing takes a team: two inside with long poles for the low tier, three outside with two hammers each. It belongs with the stone chimes in court ritual.

Deep in the bass, round in the middle, bright at the top, with a long ring that cannot be wiped away, so thickness comes from overlapping notes. The two pitches of one bell can be used as a chord. A timpani can be retuned in performance; a bell cannot, and must be cast right the first time.
