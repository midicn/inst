Prophet-5

The synthesizer learned to remember — forty patch slots and a Z80 microprocessor

1 min read375 characters条目ElectronicUpdated 2026-10-04

Dialling a sound by hand made every gig a gamble: yesterday's patch could not be wound back into today's knobs. The Prophet-5 stored the positions.

Forty slots

Before it, a synthesizer's sound existed only in the present of its knobs. Smith's answer was a Zilog Z80 that scanned every parameter, stored it, and called it back at a button press — not rebuilt, recalled.

That sounds like convenience. It changed the instrument's place: it could tour, enter a studio, change hands. In the phrasing of the time, it moved from making unpredictable sounds to offering a standard package of familiar ones.

A score remembers notes; a patch remembers timbre. For the first time an instrument had a memory of its own.

The voice is analog, the control is digital

The voices stayed subtractive: oscillators, filter, amplifier, all analog. Only the control changed — scanned, stored, recalled by number. The first true hybrid instrument.

The tonewheel organ was a hybrid too: mechanical wheels read by magnets. Every such splice founds a new category.

Ten voices burned down to five

What they built first was the Prophet-10. It was unstable, overheated quickly, and the tuning drifted. They removed half the electronics, cut the voices to five, and the Prophet-5 existed.

Three revisions, three tempers. Rev1 was hand-built, only a hundred and eighty-two, in a koa case; Rev2 added cassette storage and a walnut case; Rev3 moved to Curtis CEM chips, some six thousand built, still good to play but a little colder and less characterful. Which sounds best is argued to this day.

Hidden inside was Poly-Mod: an oscillator and the filter envelope modulating another's frequency and pulse width — analog FM. Two years later Yamaha's real FM reset the industry.

From Thriller to Kid A

It is on Thriller, Like a Virgin, In the Air Tonight; Kraftwerk toured with it in 1981 and Radiohead's Kid A still has it. Peter Gabriel called his an old warhorse.

Sequential went bankrupt in 1987. Smith went to Yamaha, founded Dave Smith Instruments in 2002, bought the Sequential name back in 2015, and issued a faithful Rev4 in 2020. He died in 2022, leaving the programmable polysynth and MIDI behind.

Eighteen years, two musical worlds, one instrument. To hear it at its most itself, start with a five-voice pad.
