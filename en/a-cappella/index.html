A cappella singing

No instruments — voices alone become the whole band; "a cappella" was born of a misunderstanding

2 min readVoiceUpdated 2026-10-21

instrumentelectronicelectrophonewesternvoicechoir

In the nineteenth century an editor opened a Renaissance choirbook, found no instrument named anywhere in it, and concluded that this was how the chapel sang: a cappella, in the manner of the chapel. Half of that conclusion was wrong.

Those parts were often doubled by instruments. Even in the Sistine Chapel, some of Palestrina's early pieces were meant to have an organ doubling some or all of the voices. The revivalists did not know, and read a style term — Renaissance polyphony as against Baroque concertato — as a list of equipment: no instruments allowed. There were more misreaders than readers, so the world sang the misreading for a hundred and fifty years.

Three jobs, no spare hands

Without instruments, melody, harmony and rhythm all have to grow out of throats. A lead carries the tune, the inner voices lay down the harmony, and the beat goes to vocal percussion — lips, tongue and throat doing the work of a kit.

Intonation is the hard gate. With instruments, pitch is handed to you from outside; without them, every note has to be taken from the singer's own body. Barbershop quartets space the harmony so tightly that overtones surface on their own, and that ring is the ear checking the tuning. A pitch with no reference has to be grown, not tuned.

Only breath allowed

Orthodox liturgical music still uses no instruments. Bishop Kallistos Ware wrote that in the Orthodox Church singing is unaccompanied and instrumental music is not found. The rule goes back to a strict reading of Psalm 150: let every thing that hath breath praise the Lord.

Only the throat has breath. One reading of one verse turned instruments into a violation, and the ban held for a thousand years, longer than any change in musical taste.

The valley is older than the chapel

The spirituals of the American South had no instruments, and could not afford any. Then came barbershop quartets, doo-wop's nonsense syllables and vocal bass, gospel quartets, the Whiffenpoofs in 1909, the Swingle Singers turning Baroque instrumental music into voices, and now Pentatonix. Each step was a subtraction: first the instruments, then the band, at last the conductor and the score.

China's unaccompanied singing is older still. Work songs with a leader and a chorus, mountain songs answered across two hillsides, a mother's lullaby sung with nothing but breath — all of it predates the Sistine polyphony. Dong grand song has no conductor, no instruments and several parts at once, and the people who sing it have never heard the word a cappella.

They do not need the word, and they do not need the instruments. The whole band is kept in the throat.
