The plucked section's last instrument happens to hold a mirror up to the struck one — because their shapes are nearly identical.

The kalong's box: "a hollow flat trapezoid, curved on the left, straight on the right, like half a yangqin." And the Qing statutes describe it: "the karnai, eighteen steel strings, shaped like a common yangqin."

⭐⭐ Rule ㊿: the same trapezoid, two answers — one retunes, the other rebuilds. The kalong and the yangqin are both trapezoid, many-stringed — but:

  • the kalong is plucked by finger or plectrum (with a metal slider for ornament in the left hand);
  • the yangqin is struck with bamboo hammers.

    • ⭐ And they solve the problem of key differently: the yangqin rebuilds the instrument (half-tone devices, the 401 and 501 models); the kalong retunes the strings, piece by piece.

The one with the most strings

⭐ Three points:

1. It has the most strings. "The most stringed of the old Uyghur plucked instruments" — sixteen to eighteen courses, two strings each (other accounts give 17–22 pairs, or 30 / 23 / 34 strings; listed side by side). 2. The left hand is this page's hero. "The left hand holds a metal slider and presses or shifts it, producing ornaments" — the right hand speaks; the left hand supplies the tone of voice. 3. Its shape entered someone else's definition. The Qing statutes call it "shaped like a common yangqin" — ⚠️ and the truer relation is: alike, but not the same (see the figure below).

The slider in the left hand: a bird's call

⭐ The loveliest detail here is the name of that little tool:

"The slider is called koxitap in Uyghur, meaning 'birdsong'; it is said to have been created by folk players inspired by birds, first made of sheep's leg bone, later of metal."

  • Its name describes not a shape but a sound ("birdsong");
  • Its origin is an invention made by *listening*: the bird was heard first, the tool made second;
  • Its material travelled too: bone → metal.

⭐ Compare the zhamunie's "opinion-style name" (㊾): one puts the verdict on the instrument, the other on a small tool in the hand.

It retunes itself, every time

⭐ The yangqin chose to rebuild the instrument for free modulation. The kalong's answer is entirely different:

"The kalong is often tuned to the modal scale of the piece being played: for a piece with one flat, b is tuned to ♭b; for a piece with one sharp, f is tuned to ♯f."

It does not change the instrument; it changes what the strings are tuned to that day. — the cheapest possible solution to the hardest problem: if hands can retune, leave the structure alone.

"The soul of Dolan muqam"

Its post is written in the names of the southernmost counties — Mekit, Bachu, Awat:

  • "indispensable to the meshrep performance of the *Twelve Muqam*, and the chief accompanying instrument for Dolan and Hami muqam — especially in the free-rhythm (sanban) sections, where its flavour shows most";
  • hence "the soul of Dolan muqam";
  • its partners are the satar, bibi, one-string, Dolan erhu and Dolan rawap — a whole Dolan band;
  • and it is not only local: "the kalong is a world instrument, used in Turkey, the Arab countries, Uzbekistan and elsewhere."

⭐ Two old names worth recording:

Old nameNote
Seventy-two-string pipapossibly what it was called when it reached the interior with the Mongol campaigns (recorded in the *Yuan Shi* and Liu Yu's *Xishi Ji*)

⚠️ And one origin account: "the kalong was created by the famous Uyghur scholar and musician al-Farabi (c. 870–905)… in his *Book of Music* it is called 'mizaf,' and then it had forty-five strings" — a record that does not match today's sixteen to eighteen courses: another instrument whose documents and body disagree (compare the dutar and tanbur; names-history item ㉒).

Range

Common misconceptions

  • "The kalong is just a yangqin." ⚠️ Same shape (trapezoid, many strings), but the kalong is plucked (plectrum plus slider) and the yangqin is struck (bamboo hammers). Even their key solutions differ: retune versus rebuild.
  • "Most strings means thickest sound." ⚠️ Its tone is "clear, bright, chiming," and it "suits slower pieces" — here many strings buy ornament and a complete scale, not weight.
  • "It only accompanies." ⚠️ It solos, ensembles and accompanies — sometimes the player sings along.
  • "It has died out." ⚠️ It still lives in the Dolan muqam country; "Uyghur kalong making technique" entered the first regional intangible-heritage list in 2007, with a named bearer teaching it.
  • "The name is Chinese." ⚠️ Both "kalong" and the older "karnai" are transliterations; the sources even say "the name originally came from Greek," travelling east through the Arab world.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Three cues:

1. Hear the "chime." The sources' words: "clear, bright, chiming" — and "close to the guzheng, but brighter." 2. Hear the slider. Press and shift, and ornament appears — the melody comes from the right hand, the flavour from the left. 3. Hear the slowness. "Suits slower pieces" — the instrument with the most strings is the one that excels at slow.
