Gong

The alloy-disc idiophone — unpitched, with an extremely long tail

2 min readPercussionUpdated 2026-09-26

instrumentpercussionwesternworld

One stroke is enough

A large gong hangs from a frame with a hand steadying its rim. The mallet falls, and the sound does not begin on a note; it grows out of a slab of low frequency, spreads, and takes ten seconds or more to leave. It offers no pitch, only weight.

The whole alloy sheet vibrates in modes so complex that the partials never line up as a harmonic series. With no fundamental to catch, the sense of pitch disappears. It shares that origin with the cymbals, except that a gong is thicker, larger and lower.

Two instruments under one word

The tam-tam of a Western orchestra has no definite pitch and exists to make atmosphere. A Southeast Asian row gong has a raised boss, a small diameter, a definite pitch, and plays melody in sets. Chinese calls both luo; they are different things.

Do not read that as a boss causing pitch. The Chinese daluo is bossed and still has no definite pitch. What fixes a pitch is the whole geometry: diameter, curvature and how the thickness is distributed.

Where to strike, and how to stop it

No pitch does not mean no control. The centre gives a thick low roar, the edge a thinner sound that dies sooner. A hand on the rim holds the tail back for a short stroke, and a mallet moved slowly across the disc draws a crescendo from inaudible to overwhelming.

A large soft mallet, felt or leather covered, keeps the metal noise out. The gong must hang on a cord: a metal stand absorbs the vibration and ruins both colour and tail.

The weight of one stroke

The tam-tam stroke in the finale of Tchaikovsky's Sixth Symphony lands at the lowest point of the work, and only then does the music go on. That is typical orchestral use: a gong does not accompany, it arrives once and changes the passage.

Film scoring turned it into a fixed sign for ritual, ending and fate, one stroke at a time. Familiarity dulls it, but hearing a real large gong in a hall, with the low end pressing on your chest, is something a recording struggles to reproduce.
