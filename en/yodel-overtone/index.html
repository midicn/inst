Yodeling and overtone singing

Turning the register break into the product — two voices living in one throat

2 min readVoiceUpdated 2026-10-21

instrumentelectronicelectrophonewesternvoiceyodel

A herder calls across the valley, then flips straight into falsetto and calls again. The two notes sit an octave apart with nothing in between. The cattle hear it; so does anyone else.

The break is usually a fault

Vocal training spends years making the break disappear, covering and mixing and placing resonance, so the seam cannot be heard. Yodeling puts the seam in the middle. A strong yodeler flips four or five times a second, each jump wider than an octave: not a slide but two voices taking turns.

Overtone singing divides differently. A drone keeps sounding while the mouth selects one harmonic and amplifies it, so a whistle-like melody floats above. Yodel splits in time; khoomei stacks in the same instant.

From pasture to theatre

A record from 1545 describes it as the call of a cowherd from Appenzell. The Ranz des Vaches had two audiences: people heard that someone was over there, cattle heard that it was time to come home.

In the 1830s Tyrolean touring troupes carried it into theatres and music halls across Europe and America. In 1928 Jimmie Rodgers stitched it into the blues on Blue Yodel No. 1, and the singing cowboys followed that line. The Swiss Yodel Association, founded in 1912, had more than six hundred branches and fifteen thousand members by 1975.

There was a by-product. Swiss mercenaries in French service during the 1690s heard the tune, grew homesick and deserted, and the army banned it. A Swiss doctor, Hofer, coined a word for the condition in 1688: nostalgia.

A range that never grew

Neither technique extends the voice, still roughly C2 to C7. What changes is use: the same stretch is halved and alternated, or stacked in one moment.

So the training is not about singing higher. It is about not hesitating at the seam.

Two parts in one throat

The mechanism of overtone singing fits in a sentence: the folds produce a drone rich in harmonics, and the mouth is a variable filter that picks one and amplifies it. Most people use the mouth to shape vowels roughly; here the knob is turned to isolate a single harmonic.

One source, two audible pitches, no second device.

To try it, skip melody: slide from chest voice to falsetto and back twenty times, and find the seam.
