Wagner Tuba

A horn mouthpiece on a tuba-like body — built for The Ring of the Nibelung

A horn's mouthpiece, a tuba's body

A brass instrument's colour is decided by two things: the small funnel the lips meet, and the pipe the sound travels through. The Wagner tuba copies the horn for the first and the tuba for the second — conical tubing, an upward flaring bell, four rotary valves.

The mouthpiece decides who plays it: same rim as a horn, same embouchure, so horn players double on it and the fingering carries over. The body decides what it resembles: tuba thickness, tuba reticence. The valve set suits the low register; the small mouthpiece makes the top easier than a tuba's.

Two columns, and the design space of brass appears: every row is a choice. The Wagner tuba takes one end from each column — parts can be made separately, so an instrument can be assembled.

It fills a gap

Sounding B♭1 to F4, it sits exactly between horn and tuba. The working register is B♭1 to B♭3: the horn cannot reach that low, the tuba is not agile enough down there.

That is why scores treat it as a section rather than a solo voice: it comes in fours, two tenors in B♭ and two basses in F, shared among the horn players. From birth it was a harmonic layer, not a protagonist.

Built for one work, outliving it

In the 1850s Wagner, planning the Ring, asked for a colour between horn and tuba; in the 1870s makers built it to his specification and he called it a tuba. It was first heard at the 1876 premiere.

Bruckner, Richard Strauss and Stravinsky borrowed it later, turning a piece of Ring equipment into a standard orchestral colour. Bespoke instruments usually vanish with the work; this one did not, for a practical reason: nothing else fills that gap.

General MIDI has no Wagner tuba, so a horn moved into its register is playing above. The mouthpiece matches; the thickness of the tuba body is missing.
