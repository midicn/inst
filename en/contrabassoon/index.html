Contrabassoon

The double-reed an octave below the bassoon — nearly six metres of tube, lower than a double bass

1 min read115 characters条目WoodwindsUpdated 2026-09-26

The contrabassoon is the bassoon an octave lower — and the lowest standard wind instrument in the orchestra: its bottom B♭0 is two octaves below a cello and a major third below the double bass's E1.

It is the one woodwind so low that its notation has to be reconsidered — written an octave above sounding, for exactly the reason the piccolo's is written an octave below.

⚠️ One conceptual point: with the piccolo it forms an exact mirror image. The piccolo sits an octave above its family's standard and is written an octave below sounding (to avoid ledger lines above the staff); the contrabassoon sits an octave below and is written an octave above sounding (to avoid them below it). The same logic, two directions. Learn the pair and woodwind transposition stops being confusing.

Structure: how six metres of tube is held

Three points:

1. Double the tube, double the folds. The bassoon folds once, the contrabassoon twice — one size larger outside, more than twice the tube inside. The number of folds is the evidence of length. 2. Slower response. A long, winding air path makes the attack and staccato duller than a bassoon's — the physical reason it rarely carries fast lines. 3. It takes more air. A larger reed on a wider, longer tube needs a big, steady supply.

Range: the orchestra's floor

  • Sounding B♭0–B♭3: only three octaves, but placed very low.
  • The working register is B♭0–F2 — almost all its work happens at the very bottom.
  • It carries no melody. What it does is give the whole band's bass a floor and a weight. When it appears alone (rarely), it is usually to create an unusual sense of mass.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Extremely low, dark, turbid. Unlike a bassoon it has no crisp attack — it is a mass of low frequency with weight. 2. Audible mechanical noise. A long tube with large holes lets key and air noise into the low register — which has become part of its colour. 3. Hard to identify by pitch. Very low notes are also hard for the ear to place, so it is felt as weight rather than as notes. 4. It pulls the whole band's bass down. With and without a contrabassoon, the centre of gravity of the sound is completely different — that is its entire value in a score.

⚠️ On the audio: the General MIDI set has no contrabassoon. A bassoon sample, placed in its low register, is sounding above — a hint of register, not the real colour.

Playing techniques

The technique comes from the bassoon; the differences are largely physical:

  • The greatest air demand of the family. The widest, longest tube needs a very large steady supply.
  • A slow attack. A long air path makes crisp staccato hard to achieve.
  • A stand and a strap. It is far heavier than a bassoon and is usually supported.
  • The player makes the reed, as everywhere in this family, and it is larger still.

The oboe family's four stops line up by tube length: 0.6 → 0.8 → 2.5 → 5.9 m, each a step lower. Length is the only scale. Note that the contrabassoon and the double bass transpose in exactly the same way (an octave up) — that is about keeping the page readable, and has nothing to do with which family an instrument belongs to.

History

PeriodState

19th c.the folded wooden body and keywork settle; it enters orchestral scores
Late 19th–20th c.a standard orchestral member; Wagner, Mahler and Richard Strauss use it heavily to thicken the bass
20th c.modern works occasionally bring it forward (low solo passages), but its main role is still underpinning
Late 20th c.metal and composite versions appear, improving size and stability

Common misconceptions

  • "A contrabassoon is a big bassoon played the same way." Similar fingerings, but air demand, attack speed and how it is held all differ; it needs its own practice.
  • "The lowest instrument in an orchestra is the double bass." Among standard winds it is the contrabassoon: its B♭0 is lower than the double bass's E1.
  • "Written an octave high means it is a high instrument." Notation has nothing to do with register — its sound is extremely low, and the page is high only to avoid ledger lines.
  • "It is just the bassoon shifted down." Double the tube and the folds and you get different physics: slower response, more noise, harder to place by ear. It is weight, not "a bassoon an octave lower".
  • "General MIDI has one." It does not; a bassoon sample is the only stand-in (as on this page).
