The guanzi keeps an unusually complete immigration file.

Born in Kucha (modern Kuqa, Xinjiang), its name transliterated from the old Kuchean word Pi-Li; in 383 CE it entered the Central Plains with the expedition band of the general Lü Guang; through Tang and Song it sat at court as the "head of all instruments" — under the name touguan. And today it is called guanzi — "the pipe" — the plainest possible description.

⚠️ Follow that name's itinerary: bili (transliteration) → bili/quaili (the transliteration settles) → beili ("sorrow pipe," meaning added) → touguan ("head pipe," its *post*) → guanzi ("the pipe," what it *is*). ⭐⭐ Rule ㉟: an immigrant instrument's naturalisation has three notches — transliteration, post, plain speech. When its name becomes plain speech, nobody remembers it was ever foreign.

One pipe, three things

Three points:

1. The reed — the sounding core: small pipes take a cane reed (wire-tied, tip flattened, straight into the body); large pipes take a bamboo-reed one (inserted into the qinzi). Its softness and thickness decide timbre, pitch and difficulty. 2. ⭐ The qinzi — a small conical bronze part that holds the large reed and "serves to adjust the pitch." A dedicated tuning part on a wind instrument is rare (compare the dizi's plug and mouth-hole, or the xiao's bare holes). 3. The body — cylindrical (the very contrast with the suona's conical flare), eight holes. Cylinder + double reed + wood is the trio behind its "solid, voice-close" sound.

⚠️ Bluntly: there is no single "guanzi range" — large, middle and small each sit a step apart, and keys differ again. The figure shows the middle pipe, the one bands use most.

Why it "hooks the soul"

Folk judgment gives it and its cousin the suona one line each:

"Without the suona, no one can be seen off; without the guanzi, no soul can escape."

  • The suona "sees off" — weddings and funerals, soaring over everything;
  • The guanzi "hooks" — "mournful, bleak, deep", called "the saddest instrument", nicknamed "the sorrow pipe" — even its name carries sorrow.
  • ⭐ Its sorrow has three sourced sources: the double reed's throaty edge; the straight cylindrical bore (it broadcasts nothing, it just goes in); and a middle register "like a human voice sobbing" — "solid, close to the human voice."
  • ⭐ One cold fact shows its power: "because of its loud, high sound, the Hu people played it to startle horses." A sound that could spook cavalry was later reserved for weeping.

From court to wind-band

After the Song it stepped down from "head of all instruments" — and every new post was closer to the ground:

  • ⭐ Lead of the northern wind bands — Hebei chuige, Shanxi's eight suites, southwestern Shandong, Luoyang, Xi'an drum music, Liaoning… it is the main instrument, "the soul of the wind band."
  • Opera accompaniment (Hebei bangzi, pingju, Yu opera) and temple music (Daoist, Buddhist; the Zhihua Temple's capital music still leads with it);
  • Solos: *Wannian Huan*, *Fang Lü* (*Driving the Donkey*), *Yan Luo Sha Tan* and other traditional titles.

⭐ And its most famous appearance happened in 1950s Liaoning:

  • "River of Sorrow" (Jiang He Shui) began as a sheng-guan melody of Liaonan drum music (named *Jiang'er Shui*, used at weddings and funerals). In the early 1950s Wang Shilu, Zhu Guangqing and colleagues recast it as a double-pipe solo — using the double pipe's "sob like a human voice" to tell of a widow weeping by the river.
  • In 1962 the erhu player Huang Haihuai heard it in Guangzhou and transplanted it for erhu; it entered *The East Is Red*, and through Min Huifen's playing it became world-famous. In 1977 Seiji Ozawa wept hearing it: "it draws out all the sorrow in the world."
  • So the piece has two lives: a guanzi life and an erhu life. One instrument's grief can move to another body and go on grieving — proof that the tune outlives the instrument.

Common misconceptions

  • "The guanzi is native." ⚠️ It is from Kucha; the name transliterates Kuchean Pi-Li, and it entered in 383 CE with Lü Guang's band — one of the Silk Road's most thoroughly naturalised instruments.
  • "It was always 'guanzi.'" ⚠️ "Guanzi" is a Ming–Qing plain name; before it: bili, quaili, beili, touguan. An older, unrelated "guan" died out centuries before ("the instrument is lost").
  • "Guanzi and suona are the same thing." One family, different builds: suona = conical + flared bell (sound thrown outward); guanzi = cylindrical (straight, voice-close). "Seeing people off" and "hooking souls" are physically different roads.
  • "Jiang He Shui is an erhu piece." ⚠️ It was born a double-pipe solo: Liaoning wind melody → 1950s double-pipe arrangement → 1962 erhu transplant. The guanzi came first.
  • "Its sorrow is an act." ⚠️ Its file is two-sided: "bright, loud, rough-hewn" and "bleak, deep" at once; the Hu played it to spook horses. It can weep — and it can roar.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Three cues:

1. Hear the voice. Its middle register is "like a human voice sobbing" — reed plus straight bore gives one of instrumental music's closest things to a naked voice. 2. Hear the hardness. The other face: "bright, loud, rough-hewn" — its sound stands straight up, with no flare to spread it: the energy comes at you. 3. Hear both versions of *Jiang He Shui*. Double pipe beside erhu, and you verify this page's last claim with your own ears: grief transplants, and each throat has its own grief.
