Woodblock

A hollowed hardwood block borrowed from East Asia — with a clearer sense of pitch than its cousins

1 min read41 characters条目PercussionUpdated 2026-09-26

One block of hardwood, hollowed out, a long slot cut in one side. The stick comes down and the sound is dry, short, its tail cut off — and you can still hear high and low in it.

What one slot changes

Claves are solid: the wood damps itself and almost no sense of pitch survives. Castanets have a shallow cavity and give a weak sense of height. The woodblock keeps its cavity regular and opens a slot, so the air inside has a definite resonance — and the sense of pitch turns clear.

Clear is not pitched. It lands on no nameable note, and a pair makes no scale; scores treat it as unpitched.

The slot also decides where you hit: the slot's edge is bright, the solid wall dull. Mallet hardness does the same — a hard head gives grain, a soft one rounds it.

Only a pair makes a figure

Two blocks of different size give two notes. Tap-tock: hoofbeats, clogs, a clock. One block is a point; two barely make a phrase, which is the margin it has over claves.

In the orchestra it usually means mechanical repetition — walking, ticking, gears. Not loud, but it places itself clearly inside a dense texture.

A borrowed shape, a changed job

The shape is East Asian. Western composers took it into the orchestra in the early twentieth century as a wooden colour, cut loose from its original setting. The Chinese muyu has its own tradition — ritual, chant, the beat in opera — a separate line.

The same hollowed block is a ritual instrument in one place and an entry in an orchestration table in another. What changes when an instrument crosses a border is usually not its shape but its job.

The division of labour

The materials are nearly all hardwood or metal; what separates them is whether a cavity exists and how regular it is. For a point with no scene, claves. For a little height, the woodblock.
