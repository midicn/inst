Paixiao (Chinese panpipes)

Thirteen bamboo tubes in a row — one note each, old name "cencī, the uneven"

8 min readChinese WindsUpdated 2026-09-26

instrumentwindaerophoneChinesebayinbamboo

This instrument answers one question with a row of bamboo: what if every note wanted its own tube?

The answer: the scale is split into thirteen tubes of bitter bamboo — one note each. Melody is no longer made by fingers on holes, but by the mouth walking along the tube mouths.

And its luck lies there too — in 1978, two panpipes came out of the tomb of Marquis Yi of Zeng: of the 2,400-year-old tubes, one set still had eight pipes that could sound.

⚠️ And its earlier post was the band's tuning reference. The Henan Museum's research says it plainly: "the panpipes did serve to check the tuning of the ancient band." Compared with the bells' pitch series, the agreement is plain to see. Rule ㉛: one tube, one note — the scale taken apart into a row of tubes, each made for its single note. And that independence made tuning a very concrete craft: stopping the tube's foot with beeswax — "stop the foot with honey-wax, adding or removing until it agrees." (Cai Yong, via the Tongdian.)

One distinction: the site's pan flute entry covers the instrument worldwide (Greek syrinx, Romanian nai, Andean siku…); this one covers the Chinese paixiao — its archaeology, its names, its fate.

One bundle of bitter bamboo: Zeng Hou Yi's 13 tubes

Three points:

1. A bundle of unequal bamboo. Thirteen bitter-bamboo tubes: level at the top (the blowing plane), ragged below — and the difference in length is the difference in pitch: "long is muddy, short is clear," as the old text puts it. The natural node closes each foot — it is born a stopped pipe. 2. Three strips. Split bamboo strips bind the bundle into one piece — the whole of its assembly. Black lacquer over it, with red coiled and thunder patterns. 3. It is a pair. The tomb held two: same form, different lengths — the male longer, the female shorter; the sources say such pairs "played together, like a duet of male and female voices." (Hence the batch's names-history item ⑫: paired instruments named "male and female.")

One tube, one note: melody by walking

The playing method is unusual — no keys, no finger holes, no reeds:

  • One note per tube: "the paixiao has no superfluous technique; each tube gives a single note, and playing requires the lips to move quickly and precisely to the right tube."
  • Melody by walking: "the hands move the instrument left and right; the head only assists" — "like a harmonica, moved sideways to choose the tube"; long tubes (low) left, short tubes (high) right.
  • Compare the site's dizi and xiao: they are "one tube, many notes" (fingers walk on holes); the paixiao is "one note per tube" (the mouth walks on tubes). One-tube-many-notes buys gliding and vibrato, at the cost of compromise; the paixiao buys a dedicated tube for every note — even colour, no interference. The cost: it can hardly glide. Between notes, it can only leap.

Range

A row of tubes also means a fixed range — it cannot "grow" the way a fingered instrument can: as many tubes, as many notes.

Tube count is the instrument's whole margin: one more tube, one more note; a touch of wax, a note corrected. That is also why it was later "reformed" — with the old sixteen tubes or so, without new tubes there were no semitones and no wider range.

The wax is the pitch

If every tube owns a note, tuning becomes extremely concrete — it tunes each tube. And the Chinese craftsmen's answer lies at the foot of the tube:

"The xiao is bamboo bound with a closed foot. Twenty-three tubes at the largest, sixteen at the smallest. Long is muddy, short is clear. Stop the foot with honey-wax, adding or removing until it agrees." (Cai Yong, quoted in Du You's Tongdian, Tang.)

  • Length fixes the note; the wax trims it. Add or remove until it agrees.
  • After the Han there even appears the "equal-tube" form: all tubes cut to nearly the same length, "the wax plug at the foot forming the tube's sounding length" — the wax's depth is the scale's graduation. In the Tang paixiao fragments kept at the Shōsōin in Nara (18 tubes originally, 9 surviving), the adjustment was paper pellets — the same idea in another material.
  • And the beeswax found at the feet of the Zeng Hou Yi pipes ties the whole chain back 2,400 years: it "confirms that wax was used to adjust pitch in ancient China." In this batch's "three tuning actions" (rule ㉕), wax changes the effective length — a case of changing geometry.

And this instrument that had to be so carefully tuned held a special post in the band: "the panpipes did serve to check the tuning of the ancient band" — its pitch series agrees with the bells; with overblowing raising it one or two octaves, it was "the coordinating instrument of the stone-and-metal music." An instrument that is also a measuring tool — the other half of rule ㉛.

One wing, or two

This figure is a piece of history of knowing: the texts called it "phoenix-winged" — but was the wing one, or a pair?

  • The object's answer: both Zeng Hou Yi pipes are single-wing — "shaped like one wing of a butterfly," like ancient panpipes the world over.
  • The Qing answer: double-wing, symmetrical — 16 tubes, "eight yin, eight yang," arranged by lü (the lü names written on the tubes), cord replaced by a wooden frame. The Qianlong court's lacquered example (dated 1743) stands 34.5 cm tall with a shoulder of 37 cm, its tubes labelled with the twelve lü and four extra lü — less an instrument now than a lü-ordered object.
  • The summary: "through the ages the count and length varied, but always a single phoenix wing; only the Qing changed it to double." Its last great change of form happened after it had moved into the palace.

A name's history: from "xiao" to "paixiao"

One story cannot be left out — the character xiao (箫) shrank over history:

  • From pre-Qin to Tang, "xiao" meant this instrument — the bound-tube panpipe. The Book of Documents' "xiao-shao nine times, the phoenix comes": it led the Shao music, which took its name; the Book of Songs: "xiao and pipes raised together"; Confucius, hearing the Shao, "for three months did not notice the taste of meat." The single vertical tube was then called di.
  • After the Tang, "xiao" drifted to the single tube (see the xiao entry) — and the many-tubed one had to add a character to find itself again: paixiao. "The name paixiao begins with Zhao Lin's Yinhua Lu (Tang)"; by the Yuan it was the official term.
  • Its older names stayed in the books and the poetry: "dixiao · yaxiao · songxiao · shunxiao · bizhu · cencī · fengyi · duanxiao · yunxiao" — the most famous being cencī, "the uneven": the everyday phrase "cencī buqi" (uneven) comes from the shape of its tubes. A musical form became one of the commonest phrases in the language.

The arc is complete: from a Shao-music lead that "summoned phoenixes," to a lü-ordered court object, to near-silence, to a 1980s rebuild. And it returned the Chinese way: not a replica but a reform — keys, semitone tubes, five octaves.

Common misconceptions

  • "You finger the holes on a paixiao." ⚠️ It has no finger holes: one note per tube, melody by moving lips and hands from tube to tube.
  • "Paixiao and xiao are unrelated names." ⚠️ Deeply related: the pre-Qin "xiao" was the paixiao; the single tube later took over the name.
  • "Ancient panpipes were symmetrical double wings." ⚠️ The reverse: single wings nearly everywhere; the double wing is a Qing change — and the Qing scholars' 26-tube double-wing restoration was corrected by the object itself.
  • "Length alone tunes it." ⚠️ Length gives the note; wax at the foot trims it — "stop the foot with honey-wax, adding or removing until it agrees"; in equal-tube sets the wax depth is the graduation.
  • "Just an ordinary instrument." ⚠️ In the ancient band it also checked the tuning: "the panpipes did serve to check the tuning of the ancient band," the coordinator of stone-and-metal music.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Three cues:

1. A melody that "walks." Listen for the small leap between notes — it is not a glide; the player changed tubes. 2. Even colour. One tube per note keeps every note in its own home: remarkably uniform timbre — its most "engineered" quality. 3. Listen beside the sheng. The sheng is also multi-tubed (17 reeds) — but one reed one note, and several can sound at once; the paixiao blows one tube at a time. Both "multi-tube": one laid sideways, one set upright; one gives chords, one only melody.

Read the last column together: the fork between "many tubes" and "one tube" is a fork about where pitch lives — the paixiao keeps pitch in the tubes (make thirteen); the flute keeps pitch in the fingers (make six holes). The paixiao chose the tubes, and so it is born even — and born unable to glide.
