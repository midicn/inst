English Horn

An F double-reed woodwind with a metal crook and pear-shaped bell, sounding a perfect fifth below written pitch

2 min readWoodwindsUpdated 2026-11-02

instrumentwoodwindwestern

A name with a kink in it

The English horn, or cor anglais, is a double-reed woodwind rather than a horn. Its name has competing explanations involving early angled instruments and a word associated with angels. The claim that cor anglé was simply misread as cor anglais is not an established fact.

The crook survives: the reed sits on a curved tube entering the body, so the angle works and the body can be longer. The instrument runs about 80 cm, eighteen more than an oboe, a fifth lower.

It is in F: written C5 sounds F4, a perfect fifth lower. The smaller oboe d’amore in A sounds A4 for written C5. The bassoon is normally written at concert pitch; these instruments are not four uniformly scaled transpositions of one design.

What the bulb does

The pear-shaped bell is a visible feature and contributes to low-note resonance and radiation. Reed, crook, bore and playing technique also shape the tone. Its darker colour across the range cannot be reduced to a bell filtering out the high frequencies.

Compared with the oboe, its lower and middle registers are often fuller. Register and dynamic changes can also bring a brighter, more focused sound. Writing should account for register, dynamic and the surrounding texture.

Range

Sounding E3 to B♭5, a fifth below the oboe. The working register is E3 to F5, where its most characteristic sound lives: dark, slow, faintly plaintive.

The high register needs careful reed and air control, rather than being explained simply as resistance from the bulbous bell. The usual low E3 corresponds to written B3. Practical upper limits depend on the player and the demands of the piece.

One solo, and a place in the score

The solo opening the second movement of Dvořák's New World Symphony is its best-known appearance — slow, dark, homesick, effectively its calling card. Berlioz and Wagner place it mid-woodwinds, a colour between winds and strings.

The English horn has orchestral solos as well as chamber and solo repertoire. It can sustain a leading melodic role. Leave space around its lower and middle registers rather than treating modest volume as a reason it cannot carry a complete work.

To hear the bulb at work, find a passage where English horn and oboe alternate, and listen for the high frequencies dropping out.
