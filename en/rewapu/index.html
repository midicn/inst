The most instructive thing about the rawap is that its strings have two different jobs.

It is not "several strings played together" — only one string does the work:

"Strung with one melody string and four to six sympathetic strings… the outermost string plays the melody, and the others serve as resonating strings." ⭐⭐ Rule ㊺: strings divide labour — one leads, several just ring along; and the reform cancelled the ringers to gain modulation. The melody string plays; the sympathetic strings do not — they are tuned to fixed pitches and vibrate along, filling and brightening the sound (hence "clear and pure"). This is the same idea as the hulusi's drone pipes (pipes dividing labour), in a string version: an instrument providing its own accompaniment.

One kind of string, one kind of job

⭐ Three points:

1. The melody string is the outermost. "The outermost string plays the melody" — tuned c¹; the rest are tuned to fixed pitches and only resonate. 2. The ringers are not extras. They make the sound "clear and pure" — ⭐ the same idea as the hulusi's drone pipes: an instrument providing its own background. 3. It is also an inlaid ornament. "The neck and box are inlaid with camel bone and shell"; Kashgar rawaps are "both a solo instrument and a craft object."

The reform: it cancelled the ringers to gain modulation

In the 1950s the "new-type rawap" was created from the Kashgar model:

Kashgar rawapNew-type rawap
Length130 cm93 cm
Boxhemispherical · one piece of mulberry · sheep or donkey skinlarger · built from staves · python skin
Frets28 silk frets (movable)24–25 bronze frets, equal temperament
Strings1 melody ＋ 4–6 sympatheticfive steel strings, equal job
⭐ Sympathetic stringsyesnone
Rangeabout two octavesabout three octaves

⭐ Note the bottom two rows: the reform traded away the sympathetic strings for equal temperament and free modulation. The "ringers" had a cost: strings fixed to given pitches make it hard to change key — so the instrument chose to go from "a soloist with built-in resonance" to "an orchestral instrument that can modulate." The family was not lost either: alto and bass rawaps followed, making a rawap section.

Common misconceptions

  • "Several strings played together make chords." ⚠️ Most of the time only the outermost string plays the melody; the rest are sympathetic: unplucked, ringing along — "many strings" here means timbre, not harmony.
  • "The sympathetic strings are decoration." ⚠️ They define the signature sound ("clear and pure") — and the reform removed them: what can be removed is a function, not an ornament.
  • "There is only one rawap." ⚠️ Many types: Kashgar (southern) · northern · Dolan · shepherd; the Tajik equivalent is the rebub (apricot wood, flat-round box).
  • "The name has one spelling." ⚠️ 热瓦甫 / 热瓦普 / 拉瓦波 / 喇巴卜 — the last being the Qing court's official spelling in the *Lülü Zhengyi Houbian*.
  • "It always has python skin." ⚠️ Skins range from sheep, donkey and horse to python; python is commoner on reformed models (stave-built boxes), while folk Kashgar instruments use sheep or donkey.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Three cues:

1. Hear two layers. You pluck one string and hear a choir — the sympathetic strings ringing at their own pitches. 2. Hear the fineness. The Kashgar rawap is "quieter but delicately expressive" — not an instrument that wins on volume, but on fullness. 3. Hear the difference between the two generations. On the reform model the ringing layer is gone — what you gain is modulation; two trade-offs of one instrument, neither simply better.
