Mechanical Instruments

Devices that store a performance on pinned barrels and paper rolls — they record actions, not sound

7 min readEarly InstrumentsUpdated 2026-09-26

instrumentmechanicalwesternearlykeyboard

Every entry so far has described an instrument: something a person operates in real time, whose sound comes into being at that moment.

Mechanical instruments are the one exception in this group — they are not an instrument but a class of devices: they store the act of playing itself and then let it happen on its own.

⚠️ They answer an entirely new question: can a performance be preserved? Before this, the only thing that could be preserved was notation — and notation preserves instructions; each performance was still a one-off, gone as soon as it ended. Mechanical instruments did it for the first time: write "when, and which note" into hardware, then execute it repeatedly. A pin on a barrel, a hole in a paper roll — that is a record of actions. Which gives this page's central distinction: they record actions, whereas the phonograph records sound — two entirely different ways of "keeping music".

Note that nothing in this family has a sound of its own — a music box sounds its comb, a player piano sounds a piano, a barrel organ sounds pipes. Its role is the performer, not the instrument — a point that matters below.

Structure: the tune is cut into metal

Three points:

1. The pin positions are the data. Which pin, on which row of the barrel (= which note), arriving at the comb at which moment (= when it sounds) — that is a record of actions. 2. The comb sets pitch by length. Teeth of different lengths vibrate at different frequencies. It shares its vibrating body with the celesta and glockenspiel — struck metal — except that pins strike it instead of hands. 3. The repertoire is fixed in hardware. That is its earliest and hardest limitation — and the next figure shows how it was loosened step by step.

Three conclusions:

1. "Recording actions" necessarily loses things: a barrel stores only "which note, when", so it cannot play loud and soft — dynamics, timbre and pedal detail are simply not in the record. 2. The paper roll was the key upgrade. With dynamics and tempo added, the player piano could for the first time replay one particular performance — and in the early 20th century a number of famous players left their playing behind in exactly this form. 3. It lost in the end on what was recorded. The phonograph (from 1877) captured sound directly, with no need to drive an instrument and no limits from that instrument. But its idea won: today's sequencers and MIDI still record actions — whoever punches the roll or edits the data is the performer who is not in the room.

Range

  • This class has no single range — because it is not a single instrument.
  • Music boxes are the narrowest: a comb has only so many teeth and the pitches are a fixed set — like the hunting horn, a case of points rather than a stretch.
  • Player pianos are the widest: A0–C8, all 88 keys — because they are pianos, with the hands replaced by a paper roll.
  • The chart shows the widest member, because that member defines this family's ceiling.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. The sound comes from a real instrument, not a synthesiser. A music box sounds metal teeth, a player piano sounds strings — only the hands have been replaced. 2. Extremely regular. No breathing, no hesitation: everything happens at an exact moment. 3. Music boxes sound thin and bright. Metal teeth carry a metallic ring, the same family as the celesta. 4. Player pianos can sound human. Once dynamics and tempo are recorded, what comes out is one particular performance, not "the notes played straight".

History

PeriodState
Middle Ages–Renaissancetowers and churches gain automatic devices (tower clocks, chimes) — the earliest form of the family
16th–17th c.barrel organs and musical automata become fashionable in courts and collections
18th c.a peak of automaton craft: figures playing real instruments; pinned-barrel technique matures
19th c.the music box becomes a Swiss industry; the street organ fills European cities; swappable barrels and discs make changing the tune possible
late 19th–early 20th c.the player piano appears and flourishes; with dynamics and tempo recorded it can replay one particular performance, and a generation of famous players is preserved this way
first half of the 20th c.pushed out of the mainstream by the phonograph and radio
late 20th c. to todayrestored as collectors' items and early-music devices; its idea lives on in sequencers and MIDI — recording actions never died

Its fate has the same shape as the portative organ's: the principle was not wrong; the object being recorded was. The portative organ lost to a lighter implementation; mechanical instruments lost to a more direct recording object — sound. When one technology replaces another, it is rarely a question of which is better made, but of which records something more fundamental.

Common misconceptions

  • "A mechanical instrument is a gramophone." No. A gramophone records and replays sound; a mechanical instrument drives a real acoustic instrument — it plays the role of the performer and has no sound of its own.
  • "It needs no human." It does — the human has moved from the moment to beforehand: the person who punches the roll or sets the pins is the performer. Anyone programming MIDI today stands in exactly that place.
  • "It is just an antique toy." It was the first step in storing and replaying music, and player pianos captured the real playing of noted performers — the "recordings" of their day.
  • "It was replaced by recording, so it was worthless." More precisely: it lost on what it recorded (actions necessarily lose timbre and detail) — and its idea survived (sequencers, MIDI, modern reproducing pianos).
  • "A player piano sounds just like a person." A roll can record dynamics and tempo, but timbre and pedal detail are limited — its ceiling, and one reason it was superseded.
  • "Hurdy-gurdies, music boxes and accordions are the same sort of thing." Only the Chinese names collide: one is strings, one is teeth, one is free reeds — different vibrating bodies, and different answers to "who decides the pitch".
