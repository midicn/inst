The balafon

Its name is an action — the whole craft of playing this wooden keyboard

Related

⭐⭐ The main line: its name is an action

「In the Malinké language balafon is a compound of two words: balan is the name of the instrument and fô is the verb to play. Balafon therefore is really the act of playing the bala.」

In Malinké, 「balafon」 is not the name of an instrument — it is the name of the act of playing this instrument. The instrument is bala; to play it is fô. Put together: playing the bala.

⭐⭐ And the common English name balafon is probably a European invention too: 「its common name, balafon, is likely a European coinage combining its Mandinka name bála with the word fóo (to say / method of saying) or the Greek root phono」——⭐⭐ Western listeners took a verb and re-wrote it as the name of a thing (contrast the Western misnomer djun djun for the dunun: there an entirely nonexistent word was invented for an instrument, here an action was grammatically mistaken for an object —— both turn a verb into a noun, in opposite directions).

⭐⭐ The site's sixth naming pole: the proverb name names the use (djembe) · the transliteration names the bloodline (bili) · the mistaken name names the history (a cappella) · the ancestor name names the addressee (mbira) · the debased original name names the identity (kora/jali) · the Western misnomer (djun djun) · the action name (balafon).

⭐⭐ One instrument, three native names, one map

⭐⭐ The fourth case of one name, two things: Kongo's palaku and the West African balafon are both keyed calabash-resonated percussion idiophones, yet belong to two unrelated cultural circles (contrast the dunun: the Manding low-drum family vs the Yoruba dùndún — same name, different objects).

⭐⭐ Naming by size: bala for the large, balani for the small —— ⭐⭐ as with the dunun's ba = large: African family naming builds a suffix from size, while the Chinese bayin builds its categories from material.

⭐⭐ Construction: wood tuned by shaving, a spider's egg case over the gourd's mouth

⭐ The keys are made of béné wood, dried slowly over a low flame, and tuned by shaving —— ⭐⭐ shave the middle and the note flattens; shave the end and it sharpens (⭐ tuning as subtraction: less wood, higher pitch, like carving a character). In the fixed-key form the keys are strung by leather straps onto a wooden frame, beneath which hangs a graded row of calabash resonators.

⭐⭐ A small hole is cut in the mouth of each gourd and covered with a thin membrane —— originally a spider's egg case filament, today cigarette paper or thin plastic. Striking a key sets the membrane moving, and a continuous nasal buzz rides under the tone.

⭐⭐ The site's strangest material borrowing: a spider's egg case used as a diaphragm (contrast the mbira's soda-bottle-cap shaker —— one borrows an insect's egg shell, the other borrows a soft-drink bottle cap; ⭐⭐ both are the same invention: find an existing thin film and make it the sound-producing part).

⭐⭐ That buzz is precisely the boundary between balafon and marimba: marimba gourds carry no membrane and sound clean; the membraned version has a natural nasal hum —— ⭐⭐ a part that 「should not be there」 created the identity of the whole family (contrast the site's flaw-into-signature lineage: the TB-303's circuitry · the 808's out-of-spec transistors · the mbira's physical adjacency · the balafon's mismatched hole and membrane).

⭐⭐ And the marimba: same shape, different source

「Believed to have been developed independently of the Southern African and South American instrument now called the marimba」

⭐⭐ Two continents each built a keyboard of wooden keys over gourds, entirely unrelated to each other, and they came out almost identical (contrast the kora, where one sound source is forced into two families; here two peoples independently produced the same form) —— ⭐⭐ in human instrument history, independent reinvention is the norm.

⭐⭐ Essentially unchanged for centuries: oral histories date the balafon to the rise of the Mali Empire in the 12th century; in 1352 the Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta recorded ngoni and balafon at the court of Mansa Suleyman; 「European visitors to West Africa described balafons in the 17th century largely identical to the modern instrument」 —— ⭐⭐ four hundred years, essentially unchanged (contrast the site's rates of change: the Telharmonium dies within a generation · the 808 is canonised within one · the balafon does not move in four centuries —— ⭐ some instruments stay in history by changing, others by refusing to).

⭐⭐ Three roads away from home

⭐ Out to sea with the slave trade: players were carried to the Americas; the *Virginia Gazette* recorded African Americans playing a barrafoo in 1776, and by the mid-19th century every North American reference had vanished —— ⭐⭐ the instrument crossed the ocean and so did its name, leaving only a paragraph in a newspaper (contrast the talking drum's speech codes, banned by colonial authorities —— two kinds of removal: one silenced after removal, one banned in place).

⭐⭐ The technique travels, the instrument stays: in the 1980s revival of African Roots and World Music, the Rail Band led by Salif Keita was its most famous exponent; ⭐⭐ Maninka players in eastern Guinea transferred balafon playing style onto imported guitars —— a technique migrates more easily than an instrument (contrast the kora, whose versions live on a person: there the person carries the version, here the technique carries the player).

⭐ On the body: players commonly wear belled bracelets on both wrists to accentuate the keys —— ⭐⭐ the body starts producing sound (contrast the talk box's mouth · choir-scoring's space —— wrist bells are the lightest form of 「the body as an accessory to the instrument」).

⭐⭐ Who may learn it: the exact opposite of the djembe

⭐⭐ The balafon is a griot instrument —— the sentence 「The djembe is not a griot instrument」 defines the balafon by its opposite: it is inherited.

⭐⭐ Within one family there are both drums anyone may play and keyboards only the born may play —— ⭐⭐ the class character of a tool is decided by 「who keeps the knowledge」: the djembe's craft can be learned from the wood, the balafon's scales and rhythm types cannot be learned from the bloodline (contrast the institutional pairing of djembe and kora).

⭐ Scoring: in Guinea and Mali, sets of three (bass, middle, treble) are common; Cameroon has a six-piece ensemble of different sizes called a komenchang; an Ibo variant gives each player a single large tuned key.

⭐⭐ Listed in 2024 on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity —— ⭐⭐ alongside Indonesia's kolintang: two instruments written into the same list on the same day, one in Southeast Asia and one in West Africa —— a list is itself a cross-cultural catalogue.
