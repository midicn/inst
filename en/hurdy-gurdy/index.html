Hurdy-Gurdy

A crank turns a rosined wheel against the strings — one player, two hands, an unbroken drone plus a melody

6 min readEarly InstrumentsUpdated 2026-09-26

instrumentstringwesternearlyfolk

The hurdy-gurdy reaches the same result as the portative organ through completely different physics: one player, two hands, a sound that never stops.

The previous thread's keyword was "blow", this one's is "rub":

⚠️ One keeps feeding air into pipes; the other keeps rubbing a string The portative organ sustains its pipes with one hand pumping; the hurdy-gurdy sustains its strings with one hand turning a crank, dragging a rosined wooden wheel across them. Both are "one continuous bodily action keeping one uninterrupted sound alive". They differ in what the action feeds: an air column, or a string.

And it does one thing the organ cannot: the same crank drives two groups of strings —

String groupHow it soundsResult
Drone stringspressed straight against the wheela low layer that never stops
Melody string(s)stopped by the keyboard's tangentsa moving melodic line above

So it is one of the very few instruments that carries its own accompaniment — and that "accompaniment" is not played; it is produced automatically, like a machine.

Structure: crank, wheel, two groups of strings

Three points:

1. The wheel replaces the bow. It does exactly the same job — rubbing a string — but where the bow uses an arm going back and forth, the wheel uses rotation. So "the bow change" disappears: the friction never stops. 2. Tangents replace fingers. Each key pushes a small wooden blade against the string; where it presses becomes the new speaking length, hence a new pitch. No finger pressure is needed, so intonation is steady and fingers do not tire. 3. Both string groups are driven by one wheel. This is the key design point: the bass is not played, it is simply always there.

Why it cannot stop

This figure closes the "sustain" thread of this group:

DriveTime-formCan it stop?
fingers pluckinga series of dotsyes (gaps are built in)
bow rubbinga line with seams at bow changesyes (lift the bow)
wheel rubbingone unbroken lineno (unless the wheel stops, and restarting takes time)
a person pumping (portative organ)a line that rises and falls with the handyes, but never cleanly

Note the last row: the organ and the hurdy-gurdy both sit on the "cannot easily stop" side, for entirely different reasons — one because a hand keeps pumping, the other because a wheel keeps turning.

"Cannot stop" is its greatest limitation and its most distinctive power: its music is inevitably continuous, and phrases are marked by harmony and accent rather than by breath.

Range

  • About G3–G5, usually only some two octaves.
  • The limit is mechanical: the keys and tangents must fit along a short stretch of the body, and making it longer and heavier makes it unplayable.
  • Working register G3–D5 — exactly where folk dances and voices live, which says where its roots are.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. A nasal rubbed tone. The friction of wheel on string carries a slight roughness of its own. 2. A permanent buzzing floor. This is what gives it away: the drone strings never stop, so the whole piece sits on one unchanging harmony. 3. Notes stick together. The melodic line is continuous, with almost no real gaps between notes. 4. An audible mechanical layer. The wheel turning and the tangents moving all make noise, and modern recordings often keep it deliberately — it is the instrument's character.

Playing techniques

  • The right hand only turns the crank. Its speed sets the overall tempo and slightly affects the volume — it is the only tempo control, and it doubles as the volume control.
  • The left hand works the keyboard. Pressing a key moves a tangent, needing no finger pressure against the string, so intonation is steadier and more even than on a fingerboard.
  • No true staccato. The only way to stop is to stop the wheel, and getting it turning again takes a moment — so rests always come with a slight tail.
  • No dynamics. Volume can only rise and fall overall, through the crank's momentum; it cannot be shaped freely within a phrase.
  • It often shares a stage with voices, bagpipes and fiddles, usually taking the "drone" chair a bagpipe would otherwise occupy.
  • Rosin and wheel pressure need attention. How hard the wheel bears on the strings changes tone and volume — daily mechanical maintenance for the player.

The family

Three "machine instead of hand" string instruments, easily confused:

InstrumentDriving forceWheel?Bow?Keyboard?Vibrating body
Hurdy-gurdycrank turning a wheelyesnoyes (tangents)strings
Nyckelharpabownoyesyes (keys)strings
Portative organa hand pumpingnonoyesair column
  • The nyckelharpa has a bow and keys but no wheel — its machinery only handles stopping the string; the sound still comes from an arm.
  • The hurdy-gurdy mechanises the sounding itself: the wheel replaces the arm.
  • The portative organ mechanises the sounding too, but the vibrating body is not a string at all.

One sentence to tell them apart: look at what each hand is doing, then ask what vibrates. Those two questions separate every confusing name in this family.

History

PeriodState
10th–12th c.wheel-rubbed string instruments appear in Europe (earlier forms are simpler, often with a single drone string)
13th–15th c.widely used for dance and religious settings; common in church carvings and manuscript illuminations
16th–17th c.the form matures: tangent keyboard and several drone strings; used at court and in the countryside alike
18th c.becomes a fashionable instrument in France, with refined court versions, while remaining a folk instrument
19th c.takes root in the folk music of central France (Berry, Bourbonnais), becoming a fixture of local dance music
20th c.nearly lost; the folk revival brings it back, and it is now played in folk and early-music scenes worldwide

Its history has an interesting shape: the same instrument lived at court and among country people at once. It was the folk branch that kept it alive through the 19th century — once an instrument gets "refined", it is often the supposedly crude branch that saves it.

Common misconceptions

  • "A hurdy-gurdy is a kind of organ." No. Its vibrating body is a string (chordophone); an organ's is an air column (aerophone). The Chinese name containing "wind organ" is pure historical accident — there is no wind involved.
  • "It is the thing street musicians crank." That is another instrument: a crank drives an air pump and a pinned barrel or paper roll decides the notes — an automatic mechanical instrument. The two only collide in Chinese naming.
  • "It has a keyboard, so it plays polyphony." The keyboard stops only the melody strings, so only one melodic line can sound; what runs alongside is the unchanging drone.
  • "It can play staccato and dynamics." Neither. While the wheel turns the string sounds; volume can only move overall with the crank's momentum.
  • "It is a simple folk instrument." Refined court versions existed, and it is one of the very few designs to build the accompaniment into the instrument — in that respect it is more automated than most.
  • "It struggles to stay in tune." The opposite: a tangent against a string is steadier than finger pressure, which is partly why it can serve as a folk ensemble's reference drone.
