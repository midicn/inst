The mbira (thumb piano)

Its name means "the ancestors' voice" — and it has not a single string

Named after a voice, and it has no strings

In Zimbabwe, after dark, someone holds a board and plucks steel tongues with two thumbs. Bottle caps ring the front edge; every pluck sets them hissing. The instrument is mbira dzavadzimu, which in Shona means the ancestors' voice. Nothing on it is a string: ten to thirty-two steel tongues vibrate directly, staggered short to long over a gourd. The djembe is named by a proverb, the balafon by an action; the mbira is named by a voice, and not its own.

Invented twice, 1,700 years apart

A bamboo-tongued instrument appeared on the west coast about 3,000 years ago, a metal-tongued one in the Zambezi valley about 1,300 years ago. The same family was invented twice in Africa, and the second replaced the material outright: bamboo, iron, steel. It reaches from North Africa to the Kalahari, yet most African peoples never had one. Instruments have borders; the piano does not.

The hiss is for the ancestors

Bottle caps and shells are fixed on so the instrument hisses while it is played. Locally the hiss is believed to draw ancestral attention and guide souls to rest.

Noise here is a component, not a defect: the 808's sub-bass was an accident, the 303's sawtooth a decision, the mbira's hiss was fitted on purpose. The hiss is the channel.

The overtones of a steel tongue

A plucked tongue's overtones are inharmonic: brightest at the attack, decaying fast into something near a pure tone. A string stacks integers, a skin throws noise, a steel tongue leaves a fading pure tone. Pluck one and its neighbours ring too, interference in principle, thickness in practice.

In the 1920s the British musicologist Hugh Tracey recorded tunings and layouts across Africa, then built a western-standard version in South Africa called the kalimba and began exporting it in 1954. The kalimba in a shop today descends from that export.

In Zimbabwe it is still played at rituals and funerals, believed to drive evil from the sick and to bring rain. In 2020 the mbira and sansi craft of Malawi and Zimbabwe entered UNESCO's Representative List.
