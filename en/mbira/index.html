The mbira (thumb piano)

Its name means "the ancestors' voice" — and it has not a single string

Related

⭐⭐ The main line: the name means "the ancestors' voice" — and it has not a single string

「mbira dzavadzimu」 — Shona: 「the ancestors' voice」

⭐⭐ The site's fifth naming pole: djembe's proverb names the purpose, bili's transliteration names the blood, a cappella's misunderstanding names the history, kora's jali-versus-griot names the identity — and the mbira is named after a voice, and not "my voice" but "the ancestors' voice". ⭐⭐ An instrument's name is the being it calls: pluck one tine, the metal rings, and the ancestors answer — the name carries a whole cosmology (the instance "Ancestral Song of the Li": a people's song and a people's instrument are two faces of one thing).

⭐⭐ Invented twice, seventeen centuries apart

「these types of instrument appear to have been invented twice in Africa: a wood or bamboo-tined instrument appeared on the west coast of Africa about 3,000 years ago, and metal-tined lamellophones appeared in the Zambezi River valley around 1,300 years ago」

⭐⭐ The same kind of instrument was invented twice in Africa, independently — once on the west coast (c. 3000 BP, bamboo tines), once in the Zambezi valley (c. 1300 BP, metal tines) — ⭐⭐ and the second replaced the first's material wholesale: bamboo → iron → steel. ⭐ Its spread reaches "from the northern reaches of North Africa to the southern extent of the Kalahari Desert, and from the east coast to the west coast", yet "many or most groups of people in Africa did not possess mbiras" — not every African group has it (compare the site at large: the piano is worldwide, the guqin is China's, the mbira belongs to specific sub-Saharan peoples — instruments have their own geography).

⭐⭐ The hiss: a noise component designed to call the ancestors

Mbiras carry bottle caps and similar objects that add a hissing sound while played.

⭐⭐ Locally, that hiss is believed to attract the attention of ancestral spirits, to commune with them, and to guide their souls to rest — ⭐⭐ the strangest design on the site: noise used as a summoning tool. Compare the site's noise lineages: the 808's sub-bass blowing out cones (accident) · the 808's out-of-spec transistors (defect) · the talk box's plastic tube and breath (body) — the mbira's hiss is the only instrument that deliberately manufactures noise in order to call. ⭐⭐ A culture that treats noise as a part of the instrument is more direct than any flaw-into-signature: they want the hiss, because the hiss is the frequency of the passage.

⭐⭐ Acoustics: inharmonic overtones and sympathetic tines

「the overtones of a plucked lamella are inharmonic……strongest in the attack and die out rather quickly, leaving an almost pure tone」「When a tine is plucked, the adjacent tines also create secondary vibrations that increase the harmonic complexity of an individual note.」

⭐⭐ The first quotation is the site's heaviest statement about timbre physics: a plucked lamellaphone's overtones are inharmonic (not integer multiples), so the attack is brightest and the tone then decays to near-purity — unlike strings (stacked harmonics) or skins (noisy transients). ⭐⭐ Three African instruments, three overtone physics: kora (string · rich harmonics) · djembe (skin · noise burst) · mbira (tine · inharmonic, pure, momentary brightness) — ⭐⭐ material decides not just timbre but the shape of a sound: a string's overtones are a stack of integers, a skin's are a cloud of noise, a steel tine's are a decaying pure tone.

⭐⭐ The second quotation is flaw-into-signature's third form: pluck one tine and its neighbours ring along — which should be interference but is instead the thickness of the timbre (compare the 808's out-of-spec transistors: an unqualified component became an irreplaceable sound). ⭐⭐ Three flaw-into-signature cases side by side: the TB-303's sawtooth (a circuit decision) · the 808's transistors (a component grade) · the mbira's sympathetic tines (mere physical adjacency) — the source of the flaw keeps sinking, until it lands on "two pieces of metal next to each other".

⭐⭐ The agent Hugh Tracey: another African instrument exported

⭐ In the 1920s British ethnomusicologist Hugh Tracey came across the mbira on his African travels, became absorbed by it, and recorded the tunings and note layouts of many different thumb pianos; in the mid-1950s he founded an African instruments company in South Africa, made his own version to western standards, named it kalimba (after an ancient predecessor of the family), and began exporting it worldwide in 1954.

⭐⭐ Once again, an instrument of its homeland turned into a global commodity by an outsider — ⭐⭐ compare the site's three "agent" lines: Roland turned three small Japanese appliances into world products (the 808, 303, 909) · Sequential turned Dave Smith's design into a product (the Prophet-5) · Hugh Tracey turned a Shona instrument into the kalimba — the agent decides what the product is called, and who history remembers (⭐ compare kora's "jali over griot": Africans were themselves fighting over the right to name). ⭐ And the popularisation route was rock: Maurice White (Earth, Wind & Fire) and Thomas Mapfumo put the mbira on stage with electric guitar, bass, drum kit and horns in the 1960s–70s; Dumisani Maraire carried marimba/karimba music to the American Pacific Northwest; Ephat Mujuru was a pioneer teacher of mbira dzavadzimu in the United States.

⭐⭐ Function: banishing, rain-making, weddings, funerals

The mbira appears at religious ceremonies, weddings, gatherings and funerals; in Zimbabwean belief its sound drives out the evil in a sick person and can be used to ask for rain (the instances "Night Spirit Song" and "Umbrella Song" — a rain-song and a rain-making instrument are two witnesses of the same worldview on two continents). ⭐ Often accompanied by hosho percussion. ⭐⭐ Inscribed in 2020 on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity (Malawi and Zimbabwe: "The Art of crafting and playing Mbira/Sansi").
