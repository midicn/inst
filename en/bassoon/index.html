Bassoon

A double-reed conical tube folded into a U — the widest-ranging woodwind

Related

IntervalRegisterTimbreArticulation MarksOboeClarinetBass Clarinet

The bassoon has the widest range of any woodwind (about three and a half octaves), and the least tube-like shape: its body is folded into a U, 2.5 m of tubing standing only 1.35 m tall.

It belongs with the oboe (a conical tube with a double reed), but as the woodwind group's bass it does two jobs that often pull in opposite directions: underpinning the harmony, and playing the clown when called upon.

⚠️ One conceptual point: the woodwind group's two bass instruments use two different bores. The bassoon is conical (double reed, folded in a U); the bass clarinet is cylindrical (single reed, folded once). Their low registers therefore feel entirely different — the bassoon woody and grainy with a clear core, the bass clarinet supple and close-grained. That is exactly why orchestras keep both.

Structure: a U-fold and a bocal

Three points:

1. A U-fold. It takes 2.5 m of tube to reach B♭1, but a player can hold only 1.35 m — so the tube is folded once and the two limbs run side by side. Tube length is folded down to human scale. 2. The bocal is a bent metal tube joining reed to body, and its geometry directly affects pitch and colour — players keep several to suit different conditions. 3. The bell points up (as on the bass clarinet). Low frequencies radiate into the floor and get absorbed, so low instruments tend to aim their bells upward.

The same low register, two bores

One clean causal chain:

  • A conical bore (bassoon) sustains all harmonics → a rich spectrum → grain and a core, with an audible woody hum at the bottom.
  • A cylindrical bore (bass clarinet) sustains only odd harmonics → fewer, more regular partials → close-grained, supple, dark.

Hence their division of labour: the bassoon gives the low line a face (a recognisable colour), while the bass clarinet gives the low texture a floor (thick and edgeless).

Range

  • Sounding B♭1–E5, about three and a half octaves — the widest in the woodwind group, matching the clarinet.
  • The low register B♭1–C4 is its most identifiable stretch and the part most used in ensembles.
  • Above A4 it is written in tenor clef and its colour turns thinner and tenser — where the solo repertoire lives.

Timbre, and how to hear it

Four cues:

1. Woody grain, with an audible "breath of the wood", especially in detached playing. 2. A thick but clear bottom — unlike the bass clarinet it does not smear; the bass line keeps a crisp attack. 3. Two characters. The same instrument can be comic (short staccato, the traditional voice of clumsiness or mockery) or solemn and plaintive (long legato lines) — that split is its signature. 4. Moderate volume, good penetration. Warm as a soloist, yet clear inside an ensemble.

Playing techniques

  • Held on a strap. It hangs on the player's right side; the hands only work keys and steady the instrument.
  • The player makes the reed (as on the oboe). A bassoon reed is much larger and takes longer to scrape.
  • Staccato is its trademark. Short detached notes in the low register are exceptionally clear — the source of its comic reputation.
  • Overblowing and harmonics. A conical bore overblows an octave, so its top rises smoothly, like the oboe's.
  • Modern extended techniques: flutter tongue, multiphonics, glissando; a rolled paper mute in the bell is occasionally asked for.

The oboe family's four stops (oboe · English horn · bassoon · contrabassoon) are all conical with a double reed, pitched by tube length — and the bassoon is the midpoint of the span: it reaches up to the English horn and oboe, down to the contrabassoon.

History

PeriodState
16th–17th c.develops from earlier low double-reed instruments (the dulcian); often hollowed from one piece of wood, with the U-fold taking shape
18th c.the jointed form (wing / boot / long joint / bell) settles; few keys, softer than today
Late 18th–19th c.keys multiply and range and tuning improve; Mozart and Weber write concertos
Late 19th c.French and German systems compete; the German (more keys, harder) becomes standard
20th c.fixed as the woodwind bass; the solo repertoire (concertos, sonatas) grows sharply; contemporary works add extended techniques

A frequently cited example: the opening bassoon solo of Stravinsky's The Rite of Spring — written in the instrument's very top register, to make the strange feeling of a spring thawing. It shows that a bassoon's high register is not a by-product but an expressive region in its own right.

Common misconceptions

  • "A bassoon is a big oboe." Same family, but it is the bass foundation, folded, with wider key spacing and tenor clef for the top register — a different instrument to play.
  • "Being wooden, it must sound soft." Its low register is thick, yet its staccato can be sharp — "wooden" does not mean "soft", and its expressive range is wide.
  • "The bass clarinet and the bassoon do the same job." The bores differ (conical versus cylindrical), so the lows feel different — which is why both persist rather than one replacing the other.
  • "You have to transpose for it." The bassoon is in C and written at pitch. The transposing member of this family is the contrabassoon (written an octave up).
  • "The bocal is just a connector." Its geometry directly affects pitch and colour; players keep several for different temperatures, humidities and pitch standards.
